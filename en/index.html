Station brief

Start from a concrete scene, then set out the questions, methods and boundaries of this station.

A summer storm hits the vegetable market first: leafy greens rise, then transport and packaging follow a few days later. For the grower the rain means a smaller harvest; for the insurer it means claims; for a city household it means a bigger bill. Economics starts from scenes like this. Resources are limited, ends compete, and every outcome can be traced back to someone choosing under a rule.

The first questions are concrete: who is choosing, what do they hold, and what does the rule allow? Write those three things down and price, quantity and welfare changes become explainable. Skip them and “everyone wants more” starts to look like a cause.

Micro and macro divide the work here. Micro watches consumers, workers and firms as they bargain, enter and exit. Macro adds them up and deals with output, employment, prices and the balance of payments. Aggregation is not simple addition; definitions and statistical boundaries change the answer.

Models are tools, not photographs. Perfect competition, rational actors and frictionless markets isolate one mechanism at a time. When you meet a conclusion, find its assumptions. If an assumption fails, the conclusion may narrow, or it may reverse.

Two mistakes are easy to make. One is treating a price as a moral verdict; the other is treating GDP growth as evidence that every household is richer. Prices distribute, and growth depends on population, inflation and measurement. Keep those apart.

After this station, a rate, a house price, an exchange rate or an unemployment release should raise sharper questions: whose choice is this, what constraint changed, and what exactly does the number measure?

After this station, you should be able to answer

  • Why can the same good sell at different prices in different markets?
  • Why does GDP growth not necessarily mean higher income for every household?
  • Why might a rate cut fail to reach business investment?

Four common misreadings

These claims come up again and again in reader mail, comments and working discussions. Naming them is more useful than repeating definitions.

“Economics is market forecasting.”

Economics studies choice and its consequences under scarcity. Price forecasting is a narrow application, and it needs a model, data and institutional context. Most economic research does not make short-term market calls.

“If the assumptions are unrealistic, the model is useless.”

Models leave detail out by design. The question is whether the omitted mechanism matters for the problem at hand, and what happens when a key assumption fails. A model of perfect competition does not describe every street, but it can still explain how competitive pressure limits markups.

“When GDP rises, everyone gets richer.”

GDP measures final goods and services produced in a territory at market prices. It says nothing about distribution and does not subtract pollution or commuting costs. Population growth, prices and informal activity all change how far it travels from lived experience.

“Price is set by cost.”

Cost sets the floor at which a supplier is willing to sell. Demand, competition and substitutes decide where the price actually lands. In a shortage, price can sit far above cost; in excess capacity, it can sit below full cost.

Flour gets dearer: should the bakery raise prices?

Three or four articles from this station, applied to one decision. Every number is a teaching assumption for practising the order of judgement, not an estimate of a real market.

A neighbourhood bakery bakes 300 loaves a day, with flour making up about 30% of the cost per loaf. When flour rises 20%, cost goes from 3.00 to about 3.36 yuan. The owner’s first instinct is to raise the price, but the first question is: if I add one yuan, how many customers walk away?

If the other three shops on the street hold their prices, a lone increase may cut volume by 30%. If the whole street moves together, short-run demand barely falls. Write both cases as small accounts: gross margin after the increase, lost volume, and regulars who may not return.

Then do the marginal sum. What do the extra flour, electricity and casual labour cost for one more batch, and what does that batch bring in? The rent is already paid and does not change with today’s output, so it does not belong in the decision to bake one more batch.

State the conclusion as a condition: the increase works if demand is inelastic enough, rivals follow, and the marginal cost of another batch stays below the marginal revenue. Otherwise cut output, adjust the recipe or change supplier. The numbers here are classroom assumptions, not a real shop’s books.

These words recur throughout the station. Learn the sense used here before moving into the articles.

scarcity 稀缺

Wants exceed what is available, so choices are unavoidable.

opportunity cost 机会成本

The next-best option forgone, valued at that option rather than summed across every alternative.

ECO-003 · Opportunity Cost and Sunk Cost
margin 边际

The change from one more unit; decisions turn on marginal benefit and marginal cost, not averages.

ECO-004 · Margin and Incentives
externality 外部性

A cost or benefit imposed on a third party that does not pass through the price.

ECO-010 · Externalities and Public Goods
GDP GDP

Market value of final output produced in a territory over a period; production, income and expenditure approaches should agree in principle.

ECO-011 · GDP and National Accounts

The change in a price index from one period to the next, not the index level; basket, definition and adjustment all change the reading.
