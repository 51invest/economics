Games and Information

When outcomes depend on others' choices and one side knows more than the other, game theory and information economics provide testable equilibrium and contracting benchmarks.

ModuleECO-M05 · 博弈与信息
LevelFoundation
Read8 min · 12 sections

Definition

Economics studies choices under scarcity[1]; game theory studies how rational participants act when one person's payoff depends on others' choices[3]; information economics studies how asymmetric information changes transactions and contracts, with insurance as the standard example[4]. Together they form the minimum toolkit for auctions, negotiation, platform pricing, credit and insurance—the places where “I know more than you” changes the outcome.

Intuition

Two airlines on the same route are each considering a fare cut. If only one cuts, it wins passengers; if both cut, the price war hurts both; if neither cuts, both keep higher margins. Neither airline can determine the result alone, so each must guess what the other will do. Game theory writes this “outcome depends on both choices” situation as a payoff table and asks: given the other player's action, is there a better choice for me?

Formal expression

Strategies and payoffs

A strategy is a rule for choosing an action in a given situation. A payoff matrix records each player's payoff for every combination of actions. The first step is to confirm that players, action sets, payoffs and information are defined, not to search for an “optimum”.

Equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a profile in which no player can obtain a higher payoff by unilaterally changing action while others stay put (the standard textbook definition used here; the supports of [3] cover payoff matrices, dominant strategies and the prisoner's dilemma, not this sentence). It does not guarantee the best social outcome; it guarantees that, under the stated rules, nobody wants to deviate alone. A dominant strategy is better regardless of what others do and is stronger than a Nash equilibrium.

Repeated games

In a one-shot game, defection may pay. When the same interaction repeats, retaliation and reputation enter the payoff calculation. A repeated game analysis must state whether players recognise each other, remember history, how future payoffs are discounted and when the interaction ends.

Auctions

An auction turns “who is willing to pay how much” into comparable bids. Common forms include ascending, sealed-bid and double auctions; the rules change bidding strategy, winner's curse and seller revenue, not merely “highest bid wins”. Analysing an auction requires the valuation structure (private or common), whether bids are public and whether there is a reserve price.

Contracts and incentives

A contract ties payment to an observable action or outcome. If the action is hard to observe, payment must be tied to outcomes, and the agent may shift risk to the principal; if outcomes also depend on luck, the incentive design must separate effort from noise. Competition policy against cartels and collusion has a direct legal basis[2]; treating it as an application of game theory in real institutions is our summary.

Adverse selection and moral hazard

Adverse selection occurs before a transaction: the better-informed party is more likely to enter a deal that favors it, so the other party raises the price or exits. Moral hazard occurs after a transaction: a party changes behavior because a cost has been shifted or a consequence shared. Both can thin a market, but they occur at different times and call for different tools[4].

Conditions and limits

  1. Players, actions and payoffs must be written down; if payoffs depend on unobservable beliefs or culture, the model yields only conditional conclusions.
  2. A Nash equilibrium need not be unique or efficient; with multiple equilibria, state which one is selected and why.
  3. Conclusions about asymmetric information depend on who knows what and who can verify it; change the information assumption and the result may reverse.
  4. A game model is a simplification, not the whole of real motivation; experimental evidence also has sample and external-validity limits.

Scope note: the formal existence and refinements of Nash equilibrium, the strength relation between dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium, specific repeated-game strategies, auction formats and the winner's curse, contract design, platform pricing, negotiation, credit and insurance applications are outside the supports of the registered sources; this article follows standard textbook treatment, and the payoff matrix is a teaching assumption.

Worked example

Suppose two airlines on one route face the following payoffs. A fare cut wins passengers but compresses margins. The matrix uses teaching assumptions, not actual financial data:

A \ B Hold fare Cut fare
Hold fare 10, 10 2, 12
Cut fare 12, 2 5, 5

If B holds, A gets 12 by cutting versus 10 by holding; if B cuts, A gets 5 by cutting versus 2 by holding. Cutting is therefore A's dominant strategy. B is symmetric and also cuts. The outcome is (5, 5): both do worse than (10, 10), yet neither can improve by acting alone. A Nash equilibrium can be a stable bad outcome; changing the rules or the information players can see is what changes it.

Common mistakes

Games are zero-sum. Many games are positive-sum or variable-sum: cooperation, auctions and contracts can create or divide value; one player's gain is not always the other's loss.

A Nash equilibrium is the best outcome. Equilibrium only says nobody wants to deviate alone; it says nothing about maximum social welfare. The fare-war example is stable and low-payoff at the same time.

Knowledge-point review

strategy

Definition: A strategy is a plan of action chosen in light of others' possible moves, including commitments, threats and forgone options.

Mechanism: Strategies influence equilibrium by changing payoffs and expectations; a credible commitment must be observable and enforceable, while verbal threats may fail.

Example: For example, a supplier threatens to stop deliveries to late payers, but losing a large order may make the threat non-credible.

Boundary: Strategic analysis assumes actors can identify key moves and payoffs; incomplete information, emotion and multiple goals make an optimal strategy ambiguous.

equilibrium

Definition: A game-theoretic equilibrium is a situation where, given others' strategies, no player can raise payoff by unilaterally changing strategy.

Mechanism: Nash equilibrium requires each strategy to be a best response; multiple equilibria, mixed strategies and refinements address uncertainty and dynamics.

Example: For example, if both shops charge 10 yuan, undercutting to 9 wins customers and raising to 11 loses them, so 10 may be an equilibrium.

Boundary: Equilibrium need not be unique or desirable; coordination failure, multiple equilibria and shocks can trap outcomes, so selection criteria matter.

repeated games

Definition: A repeated game has the same players interacting over many periods, with observable history affecting future payoffs.

Mechanism: Repetition enables punishments and rewards, so cooperation can emerge without formal contracts; discount factors, monitoring and horizon determine stability.

Example: For example, two long-term suppliers may refrain from poaching because retaliation would destroy future profit.

Boundary: Finite horizons can invite end-game defection; an infinite horizon is a stand-in for uncertainty, and punishments must be enforceable and understood.

auctions

Definition: An auction allocates a good and sets its price through explicit competitive rules, such as English, Dutch, sealed-bid and double auctions.

Mechanism: Outcomes depend on valuation distributions, information and rules; winner's curse, collusion and strategic bidding can move prices away from theory.

Example: For example, firms bidding for spectrum may win and later lose money if construction costs were underestimated.

Boundary: Auctions do not guarantee efficiency or maximum revenue; reserve prices, entry fees and design change participation, and resale and bid-rigging need oversight.

contracts

Definition: A contract specifies future rights, obligations and payments, using terms to reduce uncertainty and opportunism.

Mechanism: Contracts coordinate incentives by allocating control, returns and risk, but anticipating every state is costly, so residual control and renegotiation remain.

Example: For example, a construction contract sets deadlines, change pricing and penalties to limit delay and hold-up.

Boundary: Contracts cannot remove all risk; vague terms, enforcement costs, asymmetric information and relationship-specific investment create lock-in and disputes.

adverse selection

Definition: Adverse selection occurs under asymmetric information when high-risk or low-quality types are more likely to enter the pool.

Mechanism: If buyers cannot distinguish quality and pay only an average price, good sellers exit and average quality falls further in a spiral.

Example: For example, used-car buyers paying only an average price drive well-maintained cars out, leaving more problem cars on the market.

Boundary: Adverse selection requires asymmetric information and type differences; mandates, certification, warranties and risk classification can ease it but raise privacy and fairness issues.

moral hazard

Definition: Moral hazard is the tendency for one party to change behaviour and take less care after a transaction because someone else bears the risk.

Mechanism: Insurance, agency and rescue arrangements weaken behavioural discipline; deductibles, co-payments, monitoring and incentive contracts realign costs.

Example: For example, full reimbursement may lead a car owner to lock or maintain the car less because the insurer bears theft or damage costs.

Boundary: Moral hazard is not fraud; it requires post-contract discretion and weak observability, and excessive penalties can suppress legitimate activity.

Exercises

  1. Concept check: distinguish a dominant strategy from a Nash equilibrium in one sentence each, and say which is stronger.
  2. Calculation: in the matrix above, if (Hold, Hold) paid (12, 12), how would the equilibrium change?
  3. Conditions: give an auction example and explain when the winner's curse is more likely under common valuations.
Suggested answers
  1. A dominant strategy is better against every action the other player can take; a Nash equilibrium is a profile from which no player can improve by deviating alone. Dominance is stronger: any profile of dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium, but not conversely.
  2. (Hold, Hold) becomes the new Nash equilibrium; (Cut, Cut) is no longer an equilibrium, because against a rival who cuts, deviating back to Hold yields 12, above 5. Cutting is no longer strictly dominant either: when the rival holds, holding and cutting both pay 12.
  3. With common valuations (bidders share the same underlying value but cannot observe it precisely), many bidders, and sealed bids, the winner is more likely to be the one who overestimated; the curse is weaker when valuations are private and precisely known.
