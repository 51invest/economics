Supply, Demand and Equilibrium

Demand and supply curves relate price and quantity; equilibrium is their intersection, and movements along a curve differ from shifts of the curve.

ModuleECO-M02 · 消费者与家庭
LevelFoundation
Read7 min · 12 sections

Definition

A demand curve shows the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices, holding other conditions constant. A supply curve shows the quantity producers are willing and able to sell. The market equilibrium is the intersection: at that price, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. OpenStax’s Principles of Economics describes equilibrium as the intersection of demand and supply and stresses that a movement along a curve is not the same as a shift of the curve[1].

Intuition

At a high price, sellers want to supply more while buyers want to purchase less, creating a surplus. At a low price, buyers want more while sellers offer less, creating a shortage. In a competitive market, price tends to adjust toward the level that clears the market.

Formal expression

A simple linear teaching model:

Q_d = 100 - 2P
Q_s = 20 + 2P

At equilibrium, Q_d = Q_s:

100 - 2P = 20 + 2P → P = 20, Q = 60

Movements versus shifts

A price change causes a movement along a curve: a lower price raises quantity demanded, and a higher price lowers it. Changes in income, preferences, substitute prices, expectations or the number of buyers shift the demand curve. Changes in input costs, technology, taxes, expectations or the number of sellers shift the supply curve.

The direction cannot be inferred from price alone. If the observed price rises, demand may have shifted right (price up, quantity up) or supply may have shifted left (price up, quantity down): the two give the same price signal and opposite quantity responses, so identifying the cause requires the observed quantity and other context. Conversely, in a comparative-static exercise that moves only one curve, a simultaneous rise in price and quantity is compatible only with a rightward demand shift; a rightward supply shift would push the price down and raise quantity.

Four-step analysis

OpenStax’s four-step process is a useful comparative-static method[2]:

  1. Draw the initial model and equilibrium.
  2. Identify which curve is affected: demand, supply, or both.
  3. Determine the direction of the shift: right or left.
  4. Compare the new equilibrium with the old one and state the direction of price and quantity changes.

Example: demand becomes Q_d = 120 - 2P while supply remains Q_s = 20 + 2P. Solving gives 120 - 2P = 20 + 2P → P = 25, Q = 70. A rightward demand shift raises both equilibrium price and quantity. If supply shifts to Q_s = 40 + 2P, then 100 - 2P = 40 + 2P → P = 15, Q = 70; the equilibrium price falls and quantity rises.

Assumptions and limits

The model usually assumes many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, available information, no price controls or significant market power, and other conditions held constant. Real markets may have product differentiation, monopoly, externalities, public goods, search frictions, expectations and sticky prices; a simple intersection may not explain the outcome.

Equilibrium is not the same as fairness or social optimality. The model shows how price and quantity become mutually consistent under its assumptions; it does not evaluate distribution, external costs or market power. Comparative statics asks what would happen if a curve shifted while other conditions stayed fixed; it is not a forecast of timing or magnitude.

Common mistakes

A price increase proves demand increased. Not necessarily: a supply decrease also raises price. A movement along the demand curve only describes the price-quantity relation, not the cause of the shift.

The equilibrium price always prevails. Price controls, menu costs, inventories and expectations can keep a market away from equilibrium; the model is a benchmark, not a record of reality.

The model answers every economic question. It is suited to competitive price-quantity analysis; externalities, public goods, market power and macroeconomic aggregates need other models.

Knowledge-point review

preferences

Definition: Preferences rank consumption bundles given information and constraints; they describe wants rather than realized demand.

Mechanism: Preferences enter the budget constraint through the marginal rate of substitution: how much of one good a consumer will give up for another, so a change in ranking shifts demand.

Example: For example, a consumer with similar ratings for two coffees picks the cheaper cup, but new information can reorder the ranking.

Boundary: Preferences shift with habits, information and income; revealed preference only infers rankings from choices, which budgets, supply or defaults may distort.

utility

Definition: Utility is the subjective satisfaction a consumer derives from a bundle; it is an ordinal ranking, not money income or physical output.

Mechanism: Constrained utility maximization equalizes marginal utility per unit of price across goods, which traces an individual demand curve.

Example: For example, the first glass of water is valued highly when thirsty and the fifth much less, so the same water is valued differently by quantity.

Boundary: Utility cannot be compared directly across people; cardinal and ordinal versions are modelling conventions, and real choices also face cognitive limits and emotion.

budget constraint

Definition: A budget constraint is the boundary of affordable bundles at given income and prices, written as income equal to total spending.

Mechanism: Price or income changes rotate or shift the budget line; the substitution effect moves along an indifference curve while the income effect moves to a new curve.

Example: For example, with income 100, apples at 5 and bread at 10, all bread buys ten loaves; a cut in apple prices rotates the line outward.

Boundary: The constraint assumes current spending cannot exceed current income and ignores borrowing, transfers and liquidity limits, although households also allocate across time.

demand

Definition: Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, shaped by preferences, income, related prices and expectations.

Mechanism: A lower price raises quantity demanded through substitution and changes real purchasing power through the income effect; normal goods usually have downward-sloping demand.

Example: For example, cutting a bus fare from 3 to 2 yuan moves some drivers to the bus and raises quantity demanded; a fare increase does the reverse.

Boundary: Demand is not mere desire: Giffen goods, status goods, rationing and liquidity constraints can make price and quantity move together, so the good and institution must be specified.

income and substitution effects

Definition: The income effect is the quantity response to a change in real purchasing power; the substitution effect is the response to relative prices.

Mechanism: The Slutsky decomposition splits total demand change into substitution and income effects; for normal goods they reinforce, while inferior goods can have an offsetting income effect.

Example: For example, a higher wage makes leisure more expensive and substitution raises work hours, while higher income may reduce them; the net depends on the two effects.

Boundary: The decomposition depends on how compensation is defined; Hicksian and Slutsky compensation differ, and labour supply, credit constraints and habit formation can break the simple dichotomy.

elasticity

Definition: Elasticity is the percentage response of quantity to a change in price, income or a related good's price, expressed as a unit-free ratio.

Mechanism: Demand is elastic when its absolute price elasticity exceeds one and inelastic when below one; elasticity determines whether a price cut raises or lowers revenue.

Example: For example, a 10 percent salt price rise that cuts purchases by 1 percent is inelastic and raises total spending, while holiday packages may be elastic.

Boundary: Elasticity varies by price range, horizon and substitutes; point and arc measures differ, and short-run and long-run elasticities often diverge.

intertemporal choice

Definition: Intertemporal choice allocates consumption and saving across time, centring on the marginal rate of substitution between present and future consumption.

Mechanism: Interest rates discount future consumption into today's terms; the Fisher equation separates nominal rates into real rates and expected inflation, while borrowing limits can distort smoothing.

Example: For example, at a 5 percent annual rate, forgoing 100 yuan today buys about 105 yuan next year; a higher borrowing rate makes earlier consumption more expensive.

Boundary: Intertemporal models assume borrowing, known lifespans and stable preferences; liquidity constraints, uncertainty, bequests and self-control make actual saving diverge.

Exercises

  1. Calculation. If Q_d = 100 - 2P and Q_s = 20 + 2P, find equilibrium price and quantity. At a price of 30, is there a shortage or surplus, and how large?
  2. Shift. For a normal good, a rise in consumer income shifts demand right while supply is unchanged. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity?
  3. Boundary. Why can’t an observed price increase by itself prove a rightward demand shift? Give one alternative explanation.
Suggested answers
  1. Equilibrium is 100 - 2P = 20 + 2P, so P = 20 and Q = 60. At P = 30, quantity demanded is 40 and quantity supplied is 80, a surplus of 40.
  2. For a normal good, a rightward demand shift normally raises both equilibrium price and quantity; the size depends on the slopes.
  3. A leftward supply shift also raises price. Measurement, quality changes or frictions may also affect observed prices, so more information is needed to identify the shift.
