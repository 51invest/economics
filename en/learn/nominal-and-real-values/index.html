Nominal and Real Values

Nominal values use current prices, while real values are adjusted with a price index; only real values support comparisons of quantities across periods.

Definition

Nominal values are measured at current prices. Real values are adjusted for inflation with a price index and reflect changes in quantities or purchasing power. The relationship is

real value = nominal value ÷ (price index ÷ 100) [1].

The GDP deflator is nominal GDP divided by real GDP, multiplied by 100 [1]. A price index equals 100 in its base year and shows the price level relative to that base. Nominal GDP from different years is not directly comparable; only after removing price change can real output be compared [3]. Purchasing-power comparisons require a price index that matches the consumption basket.

Intuition

A monthly salary rises from 5,000 yuan to 5,250 yuan, a nominal increase of 5%. Over the same period the CPI rises from 100 to 107, so prices are up 7%. The real wage is 5,250 ÷ 1.07 ≈ 4,906.54 yuan, down about 1.87%. The nominal number is larger, but it buys less. A price index measures the price level of a basket, not the price of a single item. To judge whether income, output, or asset value really grew, separate price change from quantity change.

Formal expression

real value_t = nominal value_t ÷ (P_t ÷ 100), where P_t is the price index.

GDP deflator = (nominal GDP ÷ real GDP) × 100.

Inflation is π_t = (P_t ÷ P_{t-1} - 1) × 100%; real growth is g_t = (real value_t ÷ real value_{t-1} - 1) × 100%. The base-year index is 100. Rebasing changes the level of a real series but usually not its growth rate. The real interest rate is approximately the nominal rate minus inflation.

When it applies

  1. The index matches the value. The CPI covers a household consumption basket, while the GDP deflator covers all domestic final output; they are not interchangeable [2].
  2. Base year and weights matter. Base years are updated, and basket weights or chain weighting affect the result.
  3. Quality and new goods matter. Quality improvements, new products, and substitution can make an index overstate the rise in the cost of living.
  4. Relative prices change. Deflating everything with one aggregate index hides differences across sectors.
  5. Real values are estimates. Different indexes produce different real values; there is no single correct one.

Scope note: the approximation “real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation” and the point that the CPI can overstate inflation are not within the supports of the registered sources; where the article says “GDP deflator”, the registered source wording is “GDP price index”, and this article treats them as different names for the same measure rather than as the source’s own term. These are textbook convention and naming convention: a methodological statement, not a conclusion quoted from the registered sources.

Worked example

Suppose nominal GDP is 1,200 in year 1 and 1,500 in year 2, both in 100 million yuan, with GDP deflators of 100 and 125.

Real GDP is 1,200 in year 1 and 1,500 ÷ 1.25 = 1,200 in year 2, in 100 million yuan. Nominal growth is 1,500 ÷ 1,200 - 1 = 25%, while real growth is 0%. Using the CPI instead of the deflator could give a different result. All figures are teaching assumptions.

Common mistakes

Nominal growth equals real growth. They are equal only when the price index is unchanged.

Real values are always below nominal values. That holds only when the price index exceeds 100.

The CPI and the GDP deflator are the same. Their coverage and weights differ.

Deflating wages by the CPI fully restores purchasing power. Taxes, housing, and a household's own basket also matter.

Reading checklist

A final check is the base year: changing it can change the measured growth path even when the underlying output is unchanged. Always report the base, the weighting method and the deflator with the real value.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "ECO-012". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "nominal value". Column two states the transmission channel of "real value". Column three states the institutional boundary of "price index". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "real value" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. price index is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: The same nominal growth can hide opposite real growth

Region A's nominal GDP rises from 1,200 to 1,500 billion yuan, while its deflator rises from 100 to 125. Real GDP stays at 1,200 billion, so real growth is 0%. Region B's nominal GDP rises from 1,400 to 1,650 billion, while its deflator rises from 100 to 150. Real GDP is 1,100 billion, so real growth is -21.4% (1,100 ÷ 1,400 - 1). Nominal growth in B is 17.9%, but real output contracts; ranking by nominal growth would put B ahead of A and reverse the conclusion.

The conclusion reverses when the price indices are not comparable: if A uses a GDP deflator and B uses CPI, and B's import prices rise faster, the two real values cannot be compared directly. The boundary is that real values remove price changes, not quality changes; new products, specification upgrades and free services change the basket itself.

Reading and review

Nominal versus real is not merely “subtract inflation”; it is “which price index, applied to whom”. The GDP deflator covers all domestic output, CPI covers a household consumption basket and PPI covers factory-gate prices; the same 8% nominal growth can produce very different real growth under different deflators. Cross-period comparisons also need base year, chain weighting and product-mix changes. A reproducible routine states the object first, chooses the matching deflator, then reports nominal value, real value and the deflator used, rather than one “real” label. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is measured at current prices? A. Nominal GDP; B. Real GDP; C. Constant-price GDP; D. The real wage.
  2. Calculation. A nominal wage rises from 6,000 yuan to 6,300 yuan while the CPI rises from 100 to 108. What are the real wage and its percentage change?
  3. Conditions. Why might the CPI overstate the rise in the cost of living?
Suggested answers
  1. A. The other three are adjusted with a price index.
  2. The real wage is 6,300 ÷ 1.08 ≈ 5,833.33 yuan; the change is 5,833.33 ÷ 6,000 - 1 ≈ -2.78%.
  3. Consumers substitute toward goods that become cheaper, new products enter the basket with a lag, and quality improvements are hard to price; these can make a fixed-basket index overstate the rise in the cost of living.
