Labor, Population and Education

Labor markets connect population, skills, migration and family decisions; wages and returns to education emerge from those forces together.

ModuleECO-M15 · 劳动人口与教育
LevelFoundation
Read7 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Labor economics studies how people's time, skills and effort are allocated to production and care work, and how wages, employment and population structure change as a result. Human capital is the skills and knowledge accumulated through education, training and experience that raise future productive capacity[1]. A labor market is not a market for “people as goods”: participation, hours, skill matching and family constraints belong in the same decision framework.

Intuition

A high-school graduate chooses between working now and two years of vocational education. Working now pays a wage; studying costs tuition and forgone earnings; graduation may bring a higher wage, but not a guarantee. Only by placing all three cash-flow streams on one timeline can the investment be compared—rather than deciding by slogan.

Formal expression

Labor supply and demand

Labor supply is determined by participation, hours and skill choices; labor demand depends on labor's marginal contribution, capital substitution and product demand. The wage is where supply meets demand, but real markets also carry contracts, minimum wages, unions, licensing and discrimination[3].

Wages and skills

Wage differences can come from skills, industry, region, experience and unobserved ability. The skills premium is the wage advantage of high-skilled over low-skilled workers. Reading a high wage as high skill ignores selection bias: people who enter high-paying jobs may already have stronger ability or networks. Unemployment and participation definitions differ across statistical systems, so cross-country comparisons must check the definition before citing a rate[2].

Matching and migration

Labor markets do not clear instantly; they involve search and matching: vacancies and job seekers must find, assess and adjust to each other. Migration changes the geographic distribution of labor and brings housing, family separation and public-service pressures. The net gain from migration must combine wage gaps, living costs, moving costs and family constraints.

Families and ageing

The division of household work affects who enters the labor market and who provides care. Population ageing changes labor supply, saving and pension burdens; it is not simply “more old people” but a joint change in age structure, retirement rules, healthy life expectancy and care arrangements[5].

Returns to education

Returns to education can be split into private and social returns. Private returns count after-tax benefits and private costs; social returns add spillovers, taxes and public spending. Education also performs a signalling function: a credential conveys trainability as well as skills[4].

Conditions and limits

  1. Supply-demand models assume free entry and adjustable wages; with minimum wages, long contracts or monopsony, the conclusions must be rewritten.
  2. Estimates of education returns must handle ability selection, family background and regional differences; average graduate wages are not causal evidence.
  3. Migration gains depend on housing, registration, language and care institutions; a wage gap is not a net gain.
  4. Ageing projections depend on fertility, longevity and migration assumptions; different assumptions produce very different pension gaps.

Worked example

Suppose two years of vocational education cost 20,000 yuan per year and forgo annual wages of 60,000 yuan; after graduation the wage is 12,000 yuan higher for 30 years, with a 5% discount rate. These are teaching assumptions, not actual data.

Item Value
Two years' tuition 40,000 yuan
Two years' forgone wages 120,000 yuan
Initial total cost 160,000 yuan
Annual wage increment 12,000 yuan
30-year annuity factor at 5% 15.372
Present value of increment 184,464 yuan
Net present value 24,464 yuan

Calculation: 12,000 × 15.372 = 184,464; minus 160,000 leaves an NPV of 24,464. A positive NPV says the investment pays under these assumptions, but it is highly sensitive to the discount rate, wage increment and working horizon. At an increment of 8,000, the NPV turns negative.

Common mistakes

Low pay means low effort. Wages also reflect skill matching, region, industry demand, bargaining power and institutions; blaming personal character hides structural factors.

The return to education equals average graduate pay. Average pay does not deduct tuition and forgone earnings, nor does it handle the selection of who goes to school.

Knowledge-point review

labour supply and demand

Definition: Labour supply is the hours workers are willing to provide; labour demand is the employment or hours firms want given output and costs.

Mechanism: Wages, skills, migration, care duties and alternative income shape supply; demand depends on product demand, productivity and capital substitution.

Example: For example, rising childcare costs may cut parents' hours, while automation reduces demand for some tasks.

Boundary: Labour is not homogeneous; skills, regions, contracts and discrimination segment markets, and wages are not the only adjustment mechanism.

wages

Definition: Wages are labour compensation, including base pay, bonuses, benefits and non-cash terms; real wages adjust for purchasing power.

Mechanism: Wages reflect supply and demand, productivity, bargaining, minimum wages and institutions; efficiency wages and internal labour markets can keep them above market-clearing levels.

Example: For example, two similar jobs in one city can pay differently because of union coverage and firm profitability.

Boundary: Wages are not the only labour cost; social contributions, training and dismissal costs matter, and average wage statistics hide composition and hours.

matching

Definition: Matching is the process by which jobseekers and vacancies select each other and form employment, with match quality affecting productivity and retention.

Mechanism: Information, search costs, geography and skill signals determine efficiency; platforms cut search costs but can amplify screening and bias.

Example: For example, a job platform connects candidates and vacancies quickly, but distorted skill labels make a fast match a poor one.

Boundary: Matching is not one-off; promotions, job redesign and remote work change long-run fit, and coexisting vacancies and unemployment may reflect friction rather than weak demand.

migration

Definition: Migration is the movement of people across regions or borders, driven by wage gaps, jobs, family networks, policy and risk.

Mechanism: It improves labour allocation, sends remittances and changes demographics, but can drain source regions and pressure housing at destinations.

Example: For example, young workers move from inland to coastal jobs and send money home, while labour loss raises wages for those who stay.

Boundary: Migration is not free movement; visas, language, credential recognition, housing and discrimination are barriers, and net gains differ across origins, destinations and groups.

families

Definition: Families are basic units of consumption, saving, care and human-capital investment, with internal division of labour, transfers and gender roles.

Mechanism: Time allocation, marriage, fertility and care shape labour participation; credit, housing and public services change family decisions.

Example: For example, dual-earner families buy childcare and mothers' participation rises, but price and quality determine feasibility.

Boundary: Families are not black boxes; bargaining, violence, inheritance and migration alter resource allocation, and treating them as one utility unit hides inequality.

ageing

Definition: Ageing is a rising share of older people due to longer lives and lower fertility, changing labour, saving, public finance and demand composition.

Mechanism: More pensioners and fewer contributors raise health and care costs; later retirement, migration, productivity and funded saving can ease pressure.

Example: For example, ageing in Japan lifts pension and health spending while changing labour supply and consumption.

Boundary: Ageing is not only a fiscal problem; healthy lifespan, family care, housing and intergenerational fairness matter, and projections depend on migration and fertility assumptions.

returns to education

Definition: Returns to education are the extra earnings, employment and health benefits from schooling, often estimated with Mincer returns.

Mechanism: Returns operate through skills, signalling, networks and productivity; quality, field, local demand and credential inflation change them.

Example: For example, a graduate in a field may earn 30 percent more than a high-school worker, but weak local demand can push graduates away or into low-skill jobs.

Boundary: Years of schooling are not learning; ability bias, family background and signalling contaminate estimates, and social returns differ from private ones.
