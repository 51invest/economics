Externalities, Pigouvian Taxes and Cap-and-Trade: When Price Tools Work

Externalities let private cost omit social cost. A Pigouvian tax puts a price on the damage and feeds it back into decisions, while cap-and-trade fixes the quantity of emissions and lets the permit price adjust. Both can correct overproduction, but price certainty and quantity certainty cannot both be guaranteed; damage measurement, leakage and transaction cost decide when they work.

By the end of this article, you can use marginal external damage to set a starting Pigouvian tax, compare a tax with tradable permits under uncertainty, and judge when putting a price on pollution fails.

Definition

An externality is a cost or benefit imposed on a third party that is not settled through a price. A negative externality makes private marginal cost lower than social marginal cost, so the market produces too much [1]. A Pigouvian tax is a corrective tax per unit of external damage that makes private cost include social cost. Cap-and-trade sets an emissions ceiling and lets regulated entities trade permits; the price is set by scarcity rather than by a tax authority [2].

This article uses “price tools” for two families: a tax that prices damage directly, and a permit system that prices emissions indirectly through a quantity limit. Neither is merely “making firms pay”. Both change the marginal decision: how much social cost does one more unit of output carry?

Intuition

A factory emits 100,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year. Each tonne causes 40 yuan of damage, but the emission itself is free. The factory sees fuel, equipment and labour, not the 4 million yuan of external damage, so output and emissions exceed the social optimum.

If the government taxes each tonne at 40 yuan, every extra tonne costs the factory 40 yuan. It can reduce emissions, change the process, or keep emitting and pay. Whenever abatement costs less than 40 yuan, abatement is cheaper than the tax; above 40 yuan, paying is cheaper. The tax turns “what society would pay to avoid one tonne” into a marginal price for the firm.

Cap-and-trade takes another route: the government issues permits for 60,000 tonnes, and the factory cannot emit more. Firms with cheap abatement cut first; firms with expensive abatement buy permits. The permit price ends up near the cost of the last tonne abated [2]. A tax fixes the price and lets quantity respond; a cap fixes quantity and lets the market discover the price.

Formal expression

Let private marginal cost be MPC, marginal external damage MEC, and social marginal cost:

MSC = MPC + MEC

Without correction, the firm sets MPC = price, so output is above the social optimum where MSC = price. A Pigouvian tax t starts at marginal external damage at the target quantity:

t* = MEC(Q*)

If marginal damage rises with emissions, t* is not a constant; a fixed tax is only approximately optimal in a target range. Cap-and-trade fixes Q at Q_cap, and the permit price p in a competitive market approaches marginal abatement cost:

p ≈ MAC(Q_cap)

where MAC is marginal abatement cost. Tax revenue is t × Q; auction revenue is p × Q_cap. If permits are given away free, the revenue goes to permit holders rather than the government, but the quantity constraint remains.

Under uncertainty there is a key difference: if marginal damage is steeper than marginal abatement cost, a quantity instrument is closer to the optimum; if marginal abatement cost is steeper, a price instrument is closer. This is why price and quantity tools are not interchangeable, and why the practical argument is often “estimate damage first” versus “estimate cost first”.

The damage estimate itself is not a single number. EPA's social-cost work shows that estimates depend on the discount rate, the damage function and the emissions scenario [3].

When it applies

  1. Damage can be measured per unit. A Pigouvian tax needs an estimate of marginal damage per tonne. If damage is threshold-based or highly non-linear, a fixed tax can miss badly.
  2. Marginal damage is relatively stable. Firms respond to the tax; if damage varies sharply by location, time or exposed population, one uniform rate misallocates abatement.
  3. Emission points can be defined and measured. Point sources fit taxes or permits. Non-point sources such as farm runoff or traffic congestion may have monitoring and enforcement costs above the benefit.
  4. Leakage is contained. If dirty production moves to an unregulated region, global emissions may not fall; border adjustment, coverage and cross-border agreements determine whether total damage falls.
  5. Transaction cost does not eat the saving. Permit markets need registries, monitoring, penalties and secondary trading. With few participants, market power can distort the permit price.
  6. Revenue use is stated. Tax or auction revenue can cut other distorting taxes, be rebated, or fund abatement. The use changes distribution, not the marginal abatement incentive.

Worked example

A plant emits 100,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, and marginal external damage is 40 yuan per tonne. Abatement options are shown below (teaching assumptions):

Abatement (10,000 tonnes) Marginal abatement cost (yuan/tonne) Cumulative abatement cost (10,000 yuan)
0–2 20 40
2–4 40 80
4–6 60 120
6–8 90 180

With no policy, emissions are 100,000 tonnes, external damage is 40 × 100,000 = 4 million yuan, and abatement cost is zero.

With a 40 yuan Pigouvian tax, the factory abates the first 40,000 tonnes: the first 20,000 cost 20 yuan, below the tax; the next 20,000 cost 40 yuan, equal to the tax; further abatement costs 60 yuan, above the tax, so the factory pays instead. Emissions fall to 60,000 tonnes. Tax paid is 40 × 60,000 = 2.4 million yuan; abatement cost is 20 × 20,000 + 40 × 20,000 = 1.2 million yuan. Social cost is abatement plus external damage: 1.2 + 40 × 60,000 = 3.6 million yuan, below the 4 million yuan without policy.

A cap of 60,000 tonnes puts marginal abatement cost at 40 yuan, so the permit price is about 40 yuan; abatement and social cost match the 40 yuan tax. If the cap is tightened to 40,000 tonnes, the last 20,000 tonnes cost 60 yuan to abate and the permit price rises to 60 yuan. Abatement cost becomes 400,000 + 800,000 + 1.2 million = 2.4 million yuan; external damage is 40 × 40,000 = 1.6 million yuan; social cost is 4 million yuan. The cap is too tight: marginal abatement cost exceeds marginal damage, and society pays more than the last tonnes are worth.

Conversely, if actual marginal damage is 60 yuan rather than 40, the 40 yuan tax abates too little, while a 40,000 tonne cap is close to optimal. Which instrument is closer depends on the true damage and cost curves, not on the label.

These are teaching assumptions, not real firm or carbon-market data.

Common mistakes

“A Pigouvian tax equals total external damage.” The optimal rate equals marginal external damage at the target quantity, not total damage divided by every tonne. Total damage includes stock effects; marginal damage is the last tonne.

“A cap is always greener than a tax.” A tighter cap does emit less, but if it is too tight, marginal abatement cost exceeds marginal damage and society pays too much for the last tonnes. A quantity tool guarantees the cap, not efficiency.

“Tax revenue is a free bonus.” The tax is a transfer, not a resource. The real costs are abatement and damage. Revenue use affects distribution and other tax burdens, but the transfer itself is not a social gain.

“Free permits and auctions are equivalent.” The marginal abatement incentive may be the same, but distribution is not: free allocation gives scarcity rents to incumbent emitters; auction keeps them in the public budget.

“Every externality can be priced.” Non-point sources, damage thresholds, severe leakage and high enforcement cost can defeat price tools. Direct regulation, standards or public investment may work better.
