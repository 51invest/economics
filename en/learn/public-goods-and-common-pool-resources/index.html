Public Goods and Common-Pool Resources: Why Voluntary Provision Fails

Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival; common-pool resources are rival but hard to exclude. Both make free riding and overuse the default. This article puts the Samuelson condition, the voluntary-provision gap and common-pool quotas into one account, and shows which part government, clubs and community rules can each solve.

Definition

A public good is non-excludable and non-rival: one person's consumption does not stop another's, and non-payers cannot easily be excluded[1]. A common-pool resource is rival but hard to exclude: one boat's catch reduces what others can catch, yet access is difficult to deny[2]. A club good is excludable but non-rival up to capacity, such as a toll road or a membership database.

The four categories are often conflated. Excludability asks whether a provider can charge and exclude non-payers; rivalry asks whether one person's use leaves less for others. Crossing the two dimensions yields private goods, club goods, public goods and common-pool resources. A public good is not simply something the government produces, and a common-pool resource is not a public good.

Intuition

Four households share a flood wall. Once the wall is built, no household can be excluded, and protecting one more household does not reduce protection for the other three. That is non-excludability plus non-rivalry.

Now ask each household to pay voluntarily. Each reasons: if the others pay, I can enjoy the wall anyway; if the others do not, my contribution alone will not build it. Waiting for others becomes the rational move, so nobody pays and the wall is not built. The problem is not that the households do not want the wall; it is that the benefit cannot be captured by the payer. A fishery reverses the sign: access is hard to deny, but each extra tonne caught leaves fewer fish for others. First-come, first-served becomes the default until the stock collapses.

Formal expression

Let MB_i(G) be household i's marginal benefit from public-good quantity G, and let MC(G) be the marginal cost. The Samuelson condition requires the sum of all beneficiaries' marginal benefits to equal marginal cost:

Σ MB_i(G) = MC(G)

If the summed marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, more of the public good is efficient[1]. Under voluntary provision, each household decides using only its own MB_i and ignores others' benefits. If each expects the others to free ride, the Nash equilibrium can sit at G = 0 or far below the Samuelson level. The voluntary-provision gap is:

Gap = G* − G_voluntary

For a common-pool resource, the relevant variable is extraction, not provision. Let regeneration be R and total harvest H; sustainability requires approximately H ≤ R. When each user maximizes short-run pay-off, H overshoots R, the stock falls, and the arithmetic version of the tragedy of the commons appears[2].

When it applies

The Samuelson condition and the provision gap have explicit premises:

  1. Marginal benefits can be summed. If beneficiaries are unknown or benefits cannot be monetized, Σ MB_i is only an approximation.
  2. Non-excludability holds. If technology or institutions can exclude non-payers, a club charge may dominate general taxation.
  3. Non-rivalry holds. If use causes congestion, the good becomes a club good or common-pool resource and needs a capacity rule.
  4. Government provision has costs. Taxation, administration and rent-seeking consume part of the surplus. Public provision does not mean central-government production of every public good.
  5. Common-pool rules must be enforceable. Quotas, property rights and community rules all need monitoring, punishment and exit rules[3][4].

Example and calculation

Four households have marginal benefits of 80, 60, 40 and 20 yuan per year for a flood wall (teaching assumptions); marginal cost is 120 yuan per year. The summed benefit is 200 yuan, above 120, so the Samuelson condition says build it.

Under voluntary provision, each household still faces the full 120 yuan cost. The household with 80 yuan benefit will not carry it alone, and the one with 20 yuan benefit will not either. If all four promise, a free rider can still enjoy the wall; the equilibrium easily falls to nobody paying. The 80 yuan net surplus between the 200 yuan total benefit and the 120 yuan cost is the efficiency space that collective action fails to capture automatically.

For the fishery, let regeneration be R = 100 tonnes per year. Ten users catch 12 tonnes each, so H = 120 tonnes, 20 tonnes above regeneration. A quota of 10 tonnes per user brings total harvest back to 100 tonnes, but the quota must be monitored at the boat level and backed by a credible penalty. Without monitoring and enforcement, the quota is only a number on paper. All figures are teaching assumptions, not observed fishery data.

Institutional comparison

There is no single correct institution for public goods and common-pool resources. Four common arrangements have different boundaries:

Institution Who pays What it solves Typical failure
General taxation Taxpayers share by tax rules Non-excludable, non-rival public goods Weak preference revelation, spending waste
Club charging Users share via membership fees Excludable, non-rival up to capacity High exclusion cost, congestion
Community rules Members share by agreed contributions Small common-pool resources with clear boundaries Monitoring breaks down at scale
Property and markets Owners pay or trade Resources that can be demarcated and traded High demarcation cost, residual externalities

General taxation suits defence and basic research, where beneficiaries cannot be excluded, but a tax cannot reveal each household's true marginal benefit; budget size is easily driven by political visibility rather than measured benefit. Club charging suits toll roads and databases once exclusion technology is reliable; the fee recovers fixed cost, but as traffic approaches capacity, non-rivalry disappears and the membership fee must give way to congestion pricing. Community rules suit small fisheries and irrigation systems when members know one another, monitoring is cheap and penalties are credible; once membership grows and outsiders enter, monitoring and enforcement costs rise and the rule decays into first-come, first-served. Property and markets suit demarcated water bodies and forests, but demarcation itself costs money and may exclude poor users.

The table does not rank institutions from best to worst. It says: first establish excludability, rivalry and monitoring cost, then choose the institution. Institutional mismatch is more common than the vague question of whether government should be larger or smaller.

Failure modes

Even a well-chosen institution can fail in execution. Four failure modes recur:

Preference mismatch under government provision. Household marginal benefits are hard to measure one by one, so budgets follow political visibility rather than measured benefit. The result can be a low-use landmark project funded while maintenance is cut.

Club exclusion and congestion. A toll road uses price to exclude non-payers, but as traffic approaches capacity, non-rivalry disappears. If the membership fee is not converted into a congestion charge, the road jams.

The scale limit of community rules. A small fishery can sustain a quota through peer monitoring; with hundreds of members and outside boats, monitoring and punishment costs rise, and the rule decays into whoever catches first.

The exclusion effect of property rights. Dividing a common-pool resource into private parcels can strengthen investment and stewardship, but it can also exclude low-income users who depend on the resource. Higher efficiency is not the same as acceptable distribution.

These failures are not proof that government always fails or markets always fail. They show that institutional comparison must weigh efficiency, distribution and monitoring cost together, not just the ownership label.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: a public good is whatever government provides. Public goods are defined by excludability and rivalry. Governments also produce private goods; communities, clubs and charities can provide public goods.

Mistake 2: a common-pool resource is a public good. Common-pool resources are rival: one user leaves less for another. Treating a fishery as a public good understates overfishing; treating a public good as a common-pool resource imposes a quota where none is needed.

Mistake 3: voluntary provision always fails. Small groups, strong reciprocity and monitorable community rules can solve part of the common-pool problem. Ostrom's cases document such successes, but they depend on boundaries, rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions, not on good intentions alone[4].

Exercises

  1. Classification: place a streetlight, a toll highway, a deep-sea fishery and an apple into private, club, public and common-pool categories; state excludability and rivalry for each.
  2. Calculation: four households have marginal benefits 90, 70, 50 and 30, and marginal cost is 150. Reproduce the summed benefit, test the Samuelson condition and state the net surplus.
  3. Calculation: if only the 90-benefit household is willing to pay the full 150 in the previous question, what is the net surplus? Why do the other three refuse to share?
  4. Boundary: under what conditions does a public good become a club good? Give a congestion or capacity example.
  5. Comparison: public-good under-provision and common-pool overuse lack, respectively, willingness to pay or extraction constraints. Explain each in one sentence.
  6. Rule design: write a quota rule for a fishery with ten users that includes monitoring, penalties for overharvest and a rule-adjustment mechanism.
  7. Reading: use the excludability-rivalry matrix to classify a resource you know, identify the category it is most often mistaken for, and state the policy error that mistake creates.
