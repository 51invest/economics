Carbon Prices, Allowances and Abatement Costs: The Allocation Ledger of Climate Policy

A carbon tax fixes the emissions price; an emissions trading system fixes the emissions total. Both push firms to abate where it is cheapest, but how allowances are allocated, how revenue is used and whether emissions leak decide who bears the cost behind the same carbon price. Climate policy is not a choice of tool name; it is an alignment of marginal abatement cost, coverage and complementary policy.

Definition

Carbon pricing puts a price on greenhouse-gas emissions, mainly through a carbon tax and an emissions trading system (ETS). A carbon tax sets a levy per tonne of carbon-dioxide equivalent; an ETS sets a cap on total emissions and issues or auctions allowances, with the allowance price formed by supply and demand[1][2]. Marginal abatement cost (MAC) is the incremental cost of reducing one more tonne; free allocation gives allowances to regulated firms based on historical emissions or output benchmarks, while an auction sells allowances to bidders[1][2].

The question here is not "carbon tax or trading", but three separate ledgers: abatement cost, public revenue, and emissions leakage. Even with the same tool name, different allowance allocation and revenue use change who pays and who benefits.

Intuition

A firm has several abatement options: first swapping in efficient lighting at low cost, then changing processes at medium cost, and finally relocating or shutting capacity at high cost. As the carbon price rises, the firm starts with the cheapest options and stops where "the cost of one more tonne" equals the carbon price. That ordering is the marginal abatement cost curve.

A carbon tax tells firms "pay this much per tonne", but total emissions then depend on economic activity and abatement response. An ETS tells firms "only this many tonnes may be emitted", but the allowance price moves with economic conditions. The former fixes price and leaves quantity uncertain; the latter fixes quantity and leaves price uncertain. If the tax rate is set at the allowance price corresponding to the target cap, the two can be equivalent under ideal conditions; in practice, coverage, free allocation, offset rules and market regulation make them differ (author's assessment).

Conditions

  1. When a carbon price covers only part of emissions, uncovered sectors may expand; coverage and offset rules determine the actual reduction[1][3].
  2. Emissions trading requires reliable monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV); otherwise the cap and actual emissions diverge[1].
  3. Both carbon taxes and trading can include price floors, price ceilings or cost-control mechanisms, which change price and quantity uncertainty[2][3].
  4. Free allowances may protect trade-exposed firms, but they also give scarcity rent to incumbent emitters; auction revenue can fund tax cuts, transfers or public investment[1][2].
  5. Carbon pricing cannot carry the whole abatement task alone: standards, public investment, R&D support and grid planning can lower abatement cost and address lock-in and network bottlenecks[3].
  6. Distributional outcomes depend on household energy spending, regional employment and revenue use; a uniform carbon price is not a uniform burden (author's assessment).

Common mistakes

"A carbon tax and an ETS are just different names." Both price emissions, but a tax fixes price and trading fixes quantity. They can be made equivalent under ideal conditions, but coverage, allowance allocation and price controls make them differ in practice[1][2].

"Free allowances have no cost." Free allowances still carry an opportunity cost: the holder could sell them or use them to cover emissions. Free allocation mainly shifts rent; it does not remove the marginal abatement incentive[1][2].

"Domestic abatement equals global abatement." If abatement causes relocation or more imports, the domestic fall may be partly offset abroad. Assess leakage and global net abatement, not just the domestic total[1][3].
