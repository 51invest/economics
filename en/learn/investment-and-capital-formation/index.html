Investment and Capital Formation

Capital formation measures acquisitions less disposals of produced assets on a gross basis, including fixed capital formation, inventories and valuables; only net capital formation equals an increase in the productive capital stock. National-accounts investment is fixed investment plus inventory change plus net acquisitions of valuables, and net investment subtracts depreciation, so positive gross investment does not guarantee a larger capital stock.

Definition

Capital formation measures acquisitions less disposals of produced assets on a gross basis, including fixed capital formation, inventories, and valuables[2]. Physical capital includes plant, equipment, and infrastructure such as roads — OpenStax writes verbatim "The category of physical capital includes the plant and equipment that firms use as well as things like roads (also called infrastructure)."[1]. Only net capital formation, after deducting depreciation, equals the increase in the capital stock. In the national accounts, investment (gross capital formation) is fixed investment plus inventory change plus net acquisitions of valuables[2]. Depreciation is the value of capital used up in production, and net investment is gross investment minus depreciation [3]. Under that produced-asset definition, buying shares or bonds creates no new capital and is not investment in this sense [2].

Intuition

All amounts here are in units of 10,000 yuan. A firm buys a machine for 100 and expects to use it for 10 years. With straight-line depreciation of 10 a year, gross investment is 100 and net investment is 90 in year 1. If the firm buys nothing in year 2, gross investment is 0 and net investment is -10, so the capital stock falls. Gross investment records how much was bought; net investment records how much the capital stock actually grew. This example uses a firm's straight-line depreciation, a business-accounting convention rather than the national-accounts one: BEA's glossary reads verbatim "In business accounting, depreciation is generally measured at historical cost, whereas in the NIPAs, the economic measure of depreciation, “consumption of fixed capital,” is measured at current cost."[4].

Formal expression

I_gross = I_fixed + ΔInventories + V;

I_net = I_gross - D;

K_t = K_{t-1} + I_net;

capital formation rate = I_gross ÷ GDP × 100% [2].

Here D is depreciation, K is the capital stock, and V is net acquisitions of valuables. Inventory change can be positive or negative. Depreciation is an estimate: BEA's handbook footnote 1 writes verbatim of "estimates of the stocks and depreciation of fixed assets"[3] and defines "consumption of fixed capital (CFC), which is a measure of capital used up in production"[3]. Different asset lives or conventions change measured net investment and the capital stock, and the business-accounting and NIPA cost bases differ (see Intuition and [4]), so figures measured on different conventions are not directly comparable. The World Bank's gross capital formation measure also counts acquisitions less disposals of valuables, on top of fixed capital formation and inventories [2], corresponding to V in the formula.

When it applies

  1. Investment is a flow, capital is a stock. The capital stock rises significantly only after years of sustained investment.
  2. Only produced assets, net of disposals, count. The World Bank definition reads verbatim "Gross capital formation includes acquisitions less disposals of produced assets for purposes of fixed capital formation, inventories or valuables."[2] — on that definition shares and bonds are not produced assets and a resale is an acquisition in one set of books against a disposal in another, both of which this article reads as creating no new capital; the page itself never discusses financial assets or second-hand goods. BEA's private fixed investment separately lists "net transactions in used assets"[3], meaning used assets moving between producing units, which is not the same thing as the newly produced capital goods discussed here.
  3. Inventory change can be negative. An unplanned inventory buildup may signal weak demand rather than stronger investment intentions.
  4. Depreciation is estimated. Net investment and the capital stock depend on depreciation assumptions, so figures from different conventions are not directly comparable.
  5. Deflate nominal values. Compare investment across periods using an investment-goods price index.
  6. Capital formation is not growth. Human capital and technology are separate components of economic growth [1]; allocation efficiency and institutions shape output just as much.

Worked example

Suppose a firm starts the year with a capital stock of 4,000, spends 500 on equipment, records 380 in depreciation, and has zero inventory change and zero net acquisitions of valuables, all in units of 10,000 yuan. Net investment is 500 - 380 = 120, so the capital stock ends at 4,000 + 120 = 4,120. The capital formation rate uses economy-wide totals, not one firm's spending divided by national GDP: if a small economy invests 500 over the year and its GDP is 10,000, the rate is 500 ÷ 10,000 × 100% = 5%. All figures are teaching assumptions, not statistics.

Common mistakes

Buying shares is investment. In the national accounts, buying an existing financial asset creates no capital; only newly produced capital goods count [2].

Falling gross investment means a smaller capital stock. As long as gross investment exceeds depreciation, net investment stays positive and the stock keeps growing.

All home purchases are investment. New housing counts as residential investment — BEA writes verbatim "In the NIPAs, the construction of a new house (excluding the value of the unimproved land) is treated as an investment"[3]; a resale only transfers ownership.

A higher investment rate means faster growth. Returns depend on project quality, allocation, and complementary conditions, and inefficient projects can drag growth down.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Investment and Capital Formation"

The subject of this article is "Investment and Capital Formation". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "capital formation". Column two states the transmission channel of "fixed investment". Column three states the institutional boundary of "depreciation". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "fixed investment" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. depreciation is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Boundary case: Positive gross investment can leave net capital formation at zero

Start with a capital stock of 4,000 and buy 500 of equipment during the year, all in units of 10,000 yuan. If depreciation is 380, net investment is 120 and the closing stock is 4,120; with GDP of 10,000, the capital-formation rate is 5%. If depreciation rises to 500, net investment is zero and the closing stock stays at 4,000, while gross investment and the 5% rate are unchanged. One measure says “a lot of investment”; the other says “the capital stock did not grow”.

The conclusion reverses when asset prices or depreciation conventions change: if 100 of the 500 merely replaces worn-out equipment, net investment is only 20; if research spending is capitalised, net formation changes again. The boundary is that gross investment records acquisitions less disposals, while net investment is closer to the change in the capital stock. Using gross investment to infer capacity expansion mistakes replacement for new capacity.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not company accounts.

Capital formation also depends on composition and efficiency. The same 500 million spent on equipment, buildings or software has different effects on capacity and depreciation; if investment concentrates in low-return projects, a higher capital-formation rate can coexist with weaker productivity. Sustainability requires quantity, composition and returns together.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Does buying shares on a secondary market count as investment in GDP?
  2. Calculation. Gross investment is 600, depreciation is 450, and the opening capital stock is 5,000, all in units of 10,000 yuan. Find net investment and the closing capital stock.
  3. Conditions. Why should gross investment rates be compared across countries with care?
Suggested answers
  1. No. No new capital good is produced; only ownership of an existing asset changes hands.
  2. Net investment is 600 - 450 = 150; the closing capital stock is 5,000 + 150 = 5,150, in units of 10,000 yuan.
  3. Relative investment-goods prices, depreciation conventions, the composition of capital, and the structure of GDP all differ; a high investment rate can also reflect low-return projects, so the ratio alone is not enough.
