Growth Accounting: Capital Deepening, Human Capital and TFP

Growth accounting splits output growth into capital, labour, human capital and total factor productivity. It answers whether growth comes mainly from more inputs or from using inputs more efficiently, but it cannot treat the TFP residual as pure technological progress; measurement error, utilization, allocation and rents all sit inside it.

By the end of this article, you can decompose an economy's output growth, judge how much comes from capital deepening versus TFP, and explain why “TFP rose” is not a conclusion to make casually.

Definition

Growth accounting decomposes output growth into the contributions of measured inputs and a residual. Capital deepening means more capital per worker. Human capital is the education, skills and health of workers. Total factor productivity (TFP) is the part of output growth not explained by measured inputs, often called the “residual”.

TFP is not a synonym for technology. It includes technological change but also organization, scale effects, resource allocation, capacity utilization, measurement error and unobserved inputs [1].

Intuition

Suppose an economy's output grows 5%, the capital stock grows 6%, labour input grows 1% and capital's compensation share is 0.4. The capital contribution is 0.4 × 6% = 2.4%, the labour contribution is 0.6 × 1% = 0.6%, and together they explain 3.0%. The remaining 2.0% is the TFP contribution.

That 2.0% may come from better equipment, better management, workers moving from low- to high-productivity sectors, or merely from capacity utilization recovering from a trough or a change in statistical conventions. Growth accounting gives a list of sources, not a causal verdict.

Formal expression

In its simplest form, output growth g_Y is:

g_Y = α g_K + (1 − α) g_L + g_A

where α is capital's compensation share, g_K is capital growth, g_L is labour growth and g_A is TFP growth.

With human capital:

g_Y = α g_K + β g_H + (1 − α − β) g_L + g_A

where g_H is human capital growth and β is its compensation share. Writing output per worker as y = Y/L and capital per worker as k = K/L gives:

g_y = α g_k + g_A

This is the capital-deepening form: growth in output per worker equals the capital-deepening contribution plus the TFP contribution.

When it applies

  1. Factor shares must reflect costs. α and β usually come from labour and capital compensation shares in national income; self-employment income, implicit rents and depreciation treatment change the shares [2].
  2. Inputs and output must share a boundary. Capital stock, labour hours, human capital quality and value added must cover the same sector and period.
  3. TFP is a residual, not an observation. Unmeasured inputs, measurement error and utilization changes all enter the residual [1].
  4. Separate short-run utilization. In a recession, idle labour and capital can make TFP look lower; in a recovery, higher utilization can make it look higher.
  5. Do not turn accounting into causation. A large capital contribution does not prove that capital “caused” growth; investment, technology and institutions may move together.

Problem

Growth accounting answers a calculable question: over a period, how much output growth comes from capital accumulation, how much from labour and human capital, and how much from a TFP residual that measured inputs cannot explain. It does not answer why TFP changed; that requires growth theory, institutional analysis and causal identification.

Inputs and data

The method needs value added, capital stock, labour hours, human capital and factor compensation shares for the same sector and period. Output and inputs must be price-adjusted; the capital stock must state its depreciation method; labour must distinguish persons from hours; human capital must state whether it uses years of schooling, quality or skills. Results from different source definitions and base years are not directly comparable [2].

Assumptions

Standard accounting assumes factors are paid their marginal product, technology is stable over the period and the residual is not strongly correlated with inputs. With monopoly, subsidies, externalities, hidden capital or unmeasured labour, factor shares depart from marginal contributions and the residual absorbs those factors. The result is a decomposition under stated shares and definitions, not a natural law.

Steps

  1. Calculate growth rates for output, capital, labour and human capital.
  2. Obtain capital, labour and human capital compensation shares and check their stability.
  3. Multiply each share by the corresponding growth rate to obtain input contributions.
  4. Subtract input contributions from output growth to obtain the TFP residual.
  5. Change shares, base years and utilization assumptions as a sensitivity check.

Interpreting results

A large TFP contribution may mean better technology, organization or allocation, or a large measurement residual. Negative TFP may mean technological retreat, but it may also mean falling utilization in a recession, capital misallocation or a change in statistical definitions [1]. A large capital contribution means more capital accumulation, not necessarily a high return; read it together with marginal capital output and investment returns.

Failure conditions

The result breaks down when factor shares move sharply, inputs are unmeasured, capacity utilization swings, data definitions change or price adjustment is inconsistent. Comparing two economies with different definitions directly is also a common failure.

Reproduction and verification

Fix the data version, formula, share sources and price base; publish every intermediate result; vary the capital share by 0.05 and check whether the ranking of TFP contributions flips; when comparing with official TFP estimates, compare only the same sector and period definitions [1][2].

Worked example

Suppose two economies, with a capital share of α = 0.4 and a labour share of 0.6 (teaching assumptions):

Economy Output growth Capital growth Labour growth Capital contribution Labour contribution TFP contribution
A 5.0% 6.0% 1.0% 2.4% 0.6% 2.0%
B 3.0% 7.0% 2.0% 2.8% 1.2% -1.0%

More than half of A's growth comes from TFP. B's capital and labour contributions sum to 4.0%, but TFP is -1.0%, so more input did not translate fully into more output. B may have falling utilization, poor capital allocation, measurement error or an unobserved negative shock. It is not enough to say “B did not invest enough”; B invested a lot, but efficiency retreated.

In per-worker terms, if A's capital deepening g_k = 4% and α = 0.4, the capital-deepening contribution is 1.6%. With per-worker output growth of 2.4%, TFP contributes 0.8%. The World Bank's GDP per capita growth indicator uses a related “per person” convention and can be used for comparison, but base years and price adjustment must match [3]. A's per-worker growth therefore comes more from capital deepening than from TFP.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not real economy data.

Common mistakes

“TFP is technological progress.” TFP is a residual that includes technology, organization, allocation, utilization and measurement error [1]. Treating it as pure technology overstates technology and understates institutions.

“A large capital contribution means investment-driven success.” If capital is used at low marginal return, a large contribution may reflect low-return input accumulation; TFP and returns still matter.

“Growth accounting proves causation.” It is a decomposition, not identification; inputs and TFP may both respond to a third factor [2].

“Growth per worker comes only from capital deepening.” Per-worker growth equals the capital-deepening contribution plus TFP; long-run sustained growth depends mainly on TFP, while capital deepening can matter a lot in the short run.

Exercises

  1. Output grows 4%, capital 5%, labour 2%, and capital's share is 0.35. Calculate the capital, labour and TFP contributions.
  2. Country A's capital per worker grows 3%, capital's share is 0.4, and output per worker grows 2%. Calculate the capital-deepening and TFP contributions.
  3. In a recession TFP falls from 1.5% to -0.5%, while capital and labour input also fall. Explain why this is not proof of “technological retreat”.
