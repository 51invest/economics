The question and its scope

Health economics is not mainly about how hospitals make money. It asks where health comes from, who pays, who is paid, and how those arrangements change utilisation, cost and outcomes. Medical care is one input into health, not health itself. Education, income, environment, behaviour, genetics and public health also produce health, and medical care covers only part of the process.

This article follows four interfaces: the health production function, insurance and risk pooling, moral hazard and adverse selection, and payment design and cost-effectiveness. Each has a small, reproducible ledger. The numbers are teaching examples, not official statistics for any country.

Where health comes from: a production function, not a spending list

Write health as H=f(M,L,E,G,I)H=f(M, L, E, G, I)H=f(M,L,E,G,I): MMM is medical care, LLL is lifestyle, EEE is environment, GGG is genetics and early conditions, and III is income and education. The formula is not a precise equation; it is a reminder that the same medical spending produces different health in different living conditions.

Medical input usually has diminishing marginal returns. The first money spent on emergency care, vaccines and primary care buys a lot of health; later money spent on low-value tests or very expensive drugs buys less. Health economics therefore does not treat "spend more" as "become healthier". It asks where the next dollar buys the most health.

Health is also not the same as medical consumption. A person can use many medical services and remain in poor health, or spend little and be healthy. The demand for health is a demand for a health stock; medical care is one input. Confusing the two leads policy to stare at hospital bills while ignoring the inputs that matter more.

How insurance turns bills into premiums

The core of insurance is a risk pool: many people combine uncertain large bills and pay a predictable average cost. The simplest premium is:

premium=expected claims+administration+risk margin\text{premium}=\text{expected claims}+\text{administration}+\text{risk margin}premium=expected claims+administration+risk margin

Suppose 1,000 people each have a 20% chance of a hospital stay with an average bill of 6,000 yuan. Expected claims are 1,000×0.2×6,000=1,200,0001{,}000\times0.2\times6{,}000=1{,}200{,}0001,000×0.2×6,000=1,200,000 yuan, or 1,200 per person. If administration costs 8% of premium and the risk margin is 3%, the premium is about 1,200/(1−0.08−0.03)≈1,3191{,}200/(1-0.08-0.03)\approx1{,}3191,200/(1−0.08−0.03)≈1,319 yuan. If costs are marked up instead, 1,200×1.11≈1,3321{,}200\times1.11\approx1{,}3321,200×1.11≈1,332. The difference comes from the denominator; always state which convention is used.

A larger pool makes the average claim more stable because individual fluctuations are shared across more people. But the pool is not free: it needs underwriting, claims handling, information systems and capital. Economies of scale lower administration per person, while risk selection pushes low-risk people out. The two forces pull in opposite directions.

Insurance also changes the price. Patients see deductibles, copayments and caps, not the full bill. That price is usually below the opportunity cost of care, so utilisation rises. Some of the increase is valuable; some is low-value care. Policy should not suppress all utilisation. It should keep high-value services cheap at the margin and make low-value services more expensive.

Moral hazard: what happens when the price falls

Moral hazard means behaviour or utilisation changes after insurance. Ex post moral hazard is using more care after becoming ill; ex ante moral hazard is investing less in prevention. Neither is a moral accusation; both are responses to incentives.

Imagine a visit with a true cost of 500 yuan, a 20% copayment (100 yuan), and 200 visits a year. Insurance lowers the copayment to zero and visits rise 8%, to 216. The extra 16 visits cost 8,000 yuan, paid by the insurer, while the patient pays nothing. If half of those visits are low-value, the welfare loss is not only 8,000 yuan but also waiting time and unnecessary treatment risk.

Moral hazard is not all waste. Some extra visits are services that were previously rationed by price and are genuinely valuable; some are chronic-care follow-ups that avoid expensive emergencies. The test is the marginal health gain against the marginal cost, not the volume itself.

Copayment is a common tool, but higher is not always better. If copays are too high, patients stop essential drugs and return with more expensive complications. Value-based insurance design makes high-value services cheap and low-value services expensive instead of applying one copayment to everything.

Adverse selection: why low-risk people leave first

Adverse selection occurs when insurers cannot observe individual risk and must charge an average premium. Low-risk people feel overcharged and leave; high-risk people stay; average cost rises; the premium rises again; more low-risk people leave. This loop is the death spiral.

Suppose 1,000 people: 800 have expected annual claims of 800 yuan, and 200 have expected claims of 4,000. Average cost is 800×800+200×4,000=1,440,000800\times800+200\times4{,}000=1{,}440{,}000800×800+200×4,000=1,440,000, or 1,440 per person. If the premium is set at 1,500, the low-risk group sees a 700 yuan excess and may leave. If 400 low-risk people leave, the remaining 600 have average cost 400×800+200×4,000=2,000400\times800+200\times4{,}000=2{,}000400×800+200×4,000=2,000, so the premium must rise.

The remedies include mandates, income-based subsidies, risk adjustment (compensating insurers for age and illness), open enrolment and standardised products. Each has a cost: mandates restrict choice, risk adjustment needs credible diagnostic data, and subsidies need a fiscal source. No design delivers affordability, free choice and zero risk selection at the same time.

Payment design: who carries the incentive, who carries the risk

Fee-for-service pays for each service, so providers who do more earn more; volume tends to rise. Diagnosis-based payment (DRG) pays a fixed price per admission, so providers manage length of stay and materials, but may shift services to outpatient settings or split admissions. Capitation pays a fixed annual amount per person, so providers manage total volume, but may under-serve or select healthier patients.

No payment method is optimal on every dimension. Payment design allocates cost risk and quality responsibility: fee-for-service leaves cost risk with the payer, capitation leaves it with the provider, and DRG leaves it with the hospital. A good design usually combines a base payment, quality indicators, risk adjustment and extra payment for high-value services.

Supplier-induced demand is another interface: when the clinician both decides and is paid for the service, there is an incentive to recommend more tests. Not every clinician behaves this way, but payment rules systematically amplify some behaviours. Clinical pathways, peer review, public quality data and value-based payment are counterweights.

Cost-effectiveness: ICER and QALY

Cost-effectiveness analysis compares cost and health output across options. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) is:

ICER=C1−C0E1−E0\text{ICER}=\frac{C_1-C_0}{E_1-E_0}ICER=E1​−E0​C1​−C0​​

where CCC is cost and EEE is health output, often QALYs. Suppose a new drug costs 30,000 yuan more per patient and adds 0.4 QALY. The ICER is 30000/0.4=7500030000/0.4=7500030000/0.4=75000 yuan per QALY. At a payer threshold of 80,000 it is inside; at 50,000 it is outside.

The ICER is not the whole answer. It says nothing about the budget impact: even a cheap QALY can be unaffordable when covering a million people. It also says nothing about distribution: QALY weights for older, disabled or terminally ill patients are contested. Cost-effectiveness answers efficiency, budget impact answers affordability, and equity answers who benefits. Keep the three ledgers separate.

A numerical ledger: insurance, utilisation and cost-effectiveness

Put the earlier numbers into one account:

Item Baseline With insurance Change
Visits 200 216 +16
True cost per visit 500 500 0
Total cost 100,000 108,000 +8,000
Patient out-of-pocket 20,000 0 −20,000
Insurer payment 80,000 108,000 +28,000

Patient payment falls by 20,000, insurer payment rises by 28,000, and system cost rises by 8,000. The gap comes from extra visits. If the copayment falls from 20% to 10%, utilisation rises but less than under zero copayment; the actual response must be estimated from local data, not copied from the 8% example.

The two tables must not be read as one. The first asks how insurance changes utilisation and payment; the second asks how much health the extra money buys. Policy needs both, plus budget and equity.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, medical care is not all of health. Attributing outcomes only to spending ignores education, environment, behaviour and social conditions. Second, moral hazard is not all waste. Insurance raises both essential and low-value care; judge the marginal value. Third, QALY is not the only scale. Health-state valuations, discount rates and population weights change rankings. Fourth, coverage is not access. Even with insurance, queues, distance, language and out-of-pocket costs can block use. Fifth, cross-country comparisons need care. Prices, practice patterns and accounting conventions differ; "the United States spends more" does not by itself prove inefficiency.

A one-page checklist

  1. Ask where health comes from before asking how much medical care costs.
  2. Split the premium into expected claims, administration and risk margin; state the denominator.
  3. Insurance changes the price, and price changes utilisation; judge marginal value, not volume.
  4. Adverse selection is about the risk pool, not only the premium level.
  5. Payment design allocates risk; no single method is optimal.
  6. Cost-effectiveness uses ICER, budget impact uses total spend, equity uses the distribution of benefits.
  7. Check purchasing power, definitions and years before comparing countries.

Common misreadings

Misreading one: more coverage always means better health. Coverage lowers copayments and improves access, but benefit design and supply capacity determine the result.

Misreading two: moral hazard means patients are irrational. It is a response to prices and contracts; the same design can encourage low-value use and discourage prevention.

Misreading three: capitation controls cost without side effects. It shifts risk to providers and can lead to under-service, patient selection and referral shifting; quality indicators and risk adjustment are needed.

Misreading four: a low ICER means coverage is justified. Budget impact, equity, access and implementation cost also matter. Efficiency is not affordability.

Misreading five: QALY is an objective number. It depends on health-state valuations, discounting and population weights, all of which are contestable choices.

Exercises

  1. With 1,000 people, a 20% admission rate and a 6,000 yuan average bill, recompute the premium. What happens if administration falls to 5%?
  2. If the copayment falls from 20% to 0 and visits rise from 200 to 216, compute the changes in total cost, insurer payment and patient payment.
  3. In the adverse-selection example, what is the average cost if 300 low-risk people leave?
  4. Compare fee-for-service, DRG and capitation; name one quality risk and one cost risk for each.
  5. A drug costs 30,000 yuan more and adds 0.4 QALY. What is the ICER? Is it covered at a 50,000 threshold?
  6. If the drug covers 100,000 patients, what is the budget impact? Can it contradict the ICER conclusion?
  7. Distinguish ex ante from ex post moral hazard, with one medical example of each.
  8. Why is coverage not the same as access? Name three non-price barriers.
  9. Use the health production function to explain why the same spending produces different health in groups with different education.
  10. Write a payer recommendation that includes copayment design, payment method and cost-effectiveness.
