System Costs of the Energy Transition: Grids, Storage and Capacity Markets

Falling levelised costs of wind and solar do not make the whole power system cheaper. Intermittent supply needs grid expansion, storage, balancing and capacity backup, and these system costs are not in the module price. This article puts LCOE, system cost and capacity markets into one account and calculates the true cost of a 60% renewable system.

Definition and intuition

LCOE (levelised cost of electricity) folds construction, fuel, operation and decommissioning into a cost per kWh, used to compare generation technologies. It excludes grid expansion, storage, balancing and capacity backup, and the system cost of managing intermittency.

System cost is the full cost of running a reliable power system: generation, transmission and distribution, storage, balancing plants, capacity backup, ancillary services and curtailment. A low-LCOE renewable may raise the total bill through system costs; a high-LCOE peaker may be indispensable for reliability.

A capacity market pays for available capacity, not just energy. The rationale is that energy-market prices are capped in scarcity and cannot recover the fixed cost of backup plants; without capacity remuneration, the system may lack reliable capacity[1][2].

The system-cost account

A high-renewable system has at least five incremental costs:

  1. Grid expansion: transmission, substations and distribution upgrades to move remote wind and solar to demand centres.
  2. Storage: batteries, pumped hydro or compressed air to shift midday solar to the evening.
  3. Balancing and flexibility: gas, hydro or demand response to fill periods of low wind and solar output.
  4. Capacity backup: reliable capacity for extreme weather and low-probability peaks.
  5. Curtailment: generation forgone when grids or storage are insufficient.

These costs substitute for one another. More storage can reduce peakers, more grid can reduce curtailment, and more demand response can reduce backup. System-cost optimisation is not choosing the cheapest source but choosing the least-cost portfolio[3][4].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a system peaks at 100 GW and targets 60% of energy from wind and solar. Their LCOE is 0.25 yuan/kWh; a gas peaker's LCOE is 0.55 yuan/kWh. On LCOE alone, more renewables look cheaper.

But the system needs 80 billion yuan of grid expansion (0.03 yuan/kWh), 30 GW of four-hour storage (0.06), 20 GW of peakers (0.04) and 15 GW of capacity backup (0.02). System costs total 0.15 yuan/kWh, raising the renewable portfolio from 0.25 to 0.40 yuan/kWh. Gas, though expensive per kWh, supplies only 20% of energy and may lower the total system cost.

This does not make renewables bad; it says comparisons must hold reliability constant. If storage costs fall 40% and grid expansion is deferred, system cost drops sharply. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not actual grid data.

Conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: system costs are local. Resource endowment, grid structure, load shape and interconnection capacity differ, so the same technology has very different system costs across regions.

Boundary 2: capacity markets can overpay. If demand forecasts are too high and auctions are uncompetitive, backup plants earn too much; too little capacity risks blackouts.

Boundary 3: storage is not universal. Four-hour batteries shift daily energy, not seasonal deficits; long-duration storage, hydrogen, hydro and demand response suit different time scales.

Boundary 4: policy changes relative costs. Carbon prices, quotas and subsidies alter competitiveness; state the policy baseline when comparing system costs.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: low LCOE means a cheap system. LCOE covers the plant, not grids, storage and backup.

Mistake 2: a higher renewable share is always harder. Difficulty depends on flexibility; with enough storage, grid and demand response, a high share can run reliably.

Mistake 3: capacity markets are fossil-fuel subsidies. They can buy reliability neutrally, with storage, demand response and flexible load participating; poor design turns them into fossil-fuel subsidies.

Deeper note: system cost, storage and capacity markets

Levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) is not system cost. LCOE covers generation capital and operating costs but not grid connection, backup, storage and curtailment. Wind and solar have low LCOE, but their variable output needs backup and storage, so total system cost can exceed that of baseload plants.

System cost includes generation, transmission, distribution, reserve capacity, storage, peaking and curtailment. As renewables' share rises, cost shifts from energy to capacity and flexibility. Compare resources on system LCOE or value-to-cost, not standalone LCOE.

Storage economics depend on price spreads, cycle life and capacity cost. Markets with wide daily spreads, curtailment and high ancillary-service prices reward storage; markets with narrow spreads and high capacity costs need capacity payments. Storage is not free peaking; it has losses and degradation.

Capacity markets pay for dispatchable capacity to prevent underinvestment in energy-only markets. Design questions: how to define available capacity, how to treat renewables' capacity credit and how to avoid overpayment. Energy-plus-capacity versus scarcity pricing depends on market structure and reliability preferences.

Grid and permitting are overlooked bottlenecks. Transmission lines, interconnection queues and land permits decide whether renewables can deliver; grid investment is slow, and without it generation is built but cannot connect.

Common misuses: comparing LCOE across resources with different dispatchability; treating storage as lossless; ignoring grid and curtailment. Transition cost must be counted at system level, not per plant.

Cases: the duck curve and capacity markets

California's duck curve is typical of high renewable penetration: solar output is high at midday and net load low, then solar falls in the evening as demand rises, so net load ramps fast. The system needs flexibility: storage, demand response, fast-ramping plants and inter-regional transmission. More solar without flexibility means midday curtailment and evening shortages.

Texas uses an energy-only market with scarcity pricing, relying on price spikes to reward dispatchable capacity; the UK uses a capacity market that pays for reliable capacity. Both can maintain reliability but distribute risk and investment incentives differently: scarcity pricing exposes consumers to high prices, capacity markets charge a fixed capacity fee.

Storage revenue has three parts: energy arbitrage, ancillary services and capacity payments. With arbitrage alone, markets with narrow spreads struggle to recover investment; capacity markets or long-term contracts lower financing cost.

Conclusion: the transition policy mix must cover generation, grid, storage and demand together; no single LCOE or market design can judge success.

System-cost checklist

When assessing the system cost of the energy transition, separate 'cheap generation' from 'reliable supply' and check each item:

  1. Does the cost include grids and storage? Comparing LCOE alone omits transmission, balancing and reserve capacity; system cost is a portfolio cost.
  2. Who pays for capacity? Energy markets, capacity markets, strategic reserves and demand response each cover part; without a capacity price, peak investment is missing.
  3. Which spread does storage earn? Intraday arbitrage, ancillary services and capacity payments have different revenue structures; a project relying only on peak-valley spreads is policy-sensitive.
  4. Where is the grid congestion? Corridors, interconnection queues and local opposition decide whether projects connect; a good resource is not automatically deliverable.
  5. Do policy tools conflict? Subsidies, carbon prices, capacity markets and priority dispatch can overbuild or suppress flexible resources when stacked.
  6. Who bears distributional costs? Power bills, taxes, jobs and community compensation determine whether the transition keeps political support.

System cost is not a single price tag but an account of assets, contracts and reserve capability.

What would change the conclusion

System-cost conclusions depend on technology costs and grid conditions:

  1. Storage costs fall faster. If long-duration storage and demand response get much cheaper, system cost is below the static estimate.
  2. Transmission bottlenecks persist. If interconnection queues and corridors lag, cheap generation cannot be delivered and congestion raises system cost.
  3. Capacity markets overpay. If capacity mechanisms reward existing units rather than new flexible resources, costs rise without reliability gains.
  4. Demand flexibility is underestimated. If industrial load, EVs and heat pumps shift at scale, peak capacity needs fall.
  5. Policies stack. If subsidies, carbon prices and priority dispatch all favour the same resource, overbuilding or distorted signals follow.

System cost is not static LCOE addition but the joint result of learning, grid constraints, capacity rules and demand elasticity.

What would change the conclusion (continued)

One more: the amortisation horizon. If grids and storage are depreciated over 30 years and generators over 15, comparisons must use consistent capital costs and lifetimes, or they systematically favour short-lived assets.

Exercises

  1. Distinguish LCOE and system cost; list three items each includes and excludes.

  2. Renewable LCOE 0.25 and system cost 0.15: what is the total?

  3. If storage cost falls 40%, from 0.06 to 0.036, recalculate the total.

  4. Compare more storage versus more grid: what conditions suit each?

  5. When is a capacity market necessary, and when can it overpay?

  6. What time scales suit four-hour batteries versus long-duration storage?

  7. True or false: a 60% renewable share always needs 20 GW of peakers. State the conditions.

  8. Why can wind and solar have low LCOE but higher system cost?

  9. What problem do capacity markets solve, and where is the design difficulty?

  10. What resources does the duck curve show are needed under high renewable penetration?

  11. How do scarcity pricing and capacity markets differ in risk allocation?
