Exchange-Rate Regimes and the Open Economy

An exchange-rate regime determines whether the nominal rate is set by the market, a rule, or official intervention; the core tradeoff is between exchange-rate stability and monetary policy independence.

Definition

An exchange-rate regime is a set of arrangements through which a monetary authority determines, adjusts, and intervenes in the exchange rate. The nominal exchange rate E is the number of domestic currency units per unit of foreign currency, that is, the price of one currency in units of another [1]; the real exchange rate q adjusts for price levels and measures the relative competitiveness of domestic goods.

Intuition

Suppose a good sells for 100 yuan at home and 10 foreign currency units abroad. If E = 7.20 yuan per foreign unit, the foreign good costs 72 yuan and is cheaper, so imports are attractive. A fixed rate works like a price peg: the nominal rate stays stable, but holding it often requires intervention. A floating rate moves with supply and demand, absorbing shocks but sometimes swinging widely.

This section is about direction; the index convention later is about a computable quantity. The intuition that the currency is worth more or less is a movement in the nominal rate E [1], while the real rate folds in both price levels, which is why the table below computes q = E × P* ÷ P line by line. Separating the two shows that saying “the currency depreciated” can name three different things on three layers: E rises; whether q rises depends on the relative change of P and P*; whether trade volumes change depends on elasticities and the invoicing currency (this layering is the article’s own framing, with no new number and no empirical claim here). When reading the table below, first decide which layer it is being read on.

Formal expression

q = E × P* / P, where E is in domestic currency units per unit of foreign currency (the example below uses yuan per foreign unit), P* is the foreign price index and P is the domestic price index; the indices carry no currency, and q has the same unit as E. A rise in q means domestic goods are relatively cheap, a real depreciation; a fall is a real appreciation. A nominal appreciation means E falls; a nominal depreciation means E rises [1]. A nominal effective exchange rate is a trade-weighted geometric average of bilateral rates; a real effective rate adjusts it for relative consumer prices and serves as a gauge of international competitiveness [3].

The regime spectrum:

  • Floating. The market determines the rate; the central bank may intervene but does not commit to a level. Monetary policy is more independent, and the rate is more volatile [2].
  • Soft peg. The market mostly sets the rate, but the central bank intervenes to prevent large swings. Monetary policy is constrained [2].
  • Hard peg. A legal fixed parity or a currency board. Interest rates must follow the anchor currency, and independent monetary policy largely disappears [2]. A shared currency is a separate arrangement with an effect similar to a hard peg, but it merges monetary sovereignty [2]. In the IMF's ten-category scheme, hard pegs include an exchange arrangement with no separate legal tender and a currency board. The former means another currency is sole legal tender or the country belongs to a currency union; the latter is a legislative commitment to exchange the domestic currency for an anchor at a fixed rate, with issuance restrictions [4].

When it applies

  1. The trilemma. A fixed rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy cannot all be fully achieved; under a hard peg, policy serves the rate [2].
  2. Reserves and credibility. Defending a parity requires reserves, controls, or austerity; expectations can trigger speculative attacks.
  3. Nominal versus real. A nominal depreciation need not be real; higher import prices can feed inflation.
  4. Floating is not zero intervention. Central banks may still smooth swings and manage reserves.
  5. Data definitions. Official, market, and parallel rates, period averages versus end-of-period values, and bilateral versus effective rates are not interchangeable.

Scope note: the definition q = E × P*/P, the capital-flow term in the "impossible trinity", the J-curve and its dependence on demand elasticities, the mechanism of a speculative attack, and the notion of parallel exchange rates all lie outside the supports of the registered sources. This article presents them as textbook convention and model assumptions: a methodological statement, not a conclusion quoted from the registered sources.

Worked example

Let E = 7.20 yuan per foreign unit, P = 100, and P* = 100 (price indices, which carry no currency and both start at 100 here), so q = 7.20 × 100 / 100 = 7.20 yuan per foreign unit. If domestic prices rise 5% to P = 105 while E is unchanged, q = 7.20 × 100 / 105 ≈ 6.857 yuan per foreign unit (shown as 6.86 in the table), so q falls by 1 - 100 / 105 ≈ 4.8%: a real appreciation. To restore q = 7.20 yuan per foreign unit, E must rise to 7.56, a nominal depreciation of (7.56 - 7.20) / 7.20 = 5%. Under a hard peg at 7.20 yuan per foreign unit, the authorities cannot adjust E, so competitiveness can only recover through lower domestic costs. All figures are assumptions.

Initial 7.20 100 100 7.20
Domestic prices +5% 7.20 105 100 6.86
Nominal depreciation 5% 7.56 105 100 7.20

Reading and review

Exchange-rate regime choice is not fixed versus floating in the abstract; it is where shocks come from and whether reserves and capital controls can absorb them. A peg reduces trade pricing uncertainty but ties monetary policy to the anchor; a float preserves policy space but leaves firms and households exposed to currency risk. The real rate also moves with inflation, productivity and terms of trade; a stable nominal rate does not mean stable competitiveness. Judge the regime by shock type, reserves, external debt currency and financial resilience.

Common mistakes

A fixed rate is always better. Nominal stability may sacrifice monetary policy, and the real rate can become misaligned.

Floating means no intervention. Most central banks still intervene.

Depreciation always improves the trade balance. The J-curve, import content, and demand elasticities also matter.

The nominal rate equals the real rate. Price differences offset nominal changes.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "ECO-022". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "exchange-rate regime". Column two states the transmission channel of "fixed exchange rate". Column three states the institutional boundary of "floating exchange rate". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "fixed exchange rate" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. floating exchange rate is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Boundary case: A fixed nominal rate can still appreciate in real terms

Start with E = 7.20 yuan per foreign currency and P = P* = 100, so q = 7.20. If domestic prices rise 5% and E is unchanged, q = 7.20 × 100 ÷ 105 ≈ 6.86, a real appreciation of about 4.8%. To restore q = 7.20, E must rise to 7.56, a 5% nominal depreciation. If exporters pass only half of the cost increase into prices, export prices rise 2.5%, q falls only to about 7.02, and the competitiveness hit from the same inflation is halved.

The conclusion reverses when the regime permits adjustment: under a hard peg, E cannot move, so wages, productivity and fiscal policy must adjust; under a float or managed float, E can absorb part of the price gap. The boundary is that a stable nominal rate is not a stable real rate. Judging export competitiveness by the nominal rate misses inflation, unit labour costs and relative traded-goods prices.

All numbers are teaching assumptions; the exchange rate is only an arithmetic example.

The real exchange rate also depends on relative prices of tradables and non-tradables. A nominal adjustment changes only the conversion; if wages, rents and public-service prices rise together, the internal real rate offsets part of the external adjustment. Competitiveness analysis should include unit labour costs, productivity and inflation differentials.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which regime most constrains independent monetary policy? A. Free floating; B. Soft peg; C. Hard peg; D. Floating with inflation targeting.
  2. Calculation. If E = 6.90 yuan per foreign unit, P = 100, and P* = 100 (price indices starting at 100), what is q when P rises to 110? What E restores the original q?
  3. Conditions. Why might a hard-peg economy fall into recession when the anchor currency raises rates?
Suggested answers
  1. C. A hard peg requires interest rates to follow the anchor, so independent monetary policy largely disappears.
  2. q = 6.90 × 100 / 110 ≈ 6.27 yuan per foreign unit; E = 6.90 × 110 / 100 = 7.59 yuan per foreign unit, and at that rate q = 7.59 × 100 / 110 = 6.90, back to the original value.
  3. Defending the parity requires matching the rate increase, which contracts domestic demand and rules out independent easing; if markets doubt the parity, reserve losses add pressure.
