Balance-Sheet Runoff and the Rate Corridor: How Central-Bank Assets Transmit

Quantitative tightening is not simply withdrawing liquidity. Bond runoff on the asset side, reserves and overnight reverse repos on the liability side, and the corridor's floor and ceiling jointly determine whether short rates stay controlled. This article separates the T-account, reserve supply and demand, and the three corridor segments, then calculates a runoff shock.

Definition and intuition

Balance-sheet runoff means letting assets mature without full reinvestment, shrinking the central bank's balance sheet[1]. When a Treasury security on the asset side falls, a liability must fall too: reserves, overnight reverse repos (ON RRP), or Treasury deposits.

The rate corridor is the floor and ceiling the central bank sets for short-term rates. The floor is usually supported by the reserve rate or the ON RRP rate; the ceiling by a standing lending or repo facility[2]. The corridor does not administer a rate; it lets interbank rates fluctuate inside the band, with arbitrage forcing them back when they hit the edges.

Runoff and the corridor are not separate issues. Runoff changes how scarce reserves are; the corridor determines how that scarcity becomes a rate. Looking only at the asset side and ignoring which liability absorbs the decline misreads the shock.

Transmission

Let the banking system's minimum reserve need be R_min, actual reserves R, and the ON RRP balance RRP. When maturing bonds are not reinvested, assets fall by ΔA and liabilities absorb it in sequence: first RRP, then reserves R. As long as RRP is positive, runoff mainly pushes reverse-repo cash back into the market and has little effect on reserves or short rates.

Once RRP approaches zero, further runoff directly drains reserves. Reserves move from abundant to ample to scarce, and banks compete for cash in the repo market to meet settlement, regulatory and liquidity needs, pushing short rates up. The corridor floor (the reserve rate) is no longer the anchor; market rates may sit near the ceiling, and if the ceiling facility carries stigma, they can briefly overshoot.

The sequence is: ΔA ↓ → RRP ↓ → R ↓ → repo rate ↑ → corridor position changes. That is why the same runoff had a small effect in the first half of 2022–2024 and a larger effect later: the first phase had an RRP buffer, the second exhausted it.

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose the central bank holds 400 billion yuan of government bonds, with liabilities of 240 billion reserves, 100 billion ON RRP and 60 billion Treasury deposits. It lets 50 billion of bonds mature without reinvestment.

Round one: RRP falls from 100 to 50 billion and reserves stay at 240. Assets and liabilities both fall by 50 billion; short rates barely move.

Round two: another 50 billion matures. RRP is now zero, so reserves fall from 240 to 190 billion. If the minimum reserve need R_min is 200 billion, the gap is 10 billion. Banks borrow in repo, the repo rate rises; with a corridor floor of 4.50% and ceiling of 4.75%, the equilibrium rate rises from 4.55% to 4.72%, close to the ceiling.

Round three: another 30 billion of runoff takes reserves to 160 billion and the gap to 40 billion; the repo rate may break the ceiling. The central bank must slow runoff, activate the standing repo facility, or adjust reserve requirements. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not an actual balance sheet.

Historical comparison

The 2013 taper tantrum showed that a runoff shock can arrive before the actual runoff: the Fed only signalled slower purchases, yet emerging-market flows and long rates moved first. The September 2019 repo spike showed that once reserves approach scarcity, overnight rates can jump within hours; the central bank had to restart temporary repo operations. The post-2020 quantitative easing pushed reserves back into abundance, and the runoff that began in 2022 again relied on the ON RRP buffer first.

The common lesson: read runoff through the liability buffer, corridor credibility and changing reserve demand, not through the asset size alone. The balance sheet is a stock and the repo market is a flow; a blockage anywhere in between shows up first in short rates[4].

Reading the data

Read at least four stocks: the maturity profile of Treasuries and MBS on the asset side, reserve balances, ON RRP balances and Treasury deposits. Then read three prices: the reserve rate, the ON RRP rate and SOFR.

If reserves and ON RRP fall together, runoff is consuming the buffer. If reserves fall while ON RRP is unchanged, Treasury deposits or other liabilities may be absorbing the decline. If SOFR rises persistently relative to the corridor floor, reserve distribution or settlement demand is tightening. One number is not enough; four stocks and three prices must be read together, and the Eurosystem balance sheet offers a cross-check[3].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: liability structure drives the shock. If the liability side holds large ON RRP or Treasury deposits, runoff can absorb those non-reserve liabilities first and delay the shock. If reserves are already near the minimum, runoff quickly becomes a rate shock.

Boundary 2: credibility of corridor tools. If the ceiling facility carries stigma, banks avoid it and market rates can overshoot. If the floor facility covers too few institutions, non-banks can push rates below the floor.

Boundary 3: regulation and settlement demand. Reserve demand is not constant; quarter-ends, tax dates and Treasury settlement temporarily raise it, so the same reserve level implies different rates on different days.

Mistake 1: runoff equals a rate hike. Runoff changes reserve supply; a hike changes corridor parameters. They can move together or in opposite directions.

Mistake 2: a smaller balance sheet is always better. Runoff that is too fast can break short-term funding markets; the central bank must balance exit from accommodation against keeping reserves ample.

Deeper note: the sequencing of runoff, reserves and the corridor

Runoff is not simply 'selling assets'. The central bank lets maturing bonds run off without reinvestment, the balance sheet shrinks and bank reserves fall; if the Treasury is also issuing debt and tax receipts rise, reserves fall faster. The pace depends on the maturity profile, reinvestment rules and Treasury balances.

As reserves move from ample to scarce, the corridor operates differently. When ample, short rates are anchored by IORB and ON RRP; when scarce, open-market operations and reserve demand matter again and repo rates are more volatile. The September 2019 repo spike was falling reserves meeting quarter-end and tax dates.

Runoff and rate hikes should be read separately. Hikes change the price, runoff changes the quantity; tightening both at once amplifies the shock. With high inflation and stable growth, both can run; with weakening growth, stop hiking first and run down slowly to avoid double tightening.

Communication order: give the market the runoff path and endpoint before executing; when funding tightens, adjust reinvestment or use repo facilities rather than stopping abruptly. The sequence and transparency of tools determine whether runoff is predictable or a shock.

Monitoring indicators: reserve balances, ON RRP, Treasury balances, repo-IORB spread, and the quarter-end and tax calendar. Read all five to judge whether reserves are ample or near scarce.

Common misuses: treating runoff as 'necessarily recessionary'; treating falling reserves as 'liquidity disappearing'; ignoring Treasury balances and quarter-end. Runoff is a quantity tool whose impact depends on the starting point, speed and buffers.

Balance-sheet runoff checklist

When a headline says 'runoff' or 'falling reserves', separate five things before treating the balance-sheet size as the policy stance:

  1. Active sales or passive run-off? Active sales hit market prices harder; passive run-off decays naturally but affects MBS duration and mortgage rates differently.
  2. Are reserves falling because of runoff or Treasury draining? Tax payments, issuance and repo all move reserves around; the asset side alone misreads interbank liquidity.
  3. Which side of the corridor binds? With ample reserves, IOER/ON RRP-like tools anchor the floor; with scarce reserves, open-market operations and the discount window matter again.
  4. Who bears rate volatility? Money funds, banks, dealers and offshore dollar borrowers have different sensitivities; a wider or narrower corridor has distributional effects.
  5. Are the operational details sustainable? Reserve demand shifts with regulation, payments and bank behaviour; a 'sufficient' level can become insufficient in stress.

The checklist keeps size, price and distribution separate; a conclusion missing any one of them is only half an answer.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails."
