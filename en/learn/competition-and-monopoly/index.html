Competition and Monopoly

A perfectly competitive firm takes the market price and produces where price equals marginal cost; a monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve, restricts output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost and charges a higher price.

Definition

Perfect competition is a market with many sellers, identical products, free entry and exit, and full information. Each firm is too small to affect the price and is a price taker [1]. Monopoly is a market in which one firm produces all of the output and faces no significant competition; the textbook says such a firm holds a great deal of market power and sets price subject to the demand curve [4]. It faces a downward-sloping demand curve and chooses output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost [2], while legal, technological or market barriers to entry keep potential competitors out [5]. This article summarises the same structure as "no close substitute"; on the cited page that phrase appears only in the new-drug example, not in the definition [4].

Intuition

A farmer in a competitive market sells any quantity at the market price, so selling one more unit does not lower the price and marginal revenue equals price [1]. A monopolist is the only seller: to sell more it must lower the price, and the lower price applies to every unit sold, so marginal revenue is below price. The monopolist cannot charge any price, because demand limits purchases. It restricts output to keep price above marginal cost.

Formal expression

A perfectly competitive firm has P = MR and chooses output where P = MC. In the short run it keeps producing while price covers average variable cost; in the long run economic profit tends to zero [1].

For a monopolist, demand is P = 100 - Q, total revenue is TR = 100Q - Q², marginal revenue is MR = 100 - 2Q, and marginal cost is constant at MC = 20 (output in units; prices and costs in yuan per unit). Profit maximization requires MR = MC [2]: 100 - 2Q = 20, so Q = 40, and the demand curve gives P = 60. Assuming no fixed costs, profit is (60 - 20) × 40 = 1600 yuan.

If the same market were perfectly competitive, P = MC = 20 and Q = 80. Consumer surplus would be 0.5 × (100 - 20) × 80 = 3200 yuan. Under monopoly, consumer surplus is 0.5 × (100 - 60) × 40 = 800 yuan, and the deadweight loss is the triangle between the demand curve and marginal cost on the units not produced, 0.5 × (60 - 20) × (80 - 40) = 800 yuan (computed from this teaching model).

When it applies

  1. The four competitive conditions. Many sellers, identical products, free entry and exit, and full information; these are rarely fully met, so it serves as a benchmark [1].
  2. Monopoly needs barriers to entry. The textbook groups barriers into five types: natural monopoly from economies of scale, control of a scarce physical resource, legal monopoly, patents/trademarks/copyrights, and intimidating potential competitors [5]. This article adds network effects: that item is outside the five and needs its own source before it can be stated as fact.
  3. A single price, with no price discrimination. If the monopolist can charge different buyers different prices, output, price and the welfare split change.
  4. A static model. Technology, demand and cost are given; the model does not measure innovation or dynamic efficiency.
  5. Structure is not conduct. Real markets are often oligopolies or monopolistically competitive; the Sherman Act outlaws "every contract, combination, or conspiracy in restraint of trade" and any "monopolization, attempted monopolization, or conspiracy or combination to monopolize", and courts decide which practices are illegal based on the facts of each case [3]. The page never uses the word structure and never calls a monopoly structure illegal by itself.

Worked example

Using the teaching assumptions P = 100 - Q and MC = 20:

Outcome Output Price Consumer surplus Profit Total surplus
Perfect competition 80 20 3200 0 3200
Monopoly 40 60 800 1600 2400

The monopolist cuts output from 80 to 40 units and raises price from 20 to 60 yuan. Units 40 to 80 are worth at least the 20-yuan-per-unit marginal cost, but they are not produced, creating a deadweight loss of 800 yuan. The demand, cost and results are teaching assumptions, not estimates of a real industry.

Common mistakes

A monopolist can set any price. Demand limits the quantity-price combinations; a higher price means fewer buyers.

Perfect competition means no profit. Long-run economic profit tends to zero, but accounting profit can be positive because the opportunity cost of resources is already included.

Monopoly price equals marginal cost. The monopolist chooses output where MR = MC and reads price from the demand curve, so price is normally above marginal cost [2].

Monopoly structure is always illegal. Patents, natural monopolies and competition policy differ; the antitrust laws proscribe unlawful mergers and business practices in general terms and leave illegality to the facts of each case, while "per se" violations cover only rival arrangements to fix prices, divide markets or rig bids [3].

Knowledge-point review

perfect competition

Definition: Perfect competition has many small buyers and sellers trading a homogeneous product with free entry and full information, so no participant can set price.

Mechanism: Price equals marginal cost and long-run economic profit is zero; supply sums marginal costs, and demand shocks mainly adjust quantity.

Example: For example, many farmers selling identical wheat in a wholesale market must accept the market price.

Boundary: Perfect competition is a benchmark, not a description; product differentiation, brands, fixed costs, asymmetric information and entry barriers all break it.

monopoly

Definition: A monopoly is a market with one seller controlling supply, no close substitutes and no effective entry threat.

Mechanism: The monopolist sets marginal revenue equal to marginal cost, pricing above marginal cost and creating deadweight loss; price discrimination can extract more consumer surplus.

Example: For example, a city with one water utility and fixed pipes can charge households above marginal cost.

Boundary: Natural monopoly, patents and licences can create monopoly, but substitutes, potential entry and regulation constrain pricing; high share alone does not prove monopoly.

monopolistic competition

Definition: Monopolistic competition has many firms selling differentiated products with free entry, yet each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve.

Mechanism: Differentiation gives short-run pricing power, while entry drives long-run economic profit toward zero; firms compete on price, quality and brand.

Example: For example, several neighbourhood coffee shops with distinct styles can charge slightly different prices, but new entrants erode excess profit.

Boundary: Differentiation must be perceived and substitutable; if it is only labelling and search costs are low, pricing power fades quickly.

oligopoly

Definition: An oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms whose actions materially affect one another.

Mechanism: Firms price strategically, potentially colluding, fighting price wars or following a leader; output and price depend on reaction functions and entry threats.

Example: For example, two airlines on one route may match each other's fare cuts, ending in a price war or tacit coordination.

Boundary: Oligopoly models are sensitive to information, retaliation speed and product homogeneity; concentration does not prove collusion, and competition law needs conduct evidence.

platforms

Definition: A platform links two or more user groups and creates value through matching and rules; its core product is the transaction and interaction environment.

Mechanism: Platforms often subsidise one side and charge another to ignite network effects; pricing depends on cross-side externalities, elasticities and multihoming costs.

Example: For example, an app store charges developers a commission while users join free, attracting both sides into an ecosystem.

Boundary: Platforms are not ordinary intermediaries; multihoming, exclusivity, data advantages and self-preferencing change competition, and regulation must distinguish market definition from ecosystem boundaries.

network effects

Definition: Network effects arise when one user's participation raises value for others, either directly on the same side or indirectly across sides.

Mechanism: They can produce winner-take-most outcomes and entry barriers, but congestion, noise and multihoming can make them diminish; critical mass determines take-off.

Example: For example, more users make a messaging app more valuable to join, while excessive advertising can degrade the experience and weaken same-side value.

Boundary: Network effects are not unbounded; negative effects, user heterogeneity and switching costs shape equilibrium, and large scale does not imply higher welfare.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is a barrier to entry? A. Patent protection; B. Many sellers; C. Identical products; D. Free entry.
  2. Calculation. If demand is P = 120 - Q, MR = 120 - 2Q and MC = 20 yuan per unit (prices in yuan per unit), what are the monopoly output, price and profit? What are the competitive output and price?
  3. Conditions. Why is the monopoly outcome described as inefficient?
Suggested answers
  1. A. A patent keeps rivals out; the other three are features of perfect competition.
  2. Monopoly: 120 - 2Q = 20, so Q = 50 units, P = 70 yuan, and profit, assuming no fixed costs, is (70 - 20) × 50 = 2500 yuan. Competition: 120 - Q = 20, so Q = 100 units, P = 20 yuan.
  3. At output 40 the price of 60 exceeds marginal cost of 20; units 40 to 80 are valued at least at their production cost but are not made, so total surplus is 800 yuan below the competitive outcome.
