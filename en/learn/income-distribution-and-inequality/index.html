Income Distribution and Inequality

Income distribution describes how income is spread across households; quintile shares, the Lorenz curve, and the Gini coefficient measure inequality, but definitions shape the conclusion.

Definition

Income distribution is the spread of income across factor owners, households, or individuals; inequality is the degree of difference in that distribution. Functional distribution focuses on the shares of labor and capital; personal or household distribution focuses on who receives how much. These two labels are the article's own vocabulary: the two retrieved pages (15.4 and 15.5) contain none of the word faces functional distribution, factor share, labor share, market income, before taxes, after taxes, purchasing power, equivalence, family size, capital gains, mobility or Gini once tags are stripped; 15.4 gives the measuring steps verbatim as "Measure income distribution in quintiles" and "Calculate and graph a Lorenz curve" [1] but never draws the functional/personal distinction.

Intuition

Line households up from poorest to richest and divide them into five equal-sized groups (quintiles) [1]. If income were perfectly equal, each group would receive 20%. A top group with 50% and a bottom group with 5% indicates a wide gap. The Lorenz curve plots cumulative population shares against cumulative income shares; the farther the curve lies below the 45-degree line, the greater the inequality [1]. The Gini coefficient compresses that gap into a value between 0 and 1; multiplying by 100 gives a Gini index from 0 to 100, and on either scale 0 means perfect equality and the top end means perfect inequality [3].

Formal expression

  • Quintile share = income of the group / total income, expressed as a percentage and unitless [1].
  • Lorenz curve L(p): cumulative income share at cumulative population share p, with L(0)=0, L(1)=1, and L(p) ≤ p; both p and L(p) are shares between 0 and 1 [1].
  • Gini coefficient G = 1 - 2∫₀¹ L(p) dp (the standard formula used here), unitless, ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality); multiply by 100 for a 0–100 Gini index, where 0 is perfect equality and 100 perfect inequality [3].
  • Top-to-bottom quintile ratio = top group share / bottom group share, a ratio and therefore unitless.

The Gini coefficient is a single summary: different distributions can produce the same value; it also depends on the income definition, unit, and level of analysis.

When it applies

  1. Income definition: market income, pre-tax or post-tax income, disposable income, and in-kind benefits are not directly comparable.   5. Cross-country comparisons require a common PPP, year, and definition. This item is the article's own (the two retrieved pages (15.4 and 15.5) contain none of the word faces functional distribution, factor share, labor share, market income, before taxes, after taxes, purchasing power, equivalence, family size, capital gains, mobility or Gini once tags are stripped; purchasing power does not appear).
  6. Normative and causal claims: the channels through which inequality affects growth are disputed, so correlation is not causation.

Policies such as redistribution, a ladder of opportunity, and inheritance taxes can change the income distribution, but there is a tradeoff between equality and output [2]; evaluation requires stating whether income is measured before or after taxes and who receives the gains.

Beyond measurement, causes are part of the subject: OpenStax lists changes in household composition (for example a rising share of single-person households) and changes in the wage distribution (technology and globalisation affecting skill groups differently) among the sources of income inequality [1]. The point constrains how a Gini reading should be used: the same value can come from a shifting wage structure, from household composition, or from the age and retirement mix, and those point to different policy levers. The three registered sources are not enough to rank the causes, so no ranking is offered here.

Worked example

Five households have incomes 10, 20, 30, 40, and 100, totaling 200, all in the same currency unit (no currency, year, or income concept is specified here). Each household is one quintile, so the quintile shares are income divided by the total: 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 50%; the top-to-bottom ratio is 50% ÷ 5% = 10. The Lorenz points are (20%,5%), (40%,15%), (60%,30%), (80%,50%), and (100%,100%). Using trapezoids of width 0.2, the area under the curve is 0.2 × [(0+0.05)/2 + (0.05+0.15)/2 + (0.15+0.30)/2 + (0.30+0.50)/2 + (0.50+1.00)/2] = 0.30, so the Gini coefficient is 1 - 2 × 0.30 = 0.40, and multiplying by 100 gives a Gini index of 40. Shares, ratios, and the Gini coefficient depend only on the relative distribution of income, not on the currency or the size of the unit. All figures are teaching assumptions.

Common mistakes

A higher Gini coefficient means a poorer country. Inequality and income level are different things.

Income inequality equals wealth inequality. The two are different objects, and 15.5 separates them verbatim: "Income is a flow of money received, often measured on a monthly or an annual basis. Wealth is the sum of the value of all assets, including money in bank accounts, financial investments, a pension fund, and the value of a home. In calculating wealth, one must subtract all debts" [2]; the same section states verbatim "In the United States, the wealth distribution is more unequal than the income distribution, because differences in income can accumulate over time to make even larger differences in wealth." [2] - for the United States the wealth distribution is more unequal than the income distribution, because income differences accumulate. "Affected by asset prices" is our addition; that passage does not discuss asset-price movements.

A Gini of 0.4 means 40% of people are poor. It is a distribution summary, not a poverty rate.

The Gini coefficient is enough. Also examine the Lorenz curve, quintiles, and mobility.

Redistribution is costless. 15.5 states the learning outcome verbatim as "Show the tradeoff between incentives and income equality" [2], so an incentive-versus-equality tradeoff is on the page; the "efficiency" and "administrative" terms are ours - the two retrieved pages (15.4 and 15.5) contain none of the word faces functional distribution, factor share, labor share, market income, before taxes, after taxes, purchasing power, equivalence, family size, capital gains, mobility or Gini once tags are stripped - and are not this page's wording.

Knowledge-point review

poverty

Definition: Poverty is insufficient income or consumption for basic needs, and can also be defined multi-dimensionally through capability, health and exclusion.

Mechanism: Growth, jobs, education, prices, transfers and shocks determine it; absolute and relative lines answer different questions.

Example: For example, food inflation can push a recently escaped household back into poverty even when average income is unchanged.

Boundary: Poverty-line choices change counts and targets; subsistence farming, informal work and intra-household inequality can hide real deprivation.

inequality

Definition: Inequality is disparity in income, wealth, opportunity and health, split into inequality of outcomes and of opportunity.

Mechanism: Market income, taxes and transfers, capital returns, education and inheritance shape distribution; Gini, shares and mobility capture different dimensions.

Example: For example, the top 10 percent holding most financial assets gain from asset booms, widening wealth gaps even if wage gaps are modest.

Boundary: Inequality is not one moral verdict; moderate incentives and extreme concentration can both be costly, and measurement is distorted by avoidance, in-kind income and longevity.

property rights

Definition: Property rights are bundles of rights to use, earn from, transfer and exclude others from a resource, defined by law, custom and contract.

Mechanism: Clear, enforceable and transferable rights lower transaction costs and encourage investment; unclear rights breed conflict, short-termism and depletion.

Example: For example, farmers with transferable land rights invest more in irrigation and long-term improvement than those facing arbitrary expropriation.

Boundary: Property is not absolute ownership; registration, enforcement, expropriation and customary claims determine real strength, and formal titles can exclude vulnerable users.

state capacity

Definition: State capacity is the organisational and fiscal ability to tax, enforce law, provide public goods and maintain order.

Mechanism: It rests on revenue, bureaucracy, information, infrastructure and legitimacy; wars, crises and resource rents can build or weaken it.

Example: For example, a state that can tax and register property is better able to build roads, schools and disaster response.

Boundary: Strong capacity is not necessarily good outcomes; it can serve repression or rent-seeking, and accountability, rule of law and participation shape direction.

institutions

Definition: Institutions are formal rules, informal norms and enforcement mechanisms governing repeated interaction, shaping incentives, transaction costs and power.

Mechanism: Property rights, contracts, taxes, public goods and violence constraints shape investment and innovation; organisations, path dependence and feedback reinforce them.

Example: For example, the same technology diffuses at different speeds because patent enforcement, finance and bureaucratic approval differ.

Boundary: Institutions are not a universal explanation; culture, geography, technology and shocks matter, institutional variables risk tautology, and transplants need local fit.

development policy and transition

Definition: Development policy covers investment, industrial, trade, education and institutional measures for growth, poverty reduction and structural change.

Mechanism: Policies allocate resources through public goods, incentives and coordination; capacity, pilots, evaluation and exit shape results, and transitions must handle incumbents.

Example: For example, East Asian economies combined basic education, export infrastructure and macro stability for rapid growth, while similar policies failed elsewhere due to weak implementation.

Boundary: There is no universal template; industrial picking, subsidy capture, debt and dependence carry risks, and success stories reflect initial conditions and survivorship bias.

Data and institutional check: income distribution

Before using "income distribution" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns income inequality.

Third, the institutional boundary. Gini coefficient is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: income distribution

Learning "income distribution" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "income inequality": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "Gini coefficient" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of income distribution still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind income inequality reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "income distribution" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "income inequality" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.
