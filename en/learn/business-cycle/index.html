The Business Cycle

The business cycle alternates expansion and contraction; a recession runs from peak to trough, and the dates are usually assigned after the fact.

Definition

The business cycle is the alternation of expansion and contraction in overall economic activity. A recession is a significant decline in real GDP; a depression is an especially lengthy and deep recession [1]. A peak is the high point before a recession, and a trough is the low point before recovery. A recession runs from peak to trough, and an expansion runs from trough to peak [1].

Intuition

The economy does not grow in a straight line. Demand shocks, supply shocks, financial conditions, and policy changes spread through income, employment, and investment, pushing output around its potential level. In a recession, firms cut production and hiring and unemployment rises; in an expansion, the reverse occurs. Cycle length and depth vary, so historical averages are not a timetable.

Formal expression

g_t = (Y_t ÷ Y_{t-1} - 1) × 100%, where Y_t is real GDP, that is, output already adjusted for price changes and measured as a flow in the statistic's own currency per year; g_t is in % per year.

output gap = (Y - Y*) ÷ Y* × 100%, where Y* is estimated potential output; the denominator is potential output, not actual output, so a negative gap means actual output sits below potential.

Recession depth = Y_trough ÷ Y_peak - 1; the gain needed to regain the peak = Y_peak ÷ Y_trough - 1. The two use different denominators, so their absolute values are not equal.

The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) is a private research organization that tracks U.S. business cycles and announces peak and trough dates [1]. Recession effects can persist after the official end date.

When it applies

  1. Real GDP is revised. First estimates are not final [2], and two quarterly declines do not automatically make an official recession; dates are assigned after the fact.
  2. Potential output is unobservable. The output gap depends on models and estimates and can be revised.
  3. Cycles are irregular. Expansions and recessions differ in length and depth, so a fixed cycle cannot forecast the next turning point.
  4. Aggregates hide differences. National GDP can rise while some regions, industries, or households are still contracting.
  5. Slower growth is not a recession. Lower positive growth is still expansion; a recession requires a significant decline in activity.

Scope note: potential output and the output gap, the "two consecutive quarters" rule of thumb, the NBER’s multi-indicator determination, and the tendency of equity markets to lead the real economy are all outside the supports of the registered sources (the OpenStax 19.3 review confirms it contains neither "output gap" nor "two quarters"). This article presents them as textbook convention and empirical practice — a methodological statement, not a conclusion quoted from the registered sources.

Worked example

Suppose an economy's real GDP follows this hypothetical path, stated as a constant-price teaching index with year 1 equal to 100 and not converted into any currency; only its ratios are used:

Year Real GDP (teaching index) Annual growth Phase
1 100.0 — Expansion
2 102.0 +2.00% Peak
3 100.5 -1.47% Trough
4 101.5 +1.00% Recovery
5 104.0 +2.46% Expansion

The year-3 decline is 100.5 ÷ 102.0 - 1 ≈ -1.47%; regaining the year-2 peak requires 102.0 ÷ 100.5 - 1 ≈ +1.49%. If potential output in year 3 is 103.0 on the same index, the output gap is (100.5 - 103.0) ÷ 103.0 ≈ -2.43%. Annual growth in the table is measured against the previous year and rounded to two decimal places; all figures are teaching assumptions, not data.

Common mistakes

Two consecutive quarters of decline mean an official recession. That is a common rule of thumb, not the only criterion; dates are assigned after a broader assessment.

A recession is a depression. A depression is an especially lengthy and deep recession, with no fixed threshold.

Growth below trend is a recession. Positive growth is still expansion, only slower.

A stock-market fall is a recession. Equity prices reflect expectations and discount rates and can move independently of real activity.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Define peak, trough, recession, and expansion.
  2. Calculation. On the same kind of hypothetical constant-price index, with no currency conversion, real GDP falls from 200 to 190 and then rises to 193. Find the recession decline, the rebound, and how far output still trails the prior peak.
  3. Conditions. Why are two quarters of initial GDP data insufficient to establish a recession?
Suggested answers
  1. The peak is the high point before a recession; the trough is the low point before recovery; a recession runs from peak to trough; an expansion runs from trough to peak.
  2. The decline is 190 ÷ 200 - 1 = -5.00%; the rebound is 193 ÷ 190 - 1 ≈ +1.58%; output remains 193 ÷ 200 - 1 = -3.50% below the prior peak.
  3. GDP estimates are revised, and dating also relies on a range of indicators such as employment and is settled after the fact; two quarters of initial readings can mislead.
