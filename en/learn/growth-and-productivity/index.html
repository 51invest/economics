Growth and Productivity

Growth comes from more inputs or more output per input; sustained gains in output per person depend on labor productivity, not longer hours alone.

ModuleECO-M09 · 长期增长
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 11 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Economic growth is a sustained increase in real output, usually measured by real GDP. Labor productivity is output per unit of labor input, commonly real GDP per hour worked [1]. Total factor productivity (TFP) is output growth not explained by measured inputs such as capital and labor; it proxies for technology, organization, and efficiency [2].

Intuition

An economy can produce more through more hours or more output per hour. The first is limited by population and participation; the second can compound for decades. Two identical machines operated by better-trained workers using better processes produce different amounts, reflecting human capital, technology, and organization [1].

Formal expression

Let Y be real GDP, H total hours worked, and N population. Then:

labor productivity = Y ÷ H;

GDP per capita = Y ÷ N = (Y ÷ H) × (H ÷ N).

Measure Y in real terms on a single price base: labor productivity is then in units of constant-price currency per hour and GDP per capita in constant-price currency per person, H is in hours and N in people. Growth rates are g_X = (X_t ÷ X_{t-1} - 1) × 100%. For small changes, g_GDP per capita ≈ g_Y - g_N and g_labor productivity ≈ g_Y - g_H. If a value compounds each year at a constant rate g, then ending value = starting value × (1 + g)^years [1]; given the starting value, the ending value, and the number of years, the annual compound rate is (ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ years) - 1. An aggregate production function can be written Y = A × F(K, H), where K is capital and A is TFP; a rise in A means more output from the same inputs [2].

When it applies

  1. Real, not nominal. Compare real GDP on a consistent base; nominal growth includes inflation. Published per-capita growth is usually an annual percentage change: the World Bank's "GDP per capita growth (annual %)" series uses constant-price U.S. dollars with base year 2015 [3], so cross-country or cross-period comparisons need a matching price base and currency.
  2. Hours versus workers. Productivity per hour and per worker differ. When hours fall, output per worker can fall while output per hour rises.
  3. Capital deepening has limits. Under standard production assumptions, the marginal product of capital declines as capital per worker rises; capital accumulation alone cannot sustain long-run growth.
  4. TFP is a residual. It captures technology, organization, and reallocation, but also measurement error and omitted inputs.
  5. Growth is not welfare. GDP excludes distribution, the environment, unpaid work, and leisure; higher GDP per capita does not mean every household is better off.

Worked example

Suppose an economy's real GDP grows 3.0% a year, total hours grow 1.0%, and population grows 0.5%. Using approximate decomposition:

  • Labor productivity growth ≈ 3.0%-1.0% = 2.0%;
  • Hours per capita growth ≈ 1.0%-0.5% = 0.5%;
  • GDP per capita growth ≈ 2.0% + 0.5% = 2.5%, or 3.0%-0.5% = 2.5%.

Common mistakes

Growth is the same as productivity growth. Growth can also come from more labor input; productivity is output per unit of input.

If GDP grows 3%, GDP per capita also grows 3%. Population growth dilutes per capita growth; here it is about 2.5%.

More capital always means faster growth. Capital deepening runs into the diminishing marginal product of capital; sustained growth also needs human capital, technology, and institutional improvement.

Higher productivity means workers work harder. Technology, organization, education, and economies of scale can raise productivity without longer hours.

Knowledge-point review

capital accumulation

Definition: Capital accumulation converts saving into productive assets such as machines, buildings, infrastructure and software, raising future output.

Mechanism: Additional capital faces diminishing returns; saving rates, investment efficiency, depreciation and finance determine the capital stock.

Example: For example, saving more and investing in ports and grids lowers transport and transaction costs, making private investment easier.

Boundary: Capital accumulation is not an unlimited growth engine; low-return projects, poor maintenance and capital flight reduce returns, and marginal gains fall without technical progress.

population

Definition: Population affects the economy through labour supply, demand scale, saving and innovation; size, age structure and quality all matter.

Mechanism: Growth expands labour and markets but raises dependency and infrastructure needs; fertility, longevity and migration shape the structure.

Example: For example, a young population supplies labour, but without education the demographic dividend can become job pressure.

Boundary: More people are not automatically better; resources, jobs, female participation and pension burdens determine whether a dividend materialises, and forecasts depend on migration and fertility.

technology

Definition: Technology is the knowledge, processes and organisation used to produce goods and services; technical progress raises total factor productivity.

Mechanism: Innovation improves efficiency through R&D, learning, diffusion and competition, while adoption depends on skills, capital, infrastructure and institutions.

Example: For example, automated scheduling cuts downtime and inventory, raising output from the same labour and equipment.

Boundary: New technology does not automatically become productivity; failed pilots, skill gaps, data quality and organisational resistance can produce a productivity paradox.

human capital

Definition: Human capital is the productive and problem-solving capacity formed by education, training, health and experience.

Mechanism: Education and skills raise marginal labour output, and health reduces absence and cognitive loss; returns depend on quality, matching and labour demand.

Example: For example, nurses trained in emergency care handle more patients per hour with fewer errors.

Boundary: Years of schooling are not ability; school quality, field mismatch, skill obsolescence and health inequality make returns highly uneven.

productivity

Definition: Productivity is output per unit of input, measured as labour, capital or total factor productivity.

Mechanism: It depends on technology, capital deepening, skills, management and resource allocation; misallocation across firms drags down the aggregate.

Example: For example, two workers assembling 20 devices instead of 10 doubles labour productivity, though costlier equipment may not raise capital returns.

Boundary: Productivity is not a moral grade; measurement depends on hours, quality, intangibles and sectors, and layoffs can lift output per worker while destroying capability.

growth accounting and endogenous growth

Definition: Growth accounting decomposes output growth into capital, labour and total factor productivity; endogenous growth brings the sources of technology into the model.

Mechanism: It weights input growth by factor shares, leaving TFP as the residual; endogenous models stress R&D, human capital, knowledge and increasing returns.

Example: For example, with capital growing 5 percent and labour 2 percent, weighted contributions may explain 3.5 points and the rest reflects efficiency.

Boundary: The residual is an unknown, not pure technology; measurement error, changing factor shares and institutions confound it, and endogenous models rest on testable assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Real GDP grows 4.0% and total hours grow 2.0%. What is approximate labor productivity growth?
  2. Calculation. Real GDP per capita, a teaching assumption stated in constant prices and in yuan, rises from 20,000 to 24,000 over 10 years. Find the annual compound growth rate and the approximate doubling time using the rule of 70 (doubling years ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate in percent).
  3. Conditions. Why is capital accumulation alone unlikely to sustain rapid growth in output per person?
Suggested answers
  1. About 4.0%-2.0% = 2.0%. The exact value is 1.04 ÷ 1.02 - 1 ≈ 1.96%.
  2. (24,000 ÷ 20,000)^(1/10) - 1 ≈ 1.84%; doubling takes about 70 ÷ 1.84 ≈ 38 years.
  3. Capital per worker faces diminishing marginal product; sustained growth requires technology, human capital, and institutional improvement, and must cover population growth and depreciation.
