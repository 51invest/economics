Housing Affordability: Land, Commuting and Zoning

Housing affordability is not one price-to-income ratio; it is the combined result of land supply, zoning density, commuting cost and local public finance. This article links developable land, floor-area ratio, commuting radius and the affordable range, explaining why the same income buys very different housing in different parts of a city.

Definition and intuition

Housing affordability is often measured by the price-to-income or rent-to-income ratio, but these describe outcomes, not causes. The same 8x ratio can come from land scarcity, zoning limits, commuting costs, construction costs or low interest rates; the policy response differs.

Decompose affordability into four accounts: land (how much is developable, and are property rights clear), density (what floor-area ratio and height limits allow), commuting (can lower price be traded for travel), and public finance (how infrastructure and services are funded). Together they determine what an income group can buy or rent, where, and how large[1][2].

Supply chain

A new district's housing ceiling is Q = L × coverage × FAR / unit_size. L is developable land, coverage the building footprint share, FAR the floor-area ratio and unit_size average unit size.

With 100 hectares, 60% coverage, FAR 2.0 and 90 square metres per unit: Q = 100×10,000×0.6×2.0/90 ≈ 13,333 units. If zoning cuts FAR to 1.0, supply falls to 6,667; if unit size falls to 70 square metres, supply rises to 17,143. Supply elasticity is not abstract; these three parameters set it.

On the demand side, income, interest rates and commuting cost matter. A household can spend about 30–40% of income on housing; if commuting costs 800 yuan and one hour more per month, willingness to pay for proximity rises. When land supply is short, the premium becomes price; when transport improves, the premium is offset by commuting cost.

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a city adds 5,000 households, but the new district supplies only 3,600 units (100 hectares, 60% coverage, FAR 1.2, 100 square metres per unit). The gap is 1,400 units.

If the FAR rises from 1.2 to 1.8, supply reaches 5,400 and the gap closes; but infrastructure, school places and roads must expand, or new residents convert the cost into congestion. If demand-side subsidy gives each household 200,000 yuan, total subsidy is 1 billion yuan; it raises short-run purchasing power but not supply, so prices may rise, and the benefit depends on who gets the voucher first.

If metro travel time falls from 60 to 30 minutes, the effective price of peripheral housing falls and some demand moves outward; but the metro cost must be covered by public finance or land-value capture. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Public finance and distribution

Housing supply is also a public-finance problem. A local government reliant on land-sale revenue treats land as an asset: restricting supply supports land prices, while abundant supply lowers prices and current revenue. That incentive conflicts with affordability.

Alternative revenues include property taxes, infrastructure charges, land-value capture and intergovernmental transfers. A property tax ties local revenue to the stock of housing and rewards better services; infrastructure charges make new residents pay for capacity; land-value capture recovers part of the gain created by public investment.

Distributionally, new supply lowers rents and prices for tenants and first-time buyers, while owners of multiple units and land-revenue departments lose. Policy needs transition arrangements for losers rather than assuming everyone gains from more building.

A testable implication: if a city adds land but prices do not fall, supply may be concentrated at the high end, transport may be inadequate, or credit demand may be expanding at the same time[3][4].

Conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: land is not uniform. Developable land is limited by terrain, flood risk, contamination and title disputes; empty space on a map may not be buildable.

Boundary 2: density has external costs. Higher FAR loads roads, schools, drainage and public space; density without infrastructure can lower quality of life.

Boundary 3: commuting has a time budget. Households accept only so much travel; transport improvements shift the urban edge but can also induce longer commutes.

Boundary 4: public finance shapes supply. Local governments reliant on land-sale revenue may prefer high-priced land over abundant housing; property taxes, infrastructure charges or land-value capture change the incentive.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: high prices mean land shortage. Land may be sufficient, but zoning, FAR, title and infrastructure constrain supply; credit or investment demand can also raise prices.

Mistake 2: denser always means more affordable. Density adds supply, but if infrastructure and commuting costs rise, effective affordability may not improve.

Mistake 3: subsidies always help low-income households. Demand subsidies can raise prices when supply is short; design should be tied to new supply or rental options.

Deeper note: supply elasticity, commuting and the incidence of zoning

Housing affordability is not a single price problem. It is the ratio of house prices or rents to income, shaped by income, supply, interest rates, commuting costs and population inflows. A price-to-income ratio alone misses commuting time, housing quality and public services.

Supply elasticity decides whether new demand becomes price or building. Cities with ample land, fast permits and low construction costs have high elasticity, so inflows become new homes; cities with constrained land, strict zoning and slow permits have low elasticity, so demand bids prices up.

Who bears zoning? Limiting floor-area ratios and minimum lot sizes reduces supply and raises prices; existing owners gain, tenants and first-time buyers lose. If the rules also protect environment and infrastructure, those benefits count too. Neither 'regulation is harmful' nor 'regulation is omnipotent' is right.

Commuting cost is a hidden part of housing cost. Far suburbs are cheaper but cost time and fares; homes near job centres are expensive but save time. Affordability should be measured on housing plus commuting, not price alone.

Rent control protects tenants short term but can reduce supply, maintenance and mobility long term; effects are more complex in low-elasticity cities. A more effective mix is supply expansion, transport infrastructure and targeted subsidies, not price controls alone.

Measurement: price-to-income, rent-to-income, commuting time, vacancy rate, housing starts and permit duration. Read all five to distinguish supply shortage, income shortage and spatial mismatch.

Common misuses: attributing high prices only to speculation; treating supply expansion as a panacea; ignoring commuting and services. Housing policy must address supply, demand and spatial structure together.

Policy mix and measurement

No single tool solves affordability. Supply expansion needs land, permits and construction capacity together; releasing land without permits, or approving projects without transport, does not produce homes. Targeted subsidies help low-income households but can raise rents if supply does not grow. Transport investment expands the commutable area and effectively adds land.

Measure total cost: housing outlay plus the value of commuting time, fares and maintenance. A home 20% cheaper but one hour farther and 10% costlier to commute may be more expensive overall. Evaluate the number of affordable units and jobs reachable, not the average price.

International experience: Tokyo, Singapore and Vienna used rail, public housing and social housing respectively; the common thread is supply plus transport, not price controls alone.

What would change the conclusion

Housing-affordability diagnoses depend on supply and demand structure:

  1. Land constraints are topographic, not zoning. If mountains, water or ecological red lines limit developable land, zoning reform adds little supply.
  2. Commuting subsidies substitute for building. If transport investment expands accessible land, affordability can improve without more local supply.
  3. Construction productivity stalls. If labour, material and permitting costs rise, more land does not become affordable housing.
  4. Demand is investor- or migrant-driven. If local incomes are not the main demand source, the price-to-income ratio understates true purchasing power.
  5. Rent control distorts supply. If controls reduce new-build and maintenance incentives, short-run affordability may buy long-run shortage.

Affordability is not one ratio but the sum of land, density, commuting, fiscal and demand-source accounts.

What would change the conclusion (continued)

One more: vacant or mismatched stock. If vacant homes, second homes and planning mismatches are a large share of stock, new construction has less short-run effect on affordability.

Practical implication: affordability policy must address land, density, commuting and fiscal accounts together; relaxing one link leaves supply response stuck on the others.

Data note: use disposable income and after-tax monthly payments, not gross price and pre-tax income, or affordability is overstated.

Scope note: read tenure, lease terms and location together; the same price-to-income ratio means different burdens under different tenure and commuting conditions.

Reading supplement

Housing affordability must include commuting cost. A cheap home in the core is not affordable if commuting time and transport spending are high; zoning limits reduce supply but may also protect public services. Compare land supply, transport investment and zoning rules on the total cost of housing plus commuting.
