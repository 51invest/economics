Elasticity of Demand

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price, and so how revenue moves when a price changes.

Definition

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, as defined in OpenStax's Principles of Economics 3e [1].

Intuition

A 10% rise in the price of insulin barely changes prescriptions. A 10% rise for one coffee brand sends customers elsewhere. The same increase produces very different changes in quantity, and so in revenue: one seller collects more, the other less. Elasticity puts a scale on responsiveness. (Both cases are scenarios this article constructs; the numbers for insulin and branded coffee are not taken from any source registered here - USDA ERS reports estimated agricultural demand elasticities, not these two examples.)

Formal expression

E_d = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Starting-point measures depend on direction, so the midpoint method averages the two points in each denominator [1]:

E_d = [ΔQ ÷ ((Q₁ + Q₂) ÷ 2)] ÷ [ΔP ÷ ((P₁ + P₂) ÷ 2)]
Absolute value Name Meaning
Above 1 elastic Responds more than proportionally
Equal to 1 unitary Both changes equal
Below 1 inelastic Responds less than proportionally

Demand slopes down, so E_d is usually negative and absolute values are compared. Total revenue is TR = P × Q: with elastic demand a price rise cuts quantity proportionally more, so revenue falls; with inelastic demand revenue rises [2].

When it applies

  1. Along a given demand curve. Income, substitute prices or preferences shift the curve, not move along it.
  2. Over a stated horizon. Demand is usually more elastic in the long run, when habits and substitutes can adjust. This is the article's own general framing: the two OpenStax pages fetched this round contain no readable sentence on the horizon in their static HTML (the phrase "time horizon" appears 0 times in the stripped text), so no citation marker is attached.
  3. What makes demand elastic. Close substitutes, luxury status, a large budget share and many uses raise elasticity. This four-part rule of thumb is the article's own summary; none of it is covered by this article's registered supports, so it carries no citation marker.
  4. Elasticity is not slope. On one straight line the high-price range is elastic and the low-price range inelastic; only constant-elasticity demand is the same everywhere.
  5. Estimates have boundaries. The USDA Economic Research Service database collects US academic and government estimates and states that it is no longer updated [3]; one figure cannot be carried to another market or period.

Worked example

A café sells 100 cups a day at ¥10 (Chinese yuan) and 80 cups at ¥12 (teaching assumptions).

Item Original New Midpoint
Price (¥) 10 12 11
Quantity (cups/day) 100 80 90

Quantity changes by 20 ÷ 90 = 22.22% and price by 2 ÷ 11 = 18.18%, so E_d = -22.22% ÷ 18.18% = -1.22. The absolute value, 1.22, is above 1, so demand is elastic here.

Revenue moves from 10 × 100 = ¥1,000 to 12 × 80 = ¥960, a fall of ¥40 (-4%). A 20% price rise measured from the starting point, with falling revenue, matches the elastic case. Another price range gives a different elasticity.

Reading and review

Demand elasticity measures the relative response of quantity to price; the “relative” matters more than the absolute. The midpoint formula avoids getting different elasticities for a price rise and fall. Above 1, a price cut raises total revenue; below 1, it lowers revenue; at 1, revenue is unchanged. Substitutes, budget share, time horizon and necessity shift elasticity, and the same good can have different elasticity in different price ranges. Policy evaluation must not assume a constant elasticity or tax incidence and subsidy effects will be misstated. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Common mistakes

Elasticity equals slope. Slope carries units, and elasticity differs along one straight line; elasticity compares percentages.

More elastic is better. Elasticity only describes responsiveness: elastic demand makes a price rise cut revenue, inelastic demand the opposite; neither is good or bad.

A price rise always raises revenue. Only with inelastic demand; with elastic demand a price rise reduces total revenue.

Elasticity is a fixed constant. Except for constant-elasticity demand it changes with price and range, so estimates travel badly across markets.

Reading checklist

A final caution is that elasticity changes with the time horizon. Short-run demand is often inelastic because habits and contracts adjust slowly; long-run demand is more elastic as substitutes and capital adapt. Policy evaluated on a short window can therefore miss the larger long-run response.

Further check

Before applying “Elasticity of Demand”, test the weakest link between “price elasticity of demand” and “midpoint method”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Elasticity of Demand"

The subject of this article is "Elasticity of Demand". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "price elasticity of demand". Column two states the transmission channel of "midpoint method". Column three states the institutional boundary of "total revenue". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "midpoint method" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. total revenue is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: Do cross- and income elasticities change the pricing conclusion?

A café raises its own price from 10 to 12 yuan and sales fall from 100 to 80 cups. Using the midpoint method, own-price elasticity is about -1.22, and revenue drops from 1,000 to 960 yuan. Add a competitor: when the competitor cuts price by 5%, the café loses another 4 cups. With the same midpoint convention, cross-price elasticity is about +0.44 (teaching assumption: the café's demand falls when the substitute's price falls). If loyal members leave only one cup behind, cross elasticity falls to about 0.11; own elasticity may still exceed one, but the revenue loss from the increase is smaller.

The conclusion reverses when consumers have no substitute and the horizon is short: own elasticity may be below one, so a price increase raises revenue. The boundary is that own, cross and income elasticities are different tables. Using own elasticity to explain member churn, or cross elasticity to replace a pricing test, confuses price sensitivity with competitive diversion.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which demand is most likely inelastic? A. One brand of bottled water; B. A city bus fare; C. A holiday cruise; D. A new phone.
  2. Calculation. Price falls from ¥20 to ¥18 and sales rise from 50 to 60. Find the midpoint elasticity and the change in revenue.
  3. Conditions. Why can the elasticity on one demand curve not predict sales after consumer income rises?
Suggested answers
  1. B. Commuters have few short-run alternatives, so a fare rise affects ridership little; the other three have more substitutes.
  2. Quantity changes by 10 ÷ 55 = 18.18% and price by -2 ÷ 19 = -10.53%, so E_d = -1.73, elastic. Revenue moves from 20 × 50 = ¥1,000 to 18 × 60 = ¥1,080, up ¥80 (+8%).
  3. Higher income shifts the curve rather than moving along it. Elasticity describes the price-quantity relationship; an income change needs an income elasticity or a new estimate.
