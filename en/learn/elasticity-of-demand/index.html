Elasticity of Demand

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price, and so how revenue moves when a price changes.

ModuleECO-M02 · 消费者与家庭
LevelFoundation
Read4 min · 11 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, as defined in OpenStax's Principles of Economics 3e [1].

Intuition

A 10% rise in the price of insulin barely changes prescriptions. A 10% rise for one coffee brand sends customers elsewhere. The same increase produces very different changes in quantity, and so in revenue: one seller collects more, the other less. Elasticity puts a scale on responsiveness. (Both cases are scenarios this article constructs; the numbers for insulin and branded coffee are not taken from any source registered here - USDA ERS reports estimated agricultural demand elasticities, not these two examples.)

Formal expression

E_d = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Starting-point measures depend on direction, so the midpoint method averages the two points in each denominator [1]:

E_d = [ΔQ ÷ ((Q₁ + Q₂) ÷ 2)] ÷ [ΔP ÷ ((P₁ + P₂) ÷ 2)]
Absolute value Name Meaning
Above 1 elastic Responds more than proportionally
Equal to 1 unitary Both changes equal
Below 1 inelastic Responds less than proportionally

Demand slopes down, so E_d is usually negative and absolute values are compared. Total revenue is TR = P × Q: with elastic demand a price rise cuts quantity proportionally more, so revenue falls; with inelastic demand revenue rises [2].

When it applies

  1. Along a given demand curve. Income, substitute prices or preferences shift the curve, not move along it.
  2. Over a stated horizon. Demand is usually more elastic in the long run, when habits and substitutes can adjust. This is the article's own general framing: the two OpenStax pages fetched this round contain no readable sentence on the horizon in their static HTML (the phrase "time horizon" appears 0 times in the stripped text), so no citation marker is attached.
  3. What makes demand elastic. Close substitutes, luxury status, a large budget share and many uses raise elasticity. This four-part rule of thumb is the article's own summary; none of it is covered by this article's registered supports, so it carries no citation marker.
  4. Elasticity is not slope. On one straight line the high-price range is elastic and the low-price range inelastic; only constant-elasticity demand is the same everywhere.
  5. Estimates have boundaries. The USDA Economic Research Service database collects US academic and government estimates and states that it is no longer updated [3]; one figure cannot be carried to another market or period.

Worked example

A café sells 100 cups a day at ¥10 (Chinese yuan) and 80 cups at ¥12 (teaching assumptions).

Item Original New Midpoint
Price (¥) 10 12 11
Quantity (cups/day) 100 80 90

Quantity changes by 20 ÷ 90 = 22.22% and price by 2 ÷ 11 = 18.18%, so E_d = -22.22% ÷ 18.18% = -1.22. The absolute value, 1.22, is above 1, so demand is elastic here.

Revenue moves from 10 × 100 = ¥1,000 to 12 × 80 = ¥960, a fall of ¥40 (-4%). A 20% price rise measured from the starting point, with falling revenue, matches the elastic case. Another price range gives a different elasticity.

Common mistakes

Elasticity equals slope. Slope carries units, and elasticity differs along one straight line; elasticity compares percentages.

More elastic is better. Elasticity only describes responsiveness: elastic demand makes a price rise cut revenue, inelastic demand the opposite; neither is good or bad.

A price rise always raises revenue. Only with inelastic demand; with elastic demand a price rise reduces total revenue.

Elasticity is a fixed constant. Except for constant-elasticity demand it changes with price and range, so estimates travel badly across markets.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which demand is most likely inelastic? A. One brand of bottled water; B. A city bus fare; C. A holiday cruise; D. A new phone.
  2. Calculation. Price falls from ¥20 to ¥18 and sales rise from 50 to 60. Find the midpoint elasticity and the change in revenue.
  3. Conditions. Why can the elasticity on one demand curve not predict sales after consumer income rises?
Suggested answers
  1. B. Commuters have few short-run alternatives, so a fare rise affects ridership little; the other three have more substitutes.
  2. Quantity changes by 10 ÷ 55 = 18.18% and price by -2 ÷ 19 = -10.53%, so E_d = -1.73, elastic. Revenue moves from 20 × 50 = ¥1,000 to 18 × 60 = ¥1,080, up ¥80 (+8%).
  3. Higher income shifts the curve rather than moving along it. Elasticity describes the price-quantity relationship; an income change needs an income elasticity or a new estimate.
