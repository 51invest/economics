The EU ETS in 2022: Cap, Carbon Price and Border Adjustment

The EU ETS cap was 1.528 billion allowances in 2022, about 43 million below the prior year; auction prices reached €97.51 in February and €57.91 in March. Put the cap, auctions and free allocation on one chain to see that the carbon price is not an administrative price but the price of allowance scarcity.

Background and timeline

The EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) began in 2005 and covers power, steel, cement, refining, paper and aviation. Phase four runs from 2021 to 2030, with the cap falling by a linear reduction factor of 2.2% a year; in 2021–2023 that factor removed about 43 million allowances annually[4]. In 2022, the cap for installations was 1,528,579,492 allowances and aviation 27,268,379.

The highest auction price was €97.51 on 8 February; the lowest was €57.91 on 7 March; the annual average was €80.18. The price nearly doubled and then fell, but stayed far above 2018 levels[4]. In 2023, the EU revised the ETS Directive, raising the 2030 target to a 62% cut versus 2005 and increasing the annual reduction factor to 4.3% from 2024 and 4.4% from 2028; the cap was reduced by 90 million allowances in 2024 and 27 million in 2026.

The 2024 cap fell to 1,386,051,745[4].

Information available and actors

The main actors are the European Commission (cap and rules), member states (auctions and allocation), covered firms (abate or buy allowances), investors (trade allowances) and importers covered by the border mechanism. An allowance is a property right to emit; a firm can abate, buy allowances or use free allocation. A firm’s decision is not “be green or not” but “is the marginal cost of abatement below the allowance price?” If retrofitting, fuel switching or shutting capacity costs less per tonne than the carbon price, the firm abates and sells surplus allowances; if more, it buys.

The carbon price thus becomes a signal of marginal abatement cost across the economy. The regulator’s decision is the speed of cap tightening. A tighter cap raises scarcity and price; too fast can cause a price spike and industrial relocation.

The linear reduction factor, one-off cuts and free allocation adjustments balance cap, industrial protection and fiscal revenue.

Outcome and the number chain

Key numbers: 2021 cap 1,571,583,007; 2022 1,528,579,492; 2023 1,485,575,977; 2024 1,386,051,745; annual cuts of about 43 million in 2021–2023; a 90 million one-off cut in 2024; 2022 prices €97.51 high, €57.91 low, €80.18 average[4]. Chain them: cap falls → scarcity rises → auction price rises → firms abate or buy → free allocation falls → border adjustment arrives. The 2024 cap falls from 1.486 billion to 1.386 billion, about 100 million, of which 90 million is one-off; policy is not a smooth line but “linear reduction plus one-off tightening”.

The €97.51 is an auction clearing price, not every transaction; secondary market, futures and auction prices differ. Treating one auction price as “the EU carbon price” ignores market structure and contract maturity.

Mechanism

The first layer is the cap. Total allowances set total emissions; a lower cap means greater scarcity. The second is auction and free allocation.

Auctions discover prices; free allocation protects trade-exposed sectors; more free allocation means firms buy less and the carbon price passes through less. The third is border adjustment. If the EU raises its carbon price alone, carbon-intensive industry may move to looser jurisdictions, creating “carbon leakage”; CBAM charges the carbon cost on imports to reduce that arbitrage[4].

Together, the carbon price is not “whatever the government sets” but the outcome of cap, allocation and import adjustment. If the cap tightens but free allocation stays, the price falls mainly on power; if free allocation falls, industry also feels the cost; if CBAM arrives, import competition changes.

Counterfactuals and alternative explanations

First, without the 90 million one-off cut in 2024, the cap would be about 6.5% higher, and both price and abatement pressure would be lower. Second, with a higher free-allocation share, the effective carbon cost for covered firms falls, pass-through weakens, but auction revenue is lower. Third, if a carbon-price ceiling had been set during the 2022 energy crisis, short-term prices would be lower, but scarcity would not change; a queue below the ceiling could create rationing or a black market.

Fourth, without CBAM, a unilateral EU carbon price would raise leakage risk, and abatement could become “moving emissions abroad”.

Limits to generalization

The case can show that a cap and linear reduction factor create predictable scarcity; auction prices signal that scarcity; free allocation and border adjustment change cost pass-through and trade conditions. It cannot show that the carbon price equals the social cost of all abatement; that an EU price alone determines global climate outcomes; that CBAM eliminates leakage entirely; or that the 2022 price swing is a long-run trend. Prices also reflect energy costs, the cycle, financial positions and policy expectations.

Only with all four types can “cap tightening” be separated from “price volatility”; missing one turns the carbon price into a purely administrative number[4]. Working paper: a four-column check for "The EU ETS in 2022: Cap, Carbon Price and Border Adjustment"

The subject of this article is "EU carbon market 2022: quota caps, carbon prices and border adjustments". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Cap and linear reduction". Column two states the transmission channel of "Auctions, free allocation and carbon price". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Carbon leakage and border adjustment". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Auctions, free allocation and carbon price" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Carbon leakage and border adjustment is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Decision review

From a firm’s view, the carbon price is not an “extra tax” but the relative price of “abate or buy allowances”. If a steel plant can abate at €60/t and the price is €80, it should invest; if abatement costs €100, it should buy and wait for technology costs to fall. The price sorts dispersed abatement options into a marginal-cost curve.

From a government’s view, auction revenue is not “free money” but the fiscal expression of scarcity. Revenue can support low-income households, fund abatement technology or cut other taxes; if it goes to the general budget, public acceptance falls. Free allocation is industrial protection but weakens pass-through.

From a trade view, CBAM extends the carbon price to imports. If an EU steel maker pays €80/t and an importer pays nothing, the EU producer is disadvantaged; CBAM charges the imported steel a corresponding carbon cost and reduces leakage. But CBAM covers only some products and some emissions; it cannot replace global carbon pricing.
