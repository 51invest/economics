Monetary Policy Transmission: Interest-Rate, Credit and Balance-Sheet Channels

Monetary policy does not attach the policy rate directly to every borrower. It travels through funding cost, credit conditions, asset prices, the exchange rate and expectations to spending and inflation. Transmission is lagged, leaky and weakened by fixed-rate debt, bank capital and supply shocks.

By the end of this article, you can decompose a rate hike from the policy rate to firm interest, household cash flow and aggregate demand, compare the lags and conditions of five channels, and explain why a 200 basis point hike is not an immediate 2% demand cut.

Definition

Monetary policy transmission is the process by which a central bank's policy rate or balance-sheet action changes financial conditions, credit, asset prices, the exchange rate and expectations, and then affects consumption, investment, output and inflation [1]. Common channels include:

  • Interest-rate channel: the policy rate changes market rates and borrowing costs, affecting durables, housing and business investment.
  • Credit channel: bank funding cost, risk appetite and lending standards change the credit available to firms, especially small and medium enterprises.
  • Balance-sheet channel: rates and asset prices change collateral value, net worth and cash flow, amplifying or dampening financing capacity.
  • Exchange-rate channel: interest differentials and capital flows change the currency, import prices and export demand.
  • Expectations and communication channel: households and firms change saving, pricing and investment plans when they revise expectations about future rates, inflation and the policy stance [2].

These channels are not separate switches. They are links in one chain. The policy rate is the starting point, not the destination. A central bank changes reserves and credit conditions through open market operations, reserve requirements and rate tools, but those operations reach borrowers only after passing through banks and financial markets [3].

Intuition

The central bank lifts the policy rate from 2% to 4%. One variable moves by 2 percentage points, but bank funding is not only the central bank: part is deposits, part is wholesale funding. Deposit rates may pass through only half; wholesale funding almost fully. If 60% is deposits and 40% is wholesale, bank funding cost rises by about 0.6 × 100 + 0.4 × 200 = 140 basis points.

The bank passes 140 basis points to loans, and a floating-rate business loan rises from 5% to 6.4%. A CNY 1 million loan costs CNY 14,000 more in interest per year. A CNY 2 million floating mortgage costs CNY 28,000 more. Cash flow tightens, firms delay equipment investment and households cut discretionary spending; demand falls only gradually.

The chain takes time. Contracts reprice, banks approve loans, households revise budgets and firms re-plan investment. BIS research stresses that borrowing rates rise quickly after tightening, while aggregate demand and financial stress take longer to materialise, with balance sheets and supply shocks shaping the effect [2].

Formal expression

Let the policy-rate change be Δi, the pass-through from policy rate to bank funding cost β_f, and the pass-through from funding cost to lending rate β_l. The borrower rate changes approximately as:

Δr_borrow ≈ β_l × β_f × Δi + Δspread

where Δspread is the change in risk and liquidity premia. If β_l = β_f = 1, a 200 basis point policy hike passes fully to borrowers. If banks absorb part and risk premia fall, actual pass-through may be only 100–140 basis points.

For a floating-rate loan of principal L, annual interest changes by:

Δinterest = L × Δr_borrow

Let β_y be the sensitivity of aggregate demand to the real rate. A rough output effect is:

ΔY ≈ −β_y × Δr_real

where Δr_real = Δr_borrow − Δπ_expected. If the nominal rate rises 140 basis points and expected inflation falls 50 basis points, the real rate rises 190 basis points; if β_y is 0.3% of output per 100 basis points, the rough effect is 0.57% of output. This is a teaching illustration, not a policy forecast.

Transmission also has a lag distribution. Let w(t) be the share of transmission realised in quarter t, with Σw(t) = 1. If only 30% arrives in the first two quarters, 60% by four quarters and 90% by eight, the same policy change produces different cash-flow pressure at different times.

When it applies

  1. Rates can adjust. A high share of long fixed-rate loans slows transmission to existing debt; a high share of floating-rate debt transmits faster but concentrates the shock.
  2. Banks are willing to lend. When bank capital is adequate and funding stable, the credit channel works. When capital is thin or deposits leave, banks cut lending volume and standards.
  3. Households and firms have buffers. Cash, savings and refinancing capacity absorb rate rises; highly levered, low-saving sectors are more sensitive to the same hike.
  4. Supply shocks do not dominate. If inflation comes mainly from energy, food or supply bottlenecks, raising rates may weaken demand without fixing the source and may hurt output first.
  5. Expectations are anchored. When households and firms believe inflation will return to target, transmission is smoother; if expectations de-anchor, the same rate change may need a larger move.
  6. Open-economy conditions are manageable. Exchange rates, capital flows and foreign rates can offset or amplify domestic policy; fixed-exchange-rate or high external-debt economies transmit differently.

Worked example

The policy rate rises from 2.0% to 4.0%, so Δi = 200 basis points. Bank funding is 60% deposits and 40% wholesale; deposit rates pass through 50%, wholesale funding 100%. The funding-cost change is:

Δfunding = 0.6 × 0.5 × 200 + 0.4 × 1.0 × 200 = 60 + 80 = 140 basis points.

The bank passes all 140 basis points to floating loan rates, so the loan rate rises from 5.0% to 6.4%. A CNY 1 million business loan rises from CNY 50,000 to CNY 64,000 in annual interest, CNY 14,000 more. A CNY 2 million mortgage rises from CNY 100,000 to CNY 128,000, CNY 28,000 more.

If only 30% of mortgages reprice within a year, the first-year cash-flow hit is 0.3 × 28,000 = CNY 8,400; the other 70% reprice over later years. If the bank also raises the risk premium by 20 basis points, the borrower rate becomes 6.6% and the business loan pays CNY 16,000 more. If an energy shock raises inflation at the same time, the real rate may fall and the demand compression is weakened.

These are teaching assumptions, not real bank or household data.

Common mistakes

“A 200 basis point policy hike raises every borrowing cost by 200 basis points.” Only with full pass-through and immediate repricing. Deposits, wholesale funding, risk premia and fixed-rate contracts all change the result.

“Rate hikes lower inflation immediately.” Transmission is lagged, and energy or supply shocks change the real rate. Policy rates affect financial conditions first, then demand and inflation, often over quarters or years.

“The credit channel is just whether banks feel like lending.” It also depends on bank capital, deposit stability, collateral value and borrower cash flow. A bank that does not lend may be capital-constrained, not unwilling.

“Currency appreciation always means tightening.” Appreciation lowers import prices but also weakens exports; the net effect depends on openness, export pricing and foreign-currency liabilities.

“Communication can replace the policy rate.” Communication can speed transmission, but it cannot permanently replace the real policy rate; when credibility falls, the expectations channel can amplify shocks.
