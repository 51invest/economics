Defaults, Nudges and Public Services: Effects and Limits of Behavioral Interventions

Defaults and reminders can raise participation in public services, but effects found in a trial are not the effects that survive scale-up. Put average treatment effect, unit cost, sample size, persistence and spillovers on one ledger to judge whether an intervention is worth scaling or only works in a small setting.

Definition

Choice architecture is how options are presented: defaults, ordering, wording, deadlines and administrative steps all shape choices even when the options themselves are unchanged. A default is the option that applies when a user does not actively choose; a nudge changes behavior at low cost without restricting choice[1][2]. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) assigns subjects randomly to treatment and control groups to estimate a causal effect; the average treatment effect (ATE) is the difference in mean outcomes between treated and control units; a conditional average treatment effect (CATE) splits that effect by group, place or context[1][3].

Evaluating a behavioral intervention is not just about "how much did participation rise". It also asks who the additional participants are, the cost per additional participant, whether the effect persists, whether it crowds out other behavior, and whether it replicates elsewhere.

Intuition

People do not calculate every decision. Faced with forms, queues and complex options, many follow the preset, postpone, or simply do nothing. A default changes the consequence of inaction from "not enrolled" to "enrolled", so participation can rise quickly. A reminder letter brings the task back into attention, so it can help with procrastination.

But defaults and reminders are not magic. A default may enroll people who are not well served by the program; a reminder may work mainly on those already inclined to act. Small trials often have more motivated staff, more homogeneous subjects and less contamination, so effects frequently shrink at scale. More importantly, participation is an intermediate outcome: public services ultimately care about actual treatment, error rates and cost, not form submissions.

Conditions

  1. Randomization must be credible. If subjects can select treatment, or staff know assignment and treat groups differently, estimates are biased[1].
  2. Outcome measures must be pre-specified. Choosing a favorable metric after the fact is not evidence; participation, completion, actual treatment and error rates can point in different directions[1][3].
  3. Scale changes implementation quality, subject composition and cost. A trial effect is not automatically the effect after rollout[2].
  4. Default effects may decay over time and may reverse if the default is removed; persistence needs a separate test[2][3].
  5. Interventions can spill over: a reminder may mobilize family members, or consume staff time. The unit of analysis must match the spillover range[3].
  6. Ethics and consent matter. Automatic enrollment, exploiting cognitive biases and targeting vulnerable groups require transparency and an exit option[1][2].

Example and calculation

For persistence, suppose the default raises participation from 40% to 80% in year one, a gain of 40 percentage points; three years later it is only 20 percentage points above control, so the effect has halved. For heterogeneity, suppose two equally sized groups show +8 percentage points and 0 points; the average effect is +4 points. Neither number means every group receives the average.

Common mistakes

"Stronger defaults are always better." Defaults can raise participation, but they can also enroll people poorly served by the program, raise exit costs and undermine autonomy. Legitimacy depends on transparency, an exit option and actual service quality[1][2].

"A significant RCT means it is ready to scale." Significance means the effect is unlikely to be zero in this sample and implementation setting. Subject composition, implementation quality and cost change at scale; external validity needs a separate test[2][3].

"Higher participation is success." Participation is an intermediate outcome. If participants do not receive treatment, error rates rise, or costs shift to frontline staff, higher enrollment may only move the workload from the form to the counter (author's assessment).

"The published average effect is the true effect." Journals are more likely to publish significant results; unpublished null trials bias the visible average upward. Assess publication bias and pre-registration[3].
