Utility and the Budget Constraint

Utility measures the satisfaction a consumer gets from a bundle, while the budget constraint marks what is affordable; the best bundle equalizes marginal utility per yuan.

ModuleECO-M02 · 消费者与家庭
LevelFoundation
Read4 min · 11 sections

Definition

Utility is the satisfaction a consumer derives from a bundle of goods. The budget constraint is the condition that spending does not exceed income at given prices; the budget line shows bundles that spend all income. Together they determine the best affordable bundle. OpenStax's Principles of Economics 3e defines the budget constraint as the combinations of two goods that are affordable [2] and states the utility-maximizing rule as equal marginal utility per dollar across goods [1].

Intuition

Suppose a consumer has 120 yuan a month for meals at 10 yuan and books at 20 yuan. One more book costs two meals [2]. A book costs twice as much as a meal, so even if the last book adds more satisfaction than the last meal, the book is not automatically the better buy: compare satisfaction per yuan. Spending shifts toward the good with higher marginal utility per yuan until the last yuan on each delivers the same satisfaction. No small reallocation can then raise total utility.

Formal expression

The budget constraint is 10X + 20Y ≤ 120; spending all income makes it hold with equality, giving the budget line 10X + 20Y = 120, where X is meals and Y is books. The intercepts are 12 and 6, and the slope is -10/20 = -0.5: one more book requires giving up two meals.

Assume the utility function U = √(X·Y) (a teaching assumption, measured in "utils"). Marginal utilities are MU_X = ΔU/ΔX = 0.5·√(Y/X), MU_Y = ΔU/ΔY = 0.5·√(X/Y). The optimum satisfies MU_X / P_X = MU_Y / P_Y, equivalently MU_X / MU_Y = P_X / P_Y. At X = 6 and Y = 3, MU_X ≈ 0.354 and MU_Y ≈ 0.707; utility per yuan is 0.0354 for both. Spending is 60 + 60 = 120 yuan and U = √18 ≈ 4.24.

When it applies

  1. Income and prices are given. The consumer neither borrows nor saves within the period, so the budget is a one-period spending limit.
  2. Goods are divisible. The equality holds for an interior solution with small adjustments. For whole books or minimum purchase quantities, compare total utility of feasible bundles instead.
  3. Preferences are stable and rankable. Utility is ordinal: only the ranking matters, and utility units are not comparable across people.
  4. No uncertainty or strategic interaction. Prices and quality are known, and one person's choice does not depend on others' actions.
  5. Corner solutions need a separate test. When only one good is consumed, the condition becomes an inequality; the equality should not be applied mechanically.

Scope note: “utility is ordinal” is a textbook modeling convention used here. OpenStax 6.1 states clearly that different people's utils cannot be compared and that one person's utils are subjective, but it does not use the term “ordinal utility.” This article presents the full sentence as a methodological statement, not a quotation from the registered sources.

Worked example

With the 120 yuan budget, 10 yuan meals, 20 yuan books, and U = √(X·Y):

Bundle Meals X Books Y Spending Utility
A 12 0 120 0
B 8 2 120 4.00
C 6 3 120 4.24
D 4 4 120 4.00
E 0 6 120 0

Bundle C has the highest utility. Moving from B to C gives up two meals and adds one book; spending stays at 120 yuan and utility rises from 4.00 to 4.24. At C the last yuan spent on each good yields the same marginal utility, so further swapping lowers utility. All figures are teaching assumptions, not a real household.

Common mistakes

Utility means usefulness or moral worth. Utility only ranks a consumer's own preferences; a "useful" good may be disliked.

The budget constraint requires spending all income. The constraint is "no more than"; saving is also a use of income.

Diminishing marginal utility means total utility falls. Total utility keeps rising while marginal utility is positive, only more slowly.

The equal-marginal rule compares average utility. It compares the marginal utility of the last unit per yuan, not an average.

Utility can be compared across people. Consumer theory needs only a personal ranking; utility numbers do not show who is happier.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is the budget line (the budget constraint holding with equality)? A. 10X + 20Y = 120; B. U = √(X·Y); C. MU_X/P_X = MU_Y/P_Y; D. P = MC.
  2. Calculation. Income rises to 180 yuan while prices and the utility function stay the same. What are the optimal bundle and utility?
  3. Conditions. Why can the equal-marginal rule not be used directly when books can only be bought whole?
Suggested answers
  1. A. B is the utility function, C is the optimum condition, and D is a competitive firm's pricing rule.
  2. The optimum requires Y = 0.5X; the budget 10X + 20Y = 180 becomes 20X = 180, so X = 9, Y = 4.5, and U = √40.5 ≈ 6.36.
  3. The equality assumes small adjustments. Whole books change quantity in lumps, so compare the total utility of adjacent feasible bundles or use a discrete marginal-utility table.
