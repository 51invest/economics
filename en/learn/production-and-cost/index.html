Production and Cost

A production function turns inputs into output, and costs depend on output and input prices. This article covers short-run cost and output decisions only. Marginal cost decides whether one more unit is worth making; average variable cost decides whether to shut down in the short run, and average total cost whether the price covers all costs.

By the end of this article, you can use a cost table to choose an output level, decide when to shut down, and explain why the cost of the next unit is not average cost.

Definition

A production function describes how inputs become output under a given technology. Cost is the economic value of the resources used in production. In the short run at least one input is fixed; in the long run all inputs can change [2]. Fixed costs do not vary with output, variable costs do, and marginal cost is the change in total cost from one more unit. Average total cost is total cost divided by output [3].

Intuition

A workshop pays 60 yuan a day in rent and 30 yuan per worker; rent is fixed for the day. The first worker adds 10 units, the second 8, the third 6: equipment and space are fixed, so later workers have less to work with. That is diminishing marginal product [2]. Marginal cost starts low and average cost falls; then both turn upward as bottlenecks appear.

Formal expression

The short-run production function is Q = f(L, K̄), with capital K fixed and labor L variable; marginal product is MP_L = ΔQ/ΔL.

The cost relationships are TC = FC + VC, ATC = TC/Q, AVC = VC/Q, MC = ΔTC/ΔQ [3]. Economic cost also includes the implicit cost of owned funds, premises and the owner's time. Accounting profit subtracts only explicit payments, while economic profit subtracts both explicit and implicit costs [1]. For a price-taking firm the profit-maximizing output satisfies P = MC, and it stays in operation only while price exceeds the minimum average variable cost[4].

When it applies

  1. Technology is given. There is no significant innovation, learning effect or change in product quality during the period.
  2. At least one input is fixed in the short run. Buildings, equipment or land cannot be adjusted immediately; in the long run all inputs can be re-chosen.
  3. Inputs are divisible and homogeneous. Labor hours and materials can be added continuously; with indivisible inputs, compare discrete rows instead.
  4. Costs use opportunity cost. Owned resources should be valued at their best alternative use, or economic profit will be overstated.
  5. P = MC applies only to price takers. The textbook derives it for a firm that takes the market price and can sell any quantity at it[4]; a monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve and chooses output where MR = MC[5].

Common mistakes

Fixed cost affects the decision to make one more unit. Fixed cost does not vary with output and is not part of marginal cost; it matters for whether to renew the lease or exit.

Average cost equals marginal cost. They meet only near the minimum of average cost; pricing on average cost ignores the true increment of the next unit.

Diminishing marginal product means output falls. Total output keeps rising while marginal product is positive, only more slowly.

Accounting profit equals economic profit. Accounting profit omits implicit costs and can overstate the true return.

Knowledge-point review

production function

Definition: A production function maps input combinations to the maximum output attainable with a given technology, usually labour, capital and materials.

Mechanism: Diminishing marginal product means extra output from one more input falls as other inputs stay fixed; technical progress shifts the whole function upward.

Example: For example, the first bags of fertiliser raise a field's yield sharply, later bags add less and too much can reduce output.

Boundary: The function simplifies technology and omits organisation, incentives and quality; short-run fixed inputs must be separated from long-run adjustable ones.

cost

Definition: Cost is the value of resources forgone to produce output, including explicit payments and implicit opportunity costs.

Mechanism: Total cost splits into fixed and variable components; average and marginal cost shape short-run supply and shutdown, and profit peaks where marginal cost equals marginal revenue.

Example: For example, a bakery's rent is fixed while flour and labour hours are variable; the extra cost of another batch is mainly ingredients and overtime.

Boundary: Accounting and economic cost differ; sunk costs should not drive marginal decisions, and joint costs need a defensible allocation rule.

economies of scale

Definition: Economies of scale mean unit costs fall as output expands, driven by specialisation, dedicated equipment, bulk purchasing and learning.

Mechanism: They slope long-run average cost downward; diseconomies arise from managerial complexity, coordination and bottlenecks, so the curve may fall, flatten and then rise.

Example: For example, an auto plant making 100,000 cars spreads tooling and R&D better than one making 10,000, but at one million management and logistics may become harder.

Boundary: Scale is not automatically good; economies of scope, network effects and minimum efficient scale differ, and market size and transport costs limit realisation.

profit

Definition: Economic profit is total revenue minus all opportunity costs, while accounting profit deducts only explicit costs; normal profit rewards entrepreneurship.

Mechanism: Competition tends to drive economic profit toward zero, but entry barriers, innovation rents and risk premia can sustain it; profit signals resource entry and exit.

Example: For example, a restaurant reports 200,000 yuan accounting profit but forgoes 250,000 in owner wages and shop rent, so economic profit is negative.

Boundary: Profit depends on accounting rules, horizon, risk and monopoly rents; a short boom does not prove replicability, and losses may reflect investment or sunk costs.

entry and exit

Definition: Entry brings new producers into a market and exit removes existing ones, changing competition and prices.

Mechanism: Positive economic profit attracts entry and expands supply, while losses trigger exit and contraction; entry barriers determine how fast adjustment occurs.

Example: For example, ride-hailing subsidies attracted many drivers, then excess supply and lower subsidies pushed some to leave.

Boundary: Entry and exit face licences, capital, sunk costs and exit barriers; price signals lag, and entrants differ in quality and scale.

boundaries of the firm

Definition: Firm boundaries decide which activities are organised internally and which are bought in markets, balancing coordination costs against transaction costs.

Mechanism: Specific assets, frequent transactions and uncertainty raise market costs and encourage vertical integration, while larger firms face managerial and incentive costs.

Example: For example, an automaker sources seats from several suppliers but keeps critical engines in-house to protect technology and quality.

Boundary: Boundaries are not fixed; outsourcing, platforms and long-term contracts sit between markets and hierarchies, and asset specificity and incomplete contracts shift the choice.

Data and institutional check: production function

Before using "production function" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns marginal product.

Third, the institutional boundary. fixed and variable costs is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: production function

Learning "production function" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "marginal product": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "fixed and variable costs" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of production function still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind marginal product reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "production function" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "marginal product" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Review questions: production function

  2. Give an example where a change in "fixed and variable costs" reverses the conclusion, and name the rule that changed.
  3. Find a time series and show how seasonal adjustment, base period or revision changes its direction.
  4. Design a transfer test: from firm to household, or bank to fund; which parameters must be re-estimated?

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is a fixed cost in the short run? A. Workshop rent; B. Hourly wages; C. Flour; D. Electricity billed by usage.
  2. Calculation. At a price of 8 yuan per unit, what are the optimal output, revenue, total cost and profit?
  3. Conditions. Why does P = MC not apply to a monopolist?
Suggested answers
  1. A. Rent does not vary with output in the short run; wages and flour do. Electricity counts as variable here because the question bills it by usage — if a supply contract carries a fixed capacity charge unrelated to units used, that part belongs to fixed cost, so the classification follows the contract rather than the label.
  2. Output is 28 units: the next 4 units cost 7.50 yuan each, below 8, while the following 2 units cost 15 yuan each, above 8. Revenue is 8 × 28 = 224 yuan, total cost 180 yuan and profit 44 yuan.
  3. A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so selling one more unit requires a lower price and marginal revenue is below price. Its optimum is MR = MC, not P = MC.
  1. Concept check. Explain "production function" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  2. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "production function" to "marginal product" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.
