Economies of Scale, Economies of Scope and Capacity Utilization

Economies of scale come from spreading fixed costs over more output and from specialization; economies of scope come from sharing capabilities across products, and capacity utilization measures how far actual output is from sustainable maximum output. All three can lower cost, but all have limits: coordination cost, weak demand and quality loss can make “bigger” more expensive.

Definition

Economies of scale means unit cost falls as output rises; diseconomies of scale means unit cost rises as output expands further [1]. Economies of scope means producing several products or services together is cheaper than producing them separately. Capacity utilization is actual output divided by sustainable maximum output; the Federal Reserve defines it as an output index divided by a capacity index [2].

The three ideas are often confused. Scale is about how much of one product a plant makes; scope is about making several products together; utilization is about how much of existing capacity is used.

Intuition

A factory pays 120,000 yuan a month in fixed cost whether it runs or not, and each extra unit costs 6 yuan in variable cost. At 6,000 units, fixed cost is 20 yuan per unit and total unit cost is 26 yuan. At 10,000 units, fixed cost is 12 yuan per unit and total unit cost is 18 yuan. Spreading fixed cost over more output is the most visible source of scale economies.

But bigger is not always cheaper. Beyond sustainable capacity, the plant needs overtime, outsourcing and expedited shipping, which raise variable cost. More management layers add coordination and communication cost. Unit cost can then turn upward.

Formal expression

Let fixed cost be FC, output Q, variable cost per unit v, and average total cost:

AC(Q) = FC/Q + v

The relationship among fixed cost, variable cost and average total cost follows the short-run cost definitions [3]. In the simple case where only fixed cost is spread, AC falls with Q. More generally, scale elasticity measures how cost responds to output:

E_scale = (ΔAC/AC) / (ΔQ/Q)

E_scale < 0 indicates economies of scale, a value near 0 indicates constant returns to scale, and a positive value indicates diseconomies of scale.

Economies of scope are defined by a cost comparison:

S = [C(Q1,0) + C(0,Q2) − C(Q1,Q2)] / [C(Q1,0) + C(0,Q2)]

S > 0 means joint production is cheaper; S < 0 means diseconomies of scope. Capacity utilization is:

u = Q / Q_cap

where Q_cap is sustainable maximum output, not a theoretical peak. The Federal Reserve treats capacity as the greatest output a plant can maintain under a realistic work schedule, after normal downtime and with sufficient inputs available [2]. It is not the limit of running machinery until it overheats.

When it applies

  1. Use long-run average cost. Economies of scale require all inputs to be adjustable. With a fixed plant, only short-run cost curves apply.
  2. Fixed cost must truly not vary with output. If expansion requires a new plant, more maintenance or overtime, that spending is a new fixed or variable cost, not something to spread.
  3. Variable cost must not hide quality losses. Rework, defects and customer attrition during rush production belong in the cost calculation.
  4. Demand must absorb the extra output. Lower unit cost is not higher profit if inventory sits unsold and ties up capital.
  5. Measure capacity against sustainable maximum output. Using a theoretical peak as the denominator understates utilization and overstates room to expand [2].

Worked example

A plant has monthly capacity of 10,000 units, fixed cost of 120,000 yuan and variable cost of 6 yuan per unit. Unit costs at different utilization rates are shown below (teaching assumptions, yuan per unit):

Utilization Output (units/month) Fixed cost (yuan) Variable cost (yuan) Total cost (yuan) Unit cost (yuan/unit)
60% 6,000 120,000 36,000 156,000 26.00
80% 8,000 120,000 48,000 168,000 21.00
100% 10,000 120,000 60,000 180,000 18.00

Moving from 60% to 80% cuts unit cost by 5 yuan; moving from 80% to 100% cuts it by another 3 yuan. The spreading effect is strongest at low utilization and weaker near full capacity.

Now consider scope. Two product lines each build a warehouse with fixed cost of 80,000 yuan, for 160,000 yuan in total. A shared warehouse costs 130,000 yuan, saving 30,000 yuan; economies of scope are 30,000/160,000 = 18.75%. If sharing adds 40,000 yuan in coordination, scheduling and cross-product management, net cost becomes 170,000 yuan, 10,000 yuan more than separate operation. That is diseconomies of scope.

All figures are teaching assumptions, not real firm data.

Common mistakes

“More output always means lower unit cost.” That is true only inside the economies-of-scale range. Beyond sustainable capacity, overtime, outsourcing, defects and management layers push cost up.

“Higher capacity utilization is always better.” Near 100%, there is no room for maintenance and delivery flexibility; running above sustainable maximum output also sacrifices quality and safety [2].

“Economies of scope are vertical integration.” Scope is about whether sharing capabilities across products saves money. Vertical integration is about internalizing upstream or downstream stages. They can overlap, but they are not the same test.

“Fixed cost can always be spread.” If expansion needs new capacity, more maintenance or a different organization, the new spending is a new fixed cost, not something to divide by the old denominator.

Deeper note: utilisation, operating leverage and the full-capacity trap

High capacity utilisation is not automatically good; three cases matter. First, real demand at prices above marginal cost: full utilisation spreads fixed cost and builds delivery credibility, and expansion deserves review. Second, demand supported by discounts or credit: full utilisation spreads fixed cost over loss-making orders and may worsen cash flow. Third, no maintenance window: 100% utilisation leaves no time for repairs or product changeovers; breakdowns and defects rise and long-run cost increases.

Operating leverage links utilisation to profit volatility. The higher the fixed-cost share, the faster unit cost rises and profit falls when utilisation drops 10%. A plant with 10,000 units of capacity and 120,000 yuan of fixed cost sees unit cost rise from 18 to 21 when utilisation falls from 100% to 80%; at a price of 24, unit profit falls from 6 to 3, halving profit. With only 60,000 of fixed cost, unit cost rises from 12 to 15 and unit profit falls from 12 to 9, a much smaller drop.

Scale and scope can offset each other. A shared warehouse cuts fixed cost but cross-product scheduling, quality control and coordination raise variable cost; a shared sales team broadens coverage but may dilute expertise. Compare total cost after combination with total cost of separate operation, not one line-item saving.

Demand is the boundary of scale economies. Lower unit cost only means supply is cheaper; if the market absorbs only 8,000 units, expanding to 10,000 builds inventory and price pressure. Scale economies require demand to absorb output, or the cost is shifted into inventory and receivables.

Policy implication: capacity utilisation is a cyclical indicator, not a target that is better when higher; maintenance headroom, changeover capability and supply-chain buffers are part of sustainable capacity[2][3].

Exercises

  1. Fixed cost is 90,000 yuan, variable cost is 8 yuan per unit and monthly capacity is 6,000 units. Calculate unit cost at 50%, 75% and 100% utilization.
  2. Two product lines each have fixed cost of 50,000 yuan; sharing costs 80,000 yuan and adds 15,000 yuan in coordination. Calculate scope measure S and decide whether to share.
  3. A plant raises utilization from 85% to 95%. Unit cost falls only 0.8 yuan, while the defect rate rises from 1% to 3%. Explain why unit cost may understate the true cost.
  4. Fixed cost 120,000 yuan, variable cost 6 yuan, price 24 yuan: how does unit profit change when utilization falls from 100% to 80%?
  5. Why can 100% capacity utilization be a full-capacity trap? Give three conditions.
