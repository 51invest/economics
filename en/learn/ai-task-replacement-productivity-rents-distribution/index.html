Artificial Intelligence and Task Replacement: Productivity, Rents and Distribution

AI changes tasks before it changes jobs. Separating codifiable, judgement and relational tasks shows where productivity, labour share and rent distribution actually move.

Question and scope

The usual unit in the AI-and-jobs debate is the occupation: will accountants be replaced, will programmers lose their jobs, how many customer-service staff remain? That unit is too coarse. Technology usually enters a task before it replaces an occupation. An accounting job contains invoice entry, account matching, exception explanation, client communication and sign-off responsibility. A generative model may change the first two, draft the third and still leave the last two with a person who carries judgement and relationships.

This article uses a task ledger to separate the change. It distinguishes codifiable tasks, judgement tasks and relational tasks, then explains why adoption follows a J-shaped path, and finally puts productivity, labour share and rent distribution on one sheet using a 100-worker teaching example. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not forecasts for any country, firm or occupation. Official estimates of AI exposure can be found at the IMF, ILO and OECD entry points [1][2][3]; the survey definition of firm adoption is at the U.S. Census Bureau [5].

A task ledger: three kinds of task, not three occupations

Codifiable tasks have clear inputs, outputs and acceptance criteria. Invoice amounts, contract clauses, code errors and table formats can be written as rules or examples. If a model can reproduce them reliably, the marginal cost of substitution is low. Substitution does not necessarily mean layoffs: the same person may process more documents, or the entry rung of a junior role may narrow.

Judgement tasks require choosing among conflicting objectives: capitalise or expense this cost, accept or reject this client risk, sign or refuse this model output. The hard part is not generating an answer but carrying the consequence. AI can offer candidates, list evidence and simulate the opposing case, but accountability still has to land on a party that can be held responsible.

Relational tasks depend on trust, authorisation, reassurance and long memory. A doctor telling a family bad news, a manager handling a team conflict, a salesperson maintaining a multi-year client: the output is not a paragraph but the other party's willingness to keep cooperating. Tools can reconfigure relational tasks, but rarely replace them in one step.

This matrix cannot answer which occupations will disappear. Different tasks inside one occupation sit in different cells: a junior lawyer spends much time on search and formatting, a senior partner on judgement and client relations; a first-line support agent handles standard questions, an escalation team handles exceptions and reassurance. Putting an occupation into one cell erases the internal difference.

The adoption curve: slow, then fast, then divergent

Firm adoption is not “the model is released and diffuses”. It passes through data preparation, process redesign, permission setting, staff training and responsibility allocation. In the first months, input rises and output may not; a J-shaped curve appears. Output rises when the process stabilises, the model is embedded in systems and staff know when to use it. Figure 2 is a teaching illustration, not a statistical estimate.

The J curve explains a common contradiction: model capability improves quickly while firm-level productivity statistics move slowly. The model is not useless; adoption requires organisational change. Knowledge work in consulting, law and software is more codifiable but has higher integration cost; visual inspection and predictive maintenance in manufacturing may move faster. Inferring macro productivity from model benchmarks confuses the capability curve with the adoption curve.

A numerical example: a 100-worker task ledger

Assume a firm has 100 workers with the following task mix:

Task type Workers Baseline output Output after AI Adoption mode
Codifiable 40 40 40 20 people plus AI
Judgement 35 35 49 35 people plus AI drafts
Relational 25 25 27.5 25 people plus tools
Total 100 100 116.5 —

Codifiable tasks do not disappear; they shrink from 40 people to 20. Judgement output rises 40% with AI assistance, relational output 10%. If the firm keeps all 100 workers, total output rises from 100 to 116.5 and labour productivity rises 16.5%. If it keeps only 80 workers, output can still be around 96.5 and 20 people face redeployment. Neither result is “AI necessarily causes unemployment”; both are “after tasks are reallocated, the firm chooses how many people to keep”.

Now rents. AI tools are not free: model subscriptions, compute, data governance, integration and compliance all cost money. Suppose the vendor charges 8% of output. The capital and intermediate-input share rises. If the cost cannot be passed into prices, profit is compressed; if it can, consumers pay. The labour share depends not only on productivity but on who owns the model, the data and the distribution channel.

Three labour-share scenarios

With a baseline labour share of 62% and a productivity index of 116.5, consider three wage scenarios:

  • Wages unchanged: labour share = 62 / 1.165 ≈ 53.2%.
  • Wages up 5%: 62 × 1.05 / 1.165 ≈ 55.9%.
  • Wages up 12%: 62 × 1.12 / 1.165 ≈ 59.6%.

Figure 3 draws the three scenarios as bars. Higher productivity compresses the labour share through the denominator; wage growth can offset part of it, but does not happen automatically. Wage bargaining, skill scarcity, labour-market tightness and institutions determine the result. Equating productivity growth with wage growth is the most common jump in distribution analysis.

This chart only computes a firm-level share, not government transfers, consumer prices or reinvested capital returns. Real distribution also passes through taxes, subsidies, competition policy and ownership. A more productive economy can have rising real wages and a falling labour share at the same time; the numerator and denominator move differently.

Institutions and policy: four steps, not one “retrain” slogan

Policy debate easily jumps to retraining displaced workers or paying a basic income. Both matter, but neither is enough. A more useful sequence is:

  1. Task audit: list the tasks in a job, their frequency, cost of error and accountability, then judge which segment a model can change. Without a task audit, training targets the wrong skill.
  2. Small-scale pilot: compare three modes in the same process: human, AI draft plus human review, and full automation. Measure output, error rate and worker burden; do not test only a model benchmark.
  3. Retraining and redeployment: training should follow the task ledger: from entry to exception handling, from first draft to client communication, from execution to quality sampling. Generic “learn AI” does not move a job.
  4. Distribution bargaining: write part of the productivity gain into wages, hours, profit sharing or public investment; otherwise the gain concentrates with the owners of models, data and capital.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, exposure is not adoption. The ILO exposure index measures the share of tasks that generative AI could technically affect; it does not say firms have adopted it or jobs have disappeared [2]. Treating exposure as unemployment overstates the short-run shock.

Second, adoption is not productivity. The early part of the J curve can have negative output; integration, review and error repair all consume time. Productivity appears only when the model is embedded in the process and responsibility is assigned.

Third, productivity is not wages. Wages depend on bargaining, skill supply, labour-market tightness and institutions. Productivity growth without wage growth is not rare in history; wage growth without productivity growth can also come from inflation or labour shortage.

Fourth, task substitution can create new tasks. AI lowers the cost of content generation and may increase demand for review, fact-checking, rights clearance and client communication. Whether the new tasks appear, and who does them, depends on organisation and markets, not on model capability alone.

Fifth, cross-country comparison must pass the measurement gate. A firm survey's “use of AI” may include trials, embedded features and third-party services; a task exposure index may use a different occupational classification. Before putting two numbers together, confirm sample, question and timing.

One-page checklist

  • Split the job into tasks before talking about substitution; the task ledger should record frequency, cost of error and accountability.
  • Separate exposure, adoption, productivity and wages; do not infer across layers.
  • Pilot three modes: human, AI draft plus human review, and full automation; record error rates and worker burden.
  • When computing the labour share, report wage scenarios and capital/intermediate-input costs together, not one percentage.
  • The policy sequence is task audit, pilot, redeployment, distribution bargaining; retraining cannot substitute for distribution.
  • Label every teaching number as an assumption; never present it as an official statistic or firm measurement.

Units check: three numbers that should not be mixed

Metric Question it answers Question it cannot answer
Task exposure How many tasks in a job can current technology perform Whether firms adopt it, or whether they cut jobs
Occupation substitution Whether a whole occupation is redefined Who creates new jobs, and what happens to wages
Net employment change The gap between job creation and destruction over a period Whether distribution improves, or which regions bear the cost

Task exposure comes from task classification and describes technical possibility. Occupation substitution requires adoption, falling costs and organisational redesign. Net employment change must put new jobs, exits and participation in the same window. These are three questions, not one “how many jobs will AI replace” number.

Treating exposure as a layoff forecast ignores adoption friction: integration, accountability, customer acceptance and regulation take time. Treating occupation substitution as net employment misses new tasks and new jobs. Treating net employment as distribution misses whether gains go to capital, consumers or particular skill groups. At review, ask what is counted — tasks, jobs or people — over what window, and under which adoption assumption.

Common misreadings

Misreading one: a high model benchmark means an immediate productivity gain. A benchmark measures task capability, not organisational adoption; capability becomes output only through process, data, permissions and responsibility.

Misreading two: high AI exposure means jobs disappear. Exposure is “could be affected”, substitution is “the firm chooses to adopt”, unemployment is a labour-market outcome; adoption, redeployment and aggregate demand sit between them.

Misreading three: productivity growth automatically raises wages. Distribution is institutional; without bargaining, competition and public policy, the gain can remain with capital, model vendors or consumer prices.

Exercises

  1. Take a job you know and split it into eight tasks. Classify each as codifiable, judgement or relational, and write a cost of error.
  2. Using the 100-worker example, recompute total output and labour productivity if codifiable tasks fall from 40 to 15 workers, judgement output rises 30% and relational output rises 8%.
  3. If the AI tool charges 10% of output, how much of the profit pool remains? Compute both flat wages and a 5% wage increase.
  4. Explain why the early J curve can show “more input, less output”; give two integration costs.
  5. Write a causal chain of no more than 80 words using exposure, adoption, productivity and wages, and mark the step without evidence.
  6. Design a firm pilot with three modes, two metrics and one stop rule.
  7. Why can retraining not substitute for distribution bargaining? Use the labour-share formula.
  8. Give an example of a new task created by AI and state the skills it requires.
  9. Compare the survey question on firm AI use with a task exposure index; give two reasons they are not comparable.
  10. Write one AI-adoption policy proposal for small firms, specifying whether it acts on tasks, adoption or distribution.
