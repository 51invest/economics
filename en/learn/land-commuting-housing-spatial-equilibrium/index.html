Land, Commuting and Housing: The Price Ledger of Spatial Equilibrium

Urban space is priced by three ledgers at once: access to jobs, the cost of commuting and congestion, and how housing supply responds to price. Put rent gradients, commuting costs and supply elasticity together, and the same urban growth can show up as higher prices in one city and longer commutes and lower density in another.

Definition

Spatial equilibrium describes how households and firms choose locations, housing size and travel modes so that the net utility of different sites is roughly equalised: land near employment centres commands higher rent, while distant sites carry higher commuting costs. A rent gradient is the rate at which land price falls with distance from the centre; commuting cost includes money, time and congestion discomfort; housing supply elasticity measures how much new housing appears when prices rise[1][2].

This article does not ask how large a city "should" be. It separates a calculable price ledger: how accessibility is capitalised into land rent, how commuting cost limits city radius, and how supply and land-use regulation decide whether new population shows up in prices or in space. Because countries define urban and rural areas differently, comparisons of urban population shares should start by checking the definition[3].

Intuition

Picture a city with one employment centre. Households near the centre can walk to work but pay high rent; households at the edge pay lower rent but spend more time and money commuting. If the edge is cheap enough, households move out; if commuting is too expensive, they prefer to pay higher rent near the centre. Their choices bring the rent saved and the extra commute into balance. That balance determines the city radius and the shape of the rent curve.

When population grows, the same land must hold more households. If housing can be built quickly, new demand mostly becomes more housing and prices rise modestly. If land and construction are constrained, new demand mostly becomes higher rents and more intense bidding. The same growth can therefore appear as "higher housing prices" in one place and "outward expansion plus longer commutes" in another. This is not a moral judgement; it is a difference in supply conditions.

Formal expression

In a monocentric city with commuting cost rising linearly with distance, land rent can be written as:

[ R(d)=R_0-gd ]

where (R_0) is central rent, (g) is the fall in rent per kilometre, and (d) is distance from the centre. If edge rent is (R_{\text{edge}}), the city radius is:

[ d^*=\frac{R_0-R_{\text{edge}}}{g} ]

For a household at distance (d), the daily round-trip money cost is:

[ C(d)=2cd ]

where (c) is the one-way cost per kilometre and the factor 2 accounts for the return trip. A fuller version adds time value (vT(d)), where (T(d)) is one-way travel time and (v) is the value of time per hour. Combining the parts gives net locational cost:

[ \text{NetCost}(d)=R(d)h+2cd+2vT(d) ]

where (h) is housing floor area. Spatial equilibrium means this net cost tends to equalise across sites that households consider comparable.

Housing supply responds to price according to:

[ \frac{\Delta H}{H}=\varepsilon_s\frac{\Delta P}{P} ]

where (\varepsilon_s) is housing supply elasticity. When demand rises by (\Delta H_d), a low elasticity requires a large price increase to produce a small supply response; a high elasticity produces more housing for a smaller price increase.

Road congestion can be approximated by average travel time rising with vehicle volume:

[ T(N)=t_0+\kappa N ]

where (t_0) is free-flow time, (\kappa) is the average delay added per vehicle, and (N) is the number of vehicles. If each entering vehicle raises everyone else's travel time, marginal social cost exceeds average cost. That gap is the congestion externality and the analytical case for congestion pricing (author's derivation).

Conditions

  1. The monocentric model assumes most jobs are downtown. Polycentric employment, suburban jobs and remote work change commute directions and rent gradients (author's assessment).
  2. Rent gradients require tradable land and enforceable property rights. When land is allocated administratively, rationing rules replace the price signal[1].
  3. Commuting cost is more than fuel: reliability, transfers, walking safety and crowding discomfort all matter. Counting only out-of-pocket cash understates the cost[2].
  4. Housing supply elasticity depends on land availability, floor-area limits, construction lags, infrastructure and community opposition. It can differ by neighbourhood within the same city[2].
  5. Transport investment changes accessibility, and therefore rents and migration. New road capacity can induce additional driving; it should not be judged only by short-run congestion relief (author's assessment).
  6. Spatial equilibrium does not guarantee distributive fairness. Low-income households may end up with longer commutes or worse housing; an efficiency gain is not automatically an equity gain[1][2].

Example and calculation

Take a teaching city with central rent (R_0=4{,}000) CNY per square metre, edge rent (R_{\text{edge}}=500), and a rent gradient (g=350) CNY per kilometre. The city radius is:

[ d^*=\frac{4{,}000-500}{350}=10\text{ km} ]

For a 50-square-metre dwelling, central land value is (4{,}000\times50=200{,}000) CNY and edge land value is (500\times50=25{,}000) CNY, a difference of 175,000 CNY. This is land value only, excluding construction and locational amenities.

For commuting, assume a one-way money cost of 2 CNY per kilometre. A 10-kilometre round trip costs (2\times2\times10=40) CNY per day. If the one-way trip takes 30 minutes and time is valued at 40 CNY per hour, the round-trip time cost is (2\times0.5\times40=40) CNY per day, for a total of 80 CNY per day. These are teaching assumptions, not observed commuting statistics for a particular city.

For housing supply, suppose the city has 10,000 households and demand rises 5%, or 500 households. With supply elasticity (\varepsilon_s=0.5), price must rise 10% to bring forth 5% more housing. With (\varepsilon_s=0.2), price must rise 25%:

Supply elasticity Demand increase Required price increase New housing
0.5 5% 10% 5%
0.2 5% 25% 5%

For congestion, assume free-flow one-way travel time is 20 minutes and each additional vehicle adds 0.0008 minutes of average delay. At 30,000 vehicles, one-way time is about 44 minutes; at 50,000 vehicles, it is about 60 minutes. More vehicles mean longer average commutes, but each driver bears only their own time, not the delay imposed on others (author's linear congestion calculation).

Common mistakes

"High rent is just speculation." Rent first reflects accessibility and scarcity: land near jobs, schools, transport and public services attracts higher bids. Speculation can amplify swings, but it does not explain the basic shape of a rent gradient[1].

"Higher supply elasticity is always better." High elasticity can damp price growth, but it can also push the city toward low-density expansion and raise infrastructure, commuting and ecological costs[2]. Elasticity is a condition, not a single objective.

"Congestion charging is just another tax." The external cost comes from the time each vehicle adds to everyone else's trip; charging puts that cost back into the travel decision. How the revenue is used and whether public transport is available determines the equity outcome (author's assessment).
