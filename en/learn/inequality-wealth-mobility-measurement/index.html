Inequality, Wealth Accounting and Intergenerational Mobility

Put income inequality, wealth inequality and intergenerational mobility on one accounting sheet to separate how large the gap is, why it persists and which part policy can change.

Problem and scope

Income inequality asks who receives how much disposable income in a given year. Wealth inequality asks who owns how much net worth at a point in time. Intergenerational mobility asks how much a parent's position determines a child's position. These three questions are often compressed into one sentence: "the rich are getting richer and the poor poorer." But their units, time horizons and policy implications differ. A country can have a moderate income Gini and a very concentrated wealth distribution; conversely, income gaps can be large while mobility is high. Mixing the three together produces the wrong remedy.

This article separates inequality into five accounting links: market income, taxes, transfers, public services and asset appreciation. The first four determine current disposable income; the last determines how stock wealth accumulates across generations. Intergenerational mobility then connects the current outcome back to the previous generation and tests whether the starting point is reproducing the endpoint. The framework does not aim for one universal indicator. It requires every claim to state the unit (individual or household), the period, whether income is before or after tax, and whether wealth includes pensions and housing.

Five accounting choices

First, individual or household. Individual earnings inequality is partly offset by specialization within households. Household income per equivalent adult puts a high earner supporting three people on the same scale as three medium earners. But the equivalence scale changes results: whether a two-person household is treated as 1.7 or 1.4 adults can shift bottom-quintile outcomes by several percentage points.

Second, before or after tax. Market-income Gini is usually higher than disposable-income Gini; the gap is the redistributive size of taxes and transfers. Looking only at market income labels a high-tax, high-benefit country as more unequal; looking only at disposable income hides the unemployment risk that exists before transfers.

Third, flow or stock. Income is an annual flow; wealth is a point-in-time stock. Wealth can come from saving, inheritance, asset price gains and leverage. A household with modest annual income may have high net worth because it bought housing early. Inferring wealth inequality from income inequality usually understates concentration.

Fourth, cash or in-kind. Public health care, education and housing subsidies do not appear in cash income, yet they materially change available resources. Ignoring in-kind transfers understates the real consumption capacity of low-income households; valuing them at full cost overstates their welfare benefit.

Fifth, nominal or real. Consumption baskets differ by income group: low-income households spend more on food and rent, high-income households more on services and culture. Deflating everything by one national CPI can understate the cost-of-living pressure at the bottom. A sound report shows nominal income, national-CPI real income and a group-specific basket deflator side by side.

Why wealth is harder than income

Wealth accounting has at least four problems. The first is valuation: listed equity has a market price; private firms, art and real estate need appraisal. The same house may differ by thirty percent between 2019 and 2022 while its rent stream is unchanged. The second is debt: net worth must subtract mortgages, consumer credit and business liabilities. Counting only assets turns a highly leveraged household into a rich one. The third is pension entitlement: a pay-as-you-go pension is a government promise about future benefits, not a tradable asset. Discounting it to present value can sharply change the wealth distribution of older households. The fourth is hidden and cross-border wealth: offshore accounts, trusts and nominee holdings do not appear automatically in household surveys, so top-end wealth is often underestimated.

These problems do not make wealth data unusable. They require reporting survey-based, national-accounts-based and rich-list-based measures separately. Household surveys cover the middle and bottom but have few top-end observations. Rich lists capture very large fortunes but miss much of the distribution. Sector balance sheets give totals without within-household distribution. Comparing the three sources tells us whether a concentration estimate is credible in level or only in trend.

Four numbers for mobility

Intergenerational mobility is not just a phrase about "poor kids staying poor." It can be measured with four numbers.

First, the intergenerational income elasticity: how much percent higher a child's adult income is for each one percent higher parental income. An elasticity of 0.2 implies a weak start effect; 0.6 implies a strong one. Life-cycle timing matters: children's earnings approach lifetime earnings only in their thirties or forties, so measuring too early understates the elasticity.

Second, a quintile transition matrix: the share of children born in the bottom fifth who remain in the bottom fifth as adults, and the share born in the top fifth who remain there. The matrix shows whether the trap is at the bottom, the top or the middle, which a single elasticity cannot.

Third, absolute mobility: whether a child's adult income exceeds the parent's income at the same age in real terms. Relative mobility is about rank; absolute mobility is about living standards. Growth can make absolute mobility positive while relative mobility is unchanged.

Fourth, education and occupational channels: whether education returns, licensing and geographic migration convert parental resources into child advantage. If education expands faster than good jobs, the signalling value of a degree falls and the sense of mobility weakens.

A numerical example

Take a teaching sample of 100 people divided into five income quintiles of 20. Market-income shares are 8%, 13%, 18%, 24% and 37%. The top-to-bottom ratio is 37/8 = 4.6. Suppose taxes and transfers raise the bottom share to 10% and cut the top share to 34%, leaving the middle unchanged. The after-tax top-to-bottom ratio falls to 3.4. Redistribution does not need to flatten the top completely: moving two or three percentage points at each end changes the ratio materially.

For wealth, use another teaching set of shares: 5%, 10%, 17%, 25% and 43%. The top-to-bottom ratio is 8.6, higher than the income ratio of 4.6. The reason is not that the rich have higher income; it is that asset appreciation, inheritance and leverage accumulate in the stock. If bottom incomes grow 1.2% a year in real terms and top incomes grow 2.4%, after 30 years their levels are 1.43 and 2.04 times the starting values. The original 4.6 ratio becomes about 6.6. This compound arithmetic shows the role of time better than the phrase "the gap is widening."

For the mobility matrix, use four teaching probabilities: a bottom-quintile child stays at the bottom with probability 40% and reaches the top with probability 8%; a top-quintile child stays at the top with probability 35% and falls to the bottom with probability 6%. If bottom persistence falls from 40% to 30% and top persistence from 35% to 28%, mobility improves while the current income Gini may barely move. This is why current inequality and intergenerational mobility cannot substitute for each other.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, averages are not families. A rising Gini may come from asset gains at the top 1% or from unemployment at the bottom. The same coefficient change implies different policies. Look at quantiles and mechanisms.

Second, mobility is not equal opportunity. A society can let a few bottom-quintile children enter university and change their lives, but if only five percent of places are open, most are still excluded. A mobility rate is a conditional probability, not an opportunity available to everyone.

Third, redistribution has costs. High taxes on labour income can reduce work incentives; taxes on capital gains can change investment horizons and asset allocation. A serious discussion puts the distortionary cost of taxes, the targeting error of transfers and administrative cost on the table, rather than comparing Gini coefficients alone.

Fourth, wealth data have survivorship bias. Bankrupt households, negative net worth and survey refusals are often missed. Counting only positive wealth overstates the bottom. A wealth report should state the share of households with negative net worth and with zero wealth.

Fifth, the elasticity is not causal. Parental income and child income are correlated through genes, education, networks, geography and luck. Identifying policy causality needs natural experiments, instrumental variables or difference-in-differences. Correlation must not be written as "parental income determines child income."

Additional boundary one: wealth Gini is not a welfare index. It imputes rent on owner-occupied housing, discounts pensions and estimates private equity; the same household can change value under three assumptions. Negative net worth also cannot enter a zero-lower-bound share calculation and must be shown separately.

Additional boundary two: intergenerational elasticity depends on the age window. Father and child income at 40 is not the same as lifetime income; near retirement, capital income and transfers matter more. Cross-country work must also handle purchasing power, taxes and public goods.

Additional boundary three: redistribution is not moving top shares to the bottom. Tax bases move, behaviour responds and in-kind transfers cost money. The 4.6 ratio from 8/13/18/24/37 and the 3.4 after-tax ratio from 10/34 sit behind three doors: tax design, transfers and measurement.

Additional numerical counterexample: if bottom real income grows 1.2% and top income 2.4% a year, after 30 years their multiples are 1.43 and 2.04, so the 4.6 ratio widens to about 6.5. This shows compounding direction, not a forecast; policy must state whether it acts on flow, stock or the mobility channel.

Policy application: what a wealth tax can and cannot do

A wealth tax is often proposed as a direct fix for wealth concentration. The accounting framework says it can change the distribution of measured wealth, but only if three conditions hold. First, the tax base must capture the assets where concentration actually sits: listed equity, private business equity, real estate and financial claims, not only bank deposits. Second, valuation must be regular and consistent; an annual tax on assets appraised every five years creates avoidance and disputes. Third, enforcement must reach cross-border and trust structures, otherwise the base shrinks to the visible middle.

Even a well-designed wealth tax faces a revenue constraint. A 1% tax on net worth above a high threshold may raise less than expected if wealthy households revalue, relocate or shift assets into exempt forms. The distributional effect can still be progressive, but revenue may be modest relative to income taxes and transfers. The policy question is not "is concentration bad?" but "which instrument changes which link at what cost?" A wealth tax acts on the stock; a transfer acts on the flow; education and inheritance rules act on the mobility channel. They are not substitutes.

A second application is public-service valuation. If in-kind health and education transfers are counted at cost, measured disposable income at the bottom rises and the after-tax Gini falls. But the same transfer may be valued differently by recipients: a family that does not use a service does not gain the same utility as one that does. Reporting both cost-based and use-based valuations prevents one number from carrying more weight than it can bear.

One-page checklist

  • State the unit first: individual or household, before or after tax, flow or stock, cash or in-kind.
  • Report market income, disposable income and in-kind transfers as three separate versions.
  • Report assets, liabilities, net worth and the pension treatment together.
  • Report elasticity, transition matrix, absolute mobility and education channels together.
  • Every "the gap is widening" claim needs a base year, end year, price deflator and demographic structure.
  • Every policy proposal needs the distortionary cost and targeting error of taxes and transfers.
  • Label every teaching number as an assumption; never pass it off as official statistics.

Common misreads

Misread one: a high Gini equals injustice. Gini describes the shape of a distribution, not starting points, taxes, public goods or mobility channels; two countries with the same Gini can differ in whether education or inheritance locks status.

Misread two: high mobility equals equal opportunity. Mobility is an outcome; opportunity depends on access to education, health care and credit. If upward movement happens only in a few cities, the average mobility rate hides spatial stratification.

Misread three: a wealth tax solves everything at once. Valuation, migration, asset classes and collection costs change the result; without spending and competition policy, a wealth tax cuts the stock once but may not change the mobility channel.

Exercises

  1. Use 8%, 13%, 18%, 24% and 37% to compute the top-to-bottom income ratio; then recompute it with after-tax shares of 10% and 34%.
  2. Explain with valuation, inheritance and leverage why wealth Gini is usually higher than income Gini in the same society.
  3. If bottom real income grows 1.2% a year and top income 2.4%, compute their 30-year multiples.
  4. A transition matrix moves bottom persistence from 40% to 30% and top persistence from 35% to 28%; which direction of mobility improves?
  5. Give one absolute-mobility and one relative-mobility example, and explain why growth can move them differently.
  6. Why can a single CPI understate the cost of living at the bottom? Give two basket differences.
  7. How does discounting a pay-as-you-go pension change the wealth distribution of older households? State the role of the discount rate.
  8. Design a wealth survey convention that includes negative net worth, zero wealth and refusals, with two quality checks.
  9. For a 1% wealth tax, list the three necessary conditions: base, valuation frequency and enforcement reach.
  10. Use a 100-person quintile sample to explain why the same Gini can imply different policies.
  11. Identify the logical error in 'higher mobility equals equal opportunity' and explain it with conditional probability.
  12. Write one redistribution proposal and state whether it acts on income flow, wealth stock or the mobility channel.
