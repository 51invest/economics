Information Asymmetry, Signaling and Mechanism Design

When one side knows more, prices and contracts distort; signaling, screening and incentives try to reveal information through verifiable costs.

ModuleECO-M05 · 博弈与信息
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 16 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Information asymmetry means one party knows more than the other. Adverse selection occurs before a transaction: high-risk parties are more willing to buy insurance or sell bad assets, low-risk parties exit and prices rise. Moral hazard occurs after: one party changes behaviour because the cost falls on the other. A signal is an action by the high-quality party to reveal information; screening is a contract designed by the less-informed party so different types self-select. Mechanism design sets rules, payments and constraints so participants' incentives align with the objective.

Intuition

Information asymmetry is a used-car deal: the seller knows the condition, the buyer only knows the average. The buyer bids for the average car, good-car sellers exit because the price is too low, only problem cars remain, the average falls and the price falls again — adverse selection. A signal is a costly action that proves quality: degrees, warranties, collateral and brands are signals because low-quality sellers find them hard to imitate. Mechanism design sets rules that give participants incentives to tell the truth or act as agreed, such as auctions, insurance deductibles and performance contracts.

Mechanism

Insurance uses deductibles, co-payment and health questionnaires to screen risk; credit uses collateral, guarantees and records; labour markets use credentials, probation and referrals; second-hand markets use warranties, inspections and reputation. A signal is credible only if it costs the high-quality party less than the low-quality party, such as a verified track record versus a verbal promise. Auctions use reserve prices, ascending or sealed-bid rules and winner-payment rules to handle information and strategy; antitrust and disclosure rules reduce collusion and misrepresentation [1][2][3].

Worked example

Suppose an insurer expects 20 of 100 people to claim CNY 5,000 a year and 80 to claim CNY 1,000, for an average CNY 1,800. At a flat premium of CNY 1,800, low-risk people find it expensive and exit; the remaining high-risk group averages CNY 5,000, so the premium must rise to CNY 5,000, pushing more low-risk people out in a spiral. With a CNY 2,000 deductible, low-risk people bear less and the premium falls, while high-risk people still buy, stabilising the pool. If the deductible is too high, low-income people may not buy at all and adverse selection worsens. These are teaching assumptions and exclude administrative cost.

Second worked example: adverse selection in an insurance pool

Suppose 100 people buy insurance: 20 are high-risk with expected annual claims of CNY 10,000, and 80 are low-risk with expected claims of CNY 2,000. If the insurer cannot tell them apart, it prices the average: expected claims = (20 × 10,000 + 80 × 2,000)/100 = CNY 3,600; adding CNY 500 of operating cost gives a premium of CNY 4,100. Low-risk buyers see a premium above their CNY 2,000 expected loss and may exit. If 60 low-risk buyers leave, 20 high-risk and 20 low-risk remain; average claims = (20 × 10,000 + 20 × 2,000)/40 = CNY 6,000, and the premium rises to CNY 6,500. Deductibles, underwriting, group cover and mandates can mitigate it, but all have costs. These are teaching assumptions.

Formal expression

Expected value under information asymmetry can be written as V = p_H × V_H + (1 − p_H) × V_L, where p_H is the buyer’s probability of “high quality” and V_H and V_L are the values in the high- and low-quality states. A signal is credible only when the separation condition holds: the high-quality party’s signal cost C_H is low enough and the low-quality party’s imitation cost C_L is high enough, so C_H < S < C_L.

Boundary and common mistakes

First, information asymmetry is not fraud; many transactions work through contracts and reputation. Second, a costly signal is not automatically credible; the cost may be subsidised or forgeable. Third, mandatory disclosure and standardisation reduce gaps but can raise compliance cost. Fourth, moral hazard cannot be solved by raising deductibles alone; excessive cost-sharing can drive out those who most need cover. Fifth, mechanism design in real markets faces bounded rationality, strategic behaviour and regulation, so fully rational participants cannot be assumed.

Failure modes

The first failure is ignoring signal cost: if certificates, warranties or collateral are easy to fake, the signal loses credibility; it must be more costly for low-quality parties. The second is conflating adverse selection with moral hazard: adverse selection happens before the transaction (who enters), moral hazard after (behaviour changes), and deductibles affect them differently. The third is assuming a mechanism designer acts in a vacuum: regulation, competition, politics and player strategies make the theoretical optimum hard to implement. The fourth is ignoring market power: platforms can reduce information asymmetry with data, brands and ranking, or exploit it. The fifth is treating 'transparency' as a cure-all: even with full information, differences in comprehension, time and trust lead to different choices.

Three checks

First, who holds the information and who bears the consequences: does the informed party have an incentive to hide or change behaviour? Second, is the signal credible: does it cost something, can it be verified, and how expensive is imitation for low-quality parties? Third, is the mechanism implementable: does it account for regulation, competition, player strategies and exit options? Beyond these, ask who is excluded: those who cannot insure, certify or afford the signal may be left out of the market.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Information asymmetry is easily confused with adverse selection, moral hazard and signalling. Adverse selection happens before the transaction, when the less-informed party cannot distinguish quality and high-quality parties exit; moral hazard happens after, when the informed party changes behaviour; a signal is a costly disclosure or pledge by the informed party to prove quality. They often coexist but call for different tools. A counterexample is 'more transparency solves everything': if information is hard to verify, participants lack comprehension, or platforms have incentives to manipulate ranking, disclosure can become a formality. Another is 'the more expensive the signal, the more credible': a costly signal can deter low-quality imitation, but it can also exclude capable people who cannot afford it; design must consider verifiability and fairness. Compare mechanisms on information structure, incentives, verification cost and exit options.

Scope and limits

This information-asymmetry framework applies to markets where types, actions and outcomes are observable and contracts and regulation can be described. If information is completely unverifiable, participants cannot be identified, or trade happens in relational networks, standard signalling and screening models fail. It does not apply to treating adverse selection as the explanation for every market problem, nor to mechanism design that ignores fairness and privacy. Map information structure, incentives and verification cost, then test separating, pooling and exit options; an effective mechanism is not necessarily fair.

Answer key
  1. Adverse selection is before the transaction, as high-risk parties are more likely to enter; moral hazard is after, as behaviour changes when costs are shifted.
  2. (20×5,000 + 80×1,000)/100 = CNY 1,800.
  3. If the low-quality party can send the same signal at the same low cost, the signal cannot separate types; only a lower cost for the high-quality party gives it information content.
