Externalities and Public Goods

Externalities are effects on third parties that prices do not reflect, while public goods are nonexcludable and non-rival; both make market provision diverge from the efficient quantity.

ModuleECO-M06 · 福利与市场失灵
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Externalities are effects of a market exchange on third parties that prices do not reflect. A negative externality harms third parties; a positive externality benefits them [1]. A public good is both nonexcludable and non-rival: no one can prevent others from using it, and one person's use does not reduce what others can use [3].

Intuition

A factory discharges wastewater, and downstream residents bear the cleanup cost. The factory counts materials and wages but not the pollution, so output exceeds the social optimum [1]. Vaccination lowers others' infection risk, a positive externality, so the market may provide too little. A flood barrier cannot protect only those who pay, and protecting one more household costs almost nothing. If each household decides alone and hopes others will pay, the barrier may never be built even when total benefits exceed the cost.

Formal expression

Let Q be output, P price, PMC private marginal cost, and MEC marginal external cost. Then SMC = PMC + MEC. Market equilibrium satisfies P = PMC, while the optimum satisfies P = SMC; with a negative externality the market produces too much [1]. A Pigouvian tax equal to MEC at the optimal quantity aligns private and social costs [2]. The efficiency condition for a public good is Σ MRS_i = MC: the sum of users' marginal rates of substitution equals marginal cost. With voluntary payment, free riding pushes provision lower [3].

When it applies

  1. The effect does not pass through prices. Effects transmitted through input prices or wages are pecuniary externalities, not the externalities discussed here.
  2. External cost can be measured. A Pigouvian tax needs an estimate of marginal external damage; error moves the tax from the optimum.
  3. Both public-good properties hold. Nonexcludable but rival goods are common resources [3]; non-rival but excludable goods are closer to club goods.
  4. Distribution and administrative cost are set aside. The efficiency condition does not say who bears the pollution or the tax.
  5. The comparison is static. Curves are stable; accumulated pollution or technical change alters the optimal tax.

Scope note: the Samuelson condition ΣMRS=MC, the phrases “monetary externality” and “club good”, and the precise rule that a Pigouvian tax should equal the marginal external cost at the optimum are all outside the supports of the three OpenStax sources registered here (the 13.3 review found no term “club good”, and 12.3 does not state the precise rule). They are presented as textbook convention and model assumptions: a methodological statement, not a conclusion quoted from the registered sources.

Worked example

All numbers are teaching assumptions. Demand is P = 110 - Q, PMC = 20 + Q (yuan per unit), and each unit imposes MEC = 30 yuan, so SMC = 50 + Q.

Market: 110 - Q = 20 + Q gives Q = 45 and P = 65 yuan. Social optimum: 110 - Q = 50 + Q gives Q = 30 and P = 80 yuan.

Case Output Q Price P Private cost PMC Social cost SMC
Market equilibrium 45 65 65 95
Social optimum 30 80 50 80

The market overproduces by 45 − 30 = 15 units. The gap between SMC and demand widens linearly from 0 yuan at Q = 30 to 30 yuan at Q = 45, so the deadweight loss is the triangle between the two curves, 0.5 × 30 × 15 = 225 yuan. A tax of 30 yuan per unit, equal to MEC, raises effective private marginal cost to 50 + Q, the same as SMC; consumers pay 80 yuan, producers net 80 − 30 = 50 yuan per unit, and output returns to 30 units. Public-good example: a flood barrier costs 100 yuan and two households each value it at 60 yuan. Total value, 120 yuan, exceeds the cost, so building it is efficient [3], but neither household gains by paying alone (100 yuan of cost for 60 yuan of benefit).

Common mistakes

Any side effect is an externality. Lost profit from competition is price transmission, not an externality.

Public goods are whatever government provides. The test is the two properties, not the provider.

Positive externalities cause overproduction. They cause underprovision; negative externalities cause overproduction.

A Pigouvian tax bans pollution. It makes firms bear the external cost and usually leaves some output.

Knowledge-point review

externality

Definition: An externality is an uncompensated cost or unpaid benefit imposed on parties outside a transaction, either negative or positive.

Mechanism: It makes private costs and benefits diverge from social ones, so output is too high or low; Pigouvian taxes, subsidies, property rights and regulation can internalise it.

Example: For example, factory pollution imposes health costs on downstream residents not reflected in steel prices, so output tends to be too high.

Boundary: Externalities require causal impact and welfare relevance; identifying affected parties, measuring costs and avoiding double counting are hard, and Coasean solutions need clear rights and low transaction costs.

public goods

Definition: Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival: one person's use does not exclude others or reduce what they can use.

Mechanism: Non-excludability invites free riding and underprovision; public provision, tax finance, club goods and co-production are common responses.

Example: For example, street lighting illuminates the whole street and cannot be limited to payers, so voluntary funding often falls short.

Boundary: Pure public goods are rare and many are quasi-public; exclusion technology, congestion and community boundaries change publicness, and public provision is not automatically efficient.

common-pool resources

Definition: Common-pool resources are non-excludable but rival, such as fisheries, pastures and groundwater, and overuse can exhaust them.

Mechanism: Each user bears only part of the cost, so collective action tends toward over-extraction; quotas, property rights, community rules and pricing can restrain use.

Example: For example, each boat in an open fishery catches a little more until the stock collapses, with short-term gain and shared long-term cost.

Boundary: Tragedy of the commons is not inevitable; Ostrom-style community governance can work with clear boundaries, cheap monitoring and participatory rules, though scale and mobility weaken it.

efficiency and equity

Definition: Efficiency means achieving goals with minimal waste under given resources and technology; equity concerns how benefits, opportunities and burdens are distributed.

Mechanism: Efficiency gains can widen inequality and redistribution can weaken incentives; compensation principles, opportunity fairness and maximin criteria offer different standards.

Example: For example, removing a subsidy may allocate resources better yet sharply raise low-income households' burdens, requiring offsetting transfers.

Boundary: There is no unique aggregation of efficiency and equity; welfare functions, rights and procedural justice carry different weights, so a Pareto improvement is not automatically acceptable.

general equilibrium and social choice

Definition: General equilibrium studies how prices and quantities clear across many markets at once; social choice studies how individual preferences aggregate into collective decisions.

Mechanism: General equilibrium stresses budgets, relative prices and cross-market feedback; social choice uses Arrow conditions, voting rules and welfare functions to aggregate preferences.

Example: For example, an energy price rise affects transport, food and wages at once, so partial-market analysis misses the chain.

Boundary: General equilibrium relies on strong assumptions such as perfect competition, no externalities and stable preferences; social choice faces disputes over intensity, capability and procedural legitimacy.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is closest to a public good? A. A toll road; B. National defense; C. Bread; D. Pay television.
  2. Calculation. If MEC rises to 40 yuan with demand and PMC unchanged, what are the social optimum quantity and price?
  3. Conditions. Why should the Pigouvian tax equal MEC at the optimal quantity rather than at the market quantity?
Suggested answers
  1. B. Defense is hard to exclude and one more user does not reduce protection. The other options are excludable, so they are not pure public goods (toll roads and pay TV are closer to club goods).
  2. SMC = 60 + Q; 110 - Q = 60 + Q gives Q = 25 units and P = 85 yuan. MEC is a constant 40 yuan per unit, so the Pigouvian tax should be 40 yuan per unit.
  3. The policy target is the social optimum, so the tax is set at the optimal quantity; if external cost varies with output, taxing the market-quantity value yields a different equilibrium.
