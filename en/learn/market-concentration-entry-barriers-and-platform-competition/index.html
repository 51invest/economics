Market Concentration, Entry Barriers and Platform Competition

Market concentration describes how shares are distributed, entry barriers describe why new rivals struggle to enter, and platform competition adds network effects, multi-homing and switching costs to the same picture. High concentration does not automatically mean high prices, and low concentration does not automatically mean effective competition; market boundaries, entry conditions and actual conduct matter.

Definition

Market concentration measures how much of a market a few firms hold, often with the HHI (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) or CR4 (combined share of the top four). Barriers to entry are the cost, legal or network obstacles that a new firm faces [2]. Market power is the ability to keep prices above competitive levels without losing too many customers.

Platform competition also depends on network effects, multi-homing and switching costs. More users can make a platform more valuable to users on the other side; merchants can join several platforms; moving social connections, reviews and historical data can change how hard entry is.

Intuition

Suppose four firms have shares of 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%. HHI is the sum of squared percentage shares: 1600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3000. If four firms each held 25%, HHI would be 4 × 625 = 2500. The more uneven the shares, the higher the HHI.

But a high HHI does not prove high prices. If new entrants appear every year, customers can switch cheaply and import capacity is ample, incumbents may still be afraid to raise prices. Conversely, a low HHI does not rule out market power: firms may coordinate through transparent pricing, common channels or tacit understandings. HHI is a screen, not a conclusion [1].

Formal expression

HHI is the sum of squared percentage market shares:

HHI = Σ s_i², where s_i is firm i's percentage share and the index runs from 0 to 10,000.

The change from a merger is:

ΔHHI = (s_a + s_b)² − s_a² − s_b² = 2 s_a s_b

where s_a and s_b are the merging firms' shares. The U.S. Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission Horizontal Merger Guidelines treat HHI below 1,500 as unconcentrated, 1,500–2,500 as moderately concentrated and above 2,500 as highly concentrated; these thresholds are screening points, not legal conclusions [1].

Market power is often summarized by the Lerner index:

L = (P − MC) / P

A larger L means a higher price relative to marginal cost. But L depends on estimating marginal cost; accounting cost, capital cost and risk adjustments can change the result.

Entry can be written as:

entry return = sustainable profit after entry − sunk entry cost − competitive loss after entry

Entry occurs only when the entrant expects a positive return and can overcome legal, capital, technical and network barriers.

When it applies

  1. Define the relevant market first. Product substitutability, geography and customer group change HHI completely; a whole-industry share is not a relevant-market share [1].
  2. Shares must reflect competitive constraints. A high share with thin margins, excess capacity or open imports may not imply matching market power.
  3. Separate structure from conduct. HHI is a structural measure; price coordination, exclusivity and predatory pricing need separate evidence.
  4. Analyze platforms as multi-sided markets. Counting users on one side only ignores merchants, advertisers and developers; network effects can raise efficiency and exclusion at the same time.
  5. Merger thresholds are not verdicts. Low-HHI mergers usually raise fewer concerns, while high-HHI mergers need more review, but neither is automatically unlawful [1].

Worked example

Suppose five firms have shares of 35%, 25%, 20%, 12% and 8% (teaching assumptions):

HHI = 35² + 25² + 20² + 12² + 8² = 1225 + 625 + 400 + 144 + 64 = 2458

That is moderately concentrated. If the second and fourth firms merge, the combined share is 37%, and the new HHI is:

2458 + 2 × 25 × 12 = 2458 + 600 = 3058

The increase of 600 points moves the market into the highly concentrated range. If prices do not change after the merger, switching costs are low and capacity is ample, regulators may still see enough competitive constraint. If the merger removes the only price challenger, deeper review is warranted.

Now consider a platform. It has 60% of users, but 70% of merchants also use a second platform and 40% of users multi-home. The user share is high, yet merchant-side multi-homing limits the platform's ability to raise merchant fees. If an exclusivity clause cuts merchant multi-homing to 15%, the same user share may support stronger market power.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not real market data.

Common mistakes

“An HHI above 2,500 means monopoly.” 2,500 is a review screen, not a legal verdict; market definition, entry, conduct and efficiencies still matter [1].

“A high share is market power.” A high share can come from lower prices or a better product. If margins are thin, customers switch easily and imports are open, the ability to raise price is limited.

“A bigger platform has stronger network effects, so it is always anticompetitive.” Network effects can improve matching and can also create entry barriers. Multi-homing, interoperability and exclusivity determine the outcome.

“A merger is only about the post-merger HHI.” Also examine the HHI increase, the competitive constraint removed, entry difficulty and efficiency offsets; a move from 2,400 to 2,600 can matter even when the level looks familiar [1].

Boundary case: Why can a high-share platform still face competitive constraints?

Five firms hold shares of 35%, 25%, 20%, 12% and 8%, giving an HHI of 2,458. If the second and fourth merge into a 37% share, the HHI becomes 2,458 + 2 × 25 × 12 = 3,058, a 600-point increase. Suppose a platform has a 60% user share, but 70% of merchants also sell on a second platform; merchant multi-homing limits the platform's ability to raise merchant fees. If exclusivity clauses cut multi-homing to 15%, the same user share may support greater market power. Conversely, if prices fall 3% after a merger, switching costs are low and capacity is ample, a higher HHI does not prove harm.

The conclusion reverses when share and behaviour diverge: a high share with low-cost multi-homing, or a new entrant reaching 10% within a year, means concentration alone cannot decide the case. The boundary is that HHI describes the distribution of shares, entry barriers describe whether rivals can enter, and platform competition also depends on multi-homing, switching costs and exclusivity. The three are not substitutes.
