Law Enforcement and Market Trust: The Testable Part of Comparative Institutions

Law promotes market trust not through the quality of statutes but through clear property rights, enforceable contracts and calculable breach costs. This article uses transaction costs, enforcement costs and trust substitution to open the 'good institutions, fast growth' black box and keep only the testable part.

Definition and intuition

Law enforcement is not the existence of statutes but whether rights can be asserted, contracts performed and breaches punished. Market trust comes from three observable mechanisms: clear property rights, so traders know what belongs to whom; enforceable contracts, so breach has a calculable remedy; and affordable enforcement, so litigation or arbitration does not cost more than the transaction.

Transaction costs include search, negotiation, contracting, monitoring and enforcement. When they are high, firms substitute relationships, vertical integration or cooperatives for market contracts; when low, market contracts are more efficient. Comparative institutions should not prove 'more law is better' but identify which governance structure saves cost for which transaction[1][2].

Comparative institutions easily slip into macro narratives that pair 'good institutions' with 'fast growth' without a mechanism. The testable part is concrete: property-registration time, contract-enforcement days, breach-remedy ratios, arbitration coverage. These variables enter regressions and firm decisions.

Three mechanisms

Property rights: registration and publicity reduce disputes over ownership. Clearer rights make mortgages, transfers and investment easier; unclear rights require more due diligence and guarantees.

Contracts: courts or arbitrators provide third-party enforcement. The more complete the contract and the more calculable the remedy, the more traders cooperate with strangers; but more complete contracts also cost more to draft and monitor.

Trust substitution: when formal enforcement is costly or slow, relationships, reputation and community norms substitute. Relational governance works in repeated, small-group, specific-asset transactions; as trading expands and participants exit, relational trust decays and formal enforcement matters again[3][4].

The three are complements, not substitutes. Formal enforcement provides a floor, relational trust lowers routine monitoring cost; clear property rights lower due diligence, enforceable contracts lower breach risk.

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a 1 million yuan equipment purchase and the buyer fears defective delivery. Formal contract: legal, notary and arbitration costs 20,000 yuan, enforcement takes six months, and breach damages are 200,000. Relational: no detailed contract, relying on long-term cooperation and industry reputation, monitoring costs 10,000, but breach only ends the relationship and may lose 300,000.

With a 5% breach probability, formal expected cost is 2 + 0.05×20 = 30,000 yuan; relational is 1 + 0.05×30 = 25,000. Relational governance is cheaper here. If breach probability rises to 15%, formal is 2 + 3 = 50,000, relational 1 + 4.5 = 55,000, so the formal contract wins.

With strangers, larger amounts and more specific assets, relational breach losses rise and formal enforcement gains value. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not actual litigation costs.

Conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: statutes are not enforcement. Registration rates, judge numbers, arbitration coverage and judgment enforcement are the observable variables; slow enforcement raises transaction costs even with good statutes.

Boundary 2: relational governance has a scale limit. Networks work in small, repeated transactions; strangers, cross-region and one-off deals need formal enforcement.

Boundary 3: comparative institutions have omitted variables. Fast-growing countries may also have geography, demography, technology and external aid; attributing growth entirely to law overstates institutions.

Boundary 4: non-market organisations have governance costs. Cooperatives, family firms and state enterprises can lower some transaction costs, but internal monitoring, incentives and exit rules also cost; do not assume they are inherently more trusting.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: more law means more trust. Enforcement quality and clear property rights matter, not the number of statutes.

Mistake 2: relational trust is backward. Relational governance is cheaper for some transactions; it complements formal enforcement rather than replacing it.

Mistake 3: comparative institutions are only macro stories. Registration, enforcement and remedies can be quantified; testable mechanisms explain more than grand narratives.

Deeper note: state capacity, informal institutions and enforcement trust

Enforcement trust is not 'more police'. It depends on three capacities: detecting violations (information), adjudicating (courts) and sustained supervision (administration). Missing one turns rules into selective enforcement: the connected escape, the unconnected are punished, and trust falls.

Informal institutions (clans, guilds, community norms) can fill gaps in formal enforcement but may protect insiders and exclude outsiders. As markets grow and transactions become anonymous, informal institutions no longer suffice and formal contracts and courts matter more.

State capacity includes taxation, registration, statistics and enforcement. Without property registration, collateral cannot be enforced; without business registration, liability cannot be traced; without statistics, policy cannot be evaluated. Enforcement capacity is part of state capacity, not a standalone variable.

Comparative institutions should avoid the 'best institution' illusion. The US relies on courts and class actions, Germany on guilds and professional supervision, China on administrative regulation and campaigns; each has costs and cannot be transplanted wholesale. Fit depends on market structure, history and legal tradition.

Measuring trust: contract enforcement time, court efficiency, perceived corruption, firm surveys of confidence in courts, and the share of informal payments. One indicator misleads; a combination separates missing rules from weak execution.

Common misuses: equating 'crackdowns' with 'rule of law'; treating trust as culture; treating informal institutions as backward. Enforcement trust is the joint product of institutions, capacity and information.

Institutional comparison checklist

Comparing law enforcement and market trust is not a binary of 'law or no law'. Split it into seven questions:

  1. Can property rights be asserted? Registration, mortgages, transfer and expropriation rules decide whether assets can be monetised; paper rights are not tradable rights.
  2. Is breach damages calculable? Scope, enforcement time and litigation cost determine whether parties sign.
  3. Who bears enforcement cost? Courts, arbitration, guarantees and relational networks all cost something; for small deals procedure can exceed the transaction.
  4. What do informal institutions substitute? Family, guilds, clans and platform rules lower enforcement cost but can exclude outsiders.
  5. Is enforcement predictable? Selective enforcement and frequent rule changes raise risk premia; firms substitute relationships or exit.
  6. Can cross-border contracts be enforced? Different jurisdictions, arbitration seats and asset locations raise difficulty.
  7. What is the comparison benchmark? Not 'more law is better', but which governance structure minimises total cost for given transaction characteristics.

What would change the conclusion

Conclusions about law enforcement and trust depend on the relative cost of formal and informal institutions:

  1. Informal institutions already enforce. If family, guilds or platform rules constrain breach, formal courts add little at the margin.
  2. Enforcement is elite-captured. If registration and judgements favour particular groups, paper rights do not become general trust.
  3. Digital platforms cut transaction costs. If platform guarantees, ratings and dispute resolution substitute for courts, small deals rely less on formal law.
  4. Cross-border arbitration bypasses local courts. If contracts choose foreign arbitration and asset-location enforcement, local enforcement quality matters less.
  5. Ambiguity is deliberate. If rules are left vague for selective enforcement, predictability falls and firms substitute relationships for contracts.
  6. De jure is measured, not de facto. If the number of statutes replaces actual outcomes, institutional quality is overstated.

Trust is not 'how much law' but assertable rights, enforceable contracts and bearable costs combined in real transactions.

What would change the conclusion (continued)

Three further conditions:

  1. Enforcement cost shifted to insurance or guarantees. If insurers, factors and platform guarantees absorb breach losses, courts matter less and trust moves to private intermediaries.
  2. Judicial capacity is insufficient. If judges, courts and enforcement staff are too few or too specialised, statutes cannot become enforceable judgements.
  3. Stated trust, not transaction trust. If survey trust replaces contracts, defaults and repeat-purchase data, stated and revealed trust diverge.

Practical implication: institutional building is less about passing more laws than lowering the real cost of asserting rights, enforcing contracts and resolving disputes; formal courts and informal rules should complement, not replace, each other.

Data note: use de facto measures such as case disposition, enforcement time and recovery rates, not the number of statutes, or institutional quality is overstated.

Scope note: also measure access to legal services and representation quality; formal equality can coexist with unequal effective access. Enforcement cost sits not only in courts but in language, time and legal aid.

Final note: legal aid and representation quality are part of enforcement cost, not a separate welfare issue; they decide whether rights are actually usable.
