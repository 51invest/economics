Who Pays the Tariff: Intermediate Goods, Rules of Origin and Value Chains

The legal tariff payer is the importer, but the economic burden is shared by consumers, importers, exporters and upstream suppliers. Intermediate goods, rules of origin and value chains turn a simple tariff line into an effective rate of protection, compliance cost and double taxation. This article uses the effective-protection formula and a three-country value-chain example to separate who really pays.

Definition and intuition

A tariff is a tax on goods crossing a border[1]. The legal payer is the importer, but the economic burden need not stay there. If import demand is inelastic and exporter supply is elastic, the importer can pass most of the tax to domestic consumers. If import demand is elastic and exporters have alternative markets, exporters may cut prices and absorb the burden. Incidence asks who ends up with less money, not who pays customs.

The effective rate of protection (ERP) combines final-good and intermediate-input tariffs. It protects domestic value added, not gross output; a tax on inputs erodes the protection. The formula is ERP = (t_f − a·t_i)/(1 − a), where t_f is the final-good rate, t_i the input rate, and a the input cost share of final output.

Rules of origin determine whether a good qualifies for a preferential rate. They usually require substantial transformation, defined by a tariff-heading change, regional value content or a specific process. More complex rules raise compliance costs; a low tariff can be eaten by certificates, audits and traceability.

Three price effects

Tariffs produce at least three price effects:

  1. Nominal protection. The final-good tariff raises the import price and gives domestic producers a price umbrella[2].
  2. Effective protection. Input tariffs raise costs and offset part of the final-good protection; if input rates exceed final-good rates, effective protection can turn negative.
  3. Compliance cost. Origin certificates, supply-chain traceability, customs valuation and anti-circumvention checks are fixed costs per shipment or per SKU; they weigh more on small, multi-product firms.

The three effects can turn the same 10% nominal rate into 15% effective protection, 5%, or negative protection for different firms. Reading only the tariff schedule misstates the protection a sector receives.

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a final good is worth 100 yuan, with 50 yuan of intermediate inputs and 50 yuan of domestic value added. The final-good tariff is t_f = 20% and the input tariff t_i = 5%. Substituting:

ERP = (0.20 − 0.5×0.05)/(1 − 0.5) = 0.35, or 35%.

The nominal rate is only 20%, but domestic value added is protected at 35% because inputs are lightly taxed. If the input rate rises to 20%, ERP = (0.20 − 0.5×0.20)/0.5 = 20%, back to the nominal level. If it rises to 30%, ERP = (0.20 − 0.15)/0.5 = 10%, half the nominal protection.

Now origin. In a three-country chain, A exports inputs to B, B processes and exports to C. C levies 10% on the final good but requires 60% regional value content. B's processing adds only 35% value, so the good fails the threshold and pays 10%. If the firm shifts sourcing from A to a costlier C supplier to qualify, the compliance cost has become a new trade barrier. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Distribution

A tariff divides the burden among at least four groups: consumers in the importing country face higher prices; importers carry inventory, exchange-rate and compliance risk; domestic producers get a price umbrella but may pay more for inputs; exporters abroad may cut prices or lose the market. The government collects revenue, which can compensate losers or become general revenue.

The distribution also crosses sectors[4]. A final-good tariff protects final-good producers; an input tariff protects input producers while hurting downstream users. If downstream is labour-intensive and export-oriented, protecting upstream can cut downstream jobs. Asking who a tariff protects requires looking at employment and profits on both sides of the chain.

Consumer losses usually exceed the sum of producer gains and tariff revenue; the gap is the efficiency cost. But efficiency is not everything: distribution and reversibility matter. A tariff that lowers total surplus slightly but protects a specific region may pass politically while remaining economically inefficient.

Policy design

If the goal is to protect domestic value added rather than upstream rents, three rules help:

  1. Match final-good and input rates. High final-good tariffs with low input tariffs raise effective protection; the reverse creates negative protection and punishes downstream firms.
  2. Match origin rules to capability. A high regional-value threshold excludes small firms; a tariff-shift rule is simpler but can be gamed by minimal processing. Design must balance anti-circumvention against compliance.
  3. Provide adjustment assistance. Tariffs reallocate jobs and profits; injured groups need training, transfers or regional policy. Without adjustment assistance, protection becomes a permanent subsidy.

Tariffs are not the only tool. Quotas, technical standards, subsidies and procurement also protect domestic industry, but their rent allocation, transparency and compliance costs differ; compare instruments together.

Conditions and boundaries

Four boundaries are easy to miss:

Elasticity determines incidence. The less elastic demand and the more elastic supply, the more the importing-country consumer bears. Saying “the importer pays” describes only the legal relation.

Intermediates are taxed repeatedly. In a value chain, the same value can cross borders several times and be taxed each time; cumulative duties can far exceed the final-good rate[3].

Origin rules have strategic uses. They can prevent transshipment, but can also exclude competitors from a preferential area. Whether a rule is necessary depends on proportionality to the evasion risk.

Exchange rates and general equilibrium feedback. Tariffs can raise importing-country prices and depress the exporter's currency, changing trade volumes and incidence; a partial example is not a general-equilibrium analysis.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: importers bear the whole tariff. The importer is only the legal payer; incidence depends on relative elasticities.

Mistake 2: the nominal rate is the protection level. Input tariffs change the effective rate; negative protection is common.

Mistake 3: origin rules are mere paperwork. Rule design changes sourcing, production steps and investment location; it is a substantive trade-policy tool.

Deeper note: incidence, origin and intermediate goods

Tariffs are paid at the border by importers, but who bears them economically depends on bargaining and substitution. If importers pass the cost to consumers with no substitutes, consumers bear most; if consumers can switch to domestic or third-country goods, importers and exporters share; if exporters cut prices to keep share, the exporting country bears some. Exchange-rate moves absorb another part.

Intermediate goods complicate incidence. A tariff on a component raises costs for downstream manufacturers; if the downstream good is exported, the tariff can become an export tax and hurt competitiveness. Deeper value chains mean more cascading and less traceability.

Rules of origin decide 'where it is made'. Substantial transformation, regional value content and cumulation rules give the same product different rates under different agreements; firms may rewire supply chains, transship or add local processing at a cost that exceeds the tariff.

The 2018-2019 US-China experience: studies generally find most tariff costs passed to US importers and consumers, but pass-through varied by product, substitutability and time; affected industries saw lower employment and investment, and supply chains reorganised over years.

Policy evaluation cannot stop at tariff revenue. Watch consumer prices, producer costs, supply-chain reorganisation, trade diversion and retaliation; tariffs are negotiating and industrial-policy tools with different success criteria.

Common misuses: stating 'foreign exporters pay the tariff' as fact; treating tariff revenue as net gain; ignoring compliance costs from intermediate goods and origin rules.

What would change the conclusion

Tariff incidence depends on three links; change one and the answer must be recomputed:

  1. Do importers absorb it? If importers compress margins rather than raise prices, consumers bear less and the tariff behaves more like a profits tax.
  2. Can rules of origin be circumvented? If transshipment and minimal processing are allowed, tariff coverage is overstated and effective protection is below the nominal rate.
  3. Are intermediates excluded? If tariffs hit only final goods, effective protection differs sharply from the nominal rate; taxing intermediates stacks costs.
  4. Does the exchange rate adjust? If currency depreciation offsets the tariff, the short-run import-price and trade-balance response is muted.

Separate who imports, where origin sits, how much is intermediate and how the currency moves before saying who is hit.

What would change the conclusion (continued)

One more: tariffs replaced by quotas or licences. If quantity limits replace tariffs, price effects and rent allocation differ completely; a tariff rate alone understates protection.

Exercises

  1. Distinguish what the legal payer, incidence and effective protection each answer.

  2. Final good 100 yuan, inputs 40 yuan, t_f=15%, t_i=10%; calculate ERP.

  3. If the input rate rises to 25%, recalculate ERP and explain why protection falls.

  4. A 10% final tariff, 60% regional-value threshold and actual 45% regional value: what is the compliance result?

  5. Compare inelastic and elastic demand: who more easily bears the tariff?

  6. What compliance costs come with the three origin standards? Give one example each.

  7. True or false: a tariff always protects domestic industry. Use a negative-protection case.

  8. What determines the economic incidence of a tariff?

  9. Why can a tariff on an intermediate good become an export tax?
