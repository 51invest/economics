China's External Balance and Value-Chain Position: Reading the External Ledger

A country's external ledger is more than a trade surplus. The current account equals saving minus investment, and net foreign assets are also changed by exchange rates and valuation; gross exports contain imported inputs, so only the value-added share shows what a country actually creates. Put saving-investment, value-added trade, net foreign assets and reserve cover on one ledger to read how China connects to the global economy.

Definition

The balance of payments records goods, services, income, transfers and financial transactions between residents and non-residents. The current account combines goods and services trade, primary income and secondary transfers; in the macroeconomic identity it equals national saving minus national investment. Net foreign assets (NFA) are the stock of external assets minus external liabilities. Trade in value added splits gross exports into domestic value added and foreign value added, avoiding double counting of imported inputs. The real effective exchange rate (REER) is a trade-weighted relative price of a country's prices or costs against its partners[1][2].

This article does not forecast exchange rates or trade flows. It breaks China's external ledger into four verifiable accounts: the saving-investment gap, gross versus value-added exports, net foreign assets and valuation changes, and reserves relative to imports.

Intuition

Think of a country as a large household. If it earns more (saves more) than it invests, the remainder is lent abroad and shows up as a current account surplus; if it invests more than it saves, the gap is borrowed abroad and shows up as a deficit. But this "lending abroad" does not happen automatically; it corresponds to the net result of exports, imports, investment income and transfers.

Global value chains complicate the ledger. When China exports a device, the export value may be counted at the whole-machine price, but part of it is imported chips, materials and components. Gross exports alone attribute foreign value to China; the domestic value-added share shows how much Chinese firms actually add along the chain. Likewise, a trade surplus is not the same as a domestic value-added surplus: processing trade can generate large gross exports with a modest domestic value-added share[2].

Formal expression

The saving-investment identity for an open economy is:

CA=S−ICA=S-ICA=S−I

where CACACA is the current account, SSS is national saving and III is national investment. Both sides are flows in the same currency and period.

The stock of net foreign assets changes as:

NFAt=NFAt−1+CAt+VALtNFA_t=NFA_{t-1}+CA_t+VAL_tNFAt​=NFAt−1​+CAt​+VALt​

where VALtVAL_tVALt​ is valuation change from exchange rates, equity prices and bond prices on external assets and liabilities. A current account surplus does not guarantee an equal increase in net foreign assets; valuation losses can offset part of the surplus[1].

The domestic value-added share of gross exports is:

DVA share=DVAX\text{DVA share}=\frac{DVA}{X}DVA share=XDVA​

where DVADVADVA is domestic value added embodied in exports and XXX is gross exports. If gross exports are 100 and domestic value added is 25, the share is 25%; the rest is imported inputs and foreign value added[2].

The real effective exchange rate can be written as:

q=eP∗Pq=\frac{eP^*}{P}q=PeP∗​

where eee is the nominal exchange rate (domestic currency per foreign currency), P∗P^*P∗ is partner prices and PPP is domestic prices. A rise in qqq means a real appreciation. The effect on exports depends on pass-through and quantity elasticity, not the nominal exchange rate alone.

Reserve cover can be expressed in months of imports:

Reserve cover=RM/12\text{Reserve cover}=\frac{R}{M/12}Reserve cover=M/12R​

where RRR is foreign exchange reserves and MMM is annual imports. Months of cover is a liquidity buffer, not a normative claim that "more reserves are always better".

Conditions

  1. The current account must separate goods, services, primary income and secondary transfers; goods trade alone omits services, investment income and transfers[1].
  2. Net foreign assets are affected by valuation changes; exchange rates and asset prices can make the stock fall in a surplus year[1].
  3. Gross exports and value-added exports cannot be used interchangeably; deeper global value chains mean more double counting of imported inputs[2].
  4. The export effect of real appreciation depends on pass-through and quantity elasticity; low pass-through means smaller price changes but compressed margins (this article's judgment).
  5. Reserve cover must consider import volatility, short-term external debt and capital-flow risk; a fixed threshold is not a stress test (this article's judgment).
  6. Balance-of-payments data are revised; initial values, revised values and definition changes cannot be mixed into one series[3].

Example and calculation

Take a teaching economy: GDP is 100 units, national saving is 43 and national investment is 41. The current account is:

CA=43−41=2CA=43-41=2CA=43−41=2

That is a current account surplus equal to 2% of GDP. The 2 units are not "newly printed money"; they are the part of domestic saving that exceeds investment and is lent to non-residents through external financial transactions.

Now look at the value-added content of exports. Suppose gross exports are 25, of which domestic value added is 18 and foreign value added is 7. The domestic share is:

1825=0.72\frac{18}{25}=0.722518​=0.72

or 72%. Using gross exports of 25 alone would overstate domestic value creation; on a value-added basis, the domestic economy actually adds 18.

Processing trade makes the point sharper: gross exports are 100, imported intermediate inputs are 60, domestic value added is 25 and the remaining 15 is other foreign value added. Gross exports are 100, but the domestic value-added share is only:

25100=0.25\frac{25}{100}=0.2510025​=0.25

or 25%. Gross exports of 100 and domestic value added of 25 are different concepts: the first is a customs measure, the second a national-accounts measure.

For net foreign assets, suppose opening NFA is 3,000, the current account surplus is 100 and valuation change is −150. Closing NFA is:

3000+100−150=29503000+100-150=29503000+100−150=2950

Despite the surplus, the stock falls by 50 because exchange rates or asset prices produced a valuation loss.

For reserve cover, suppose reserves are 600 and annual imports are 300:

600300/12=24 months\frac{600}{300/12}=24\text{ months}300/12600​=24 months

The GDP, saving, investment, export and reserve numbers above are teaching assumptions, not actual statistics for China or any other country.

Common mistakes

"A trade surplus is domestic value added." Gross exports contain imported intermediate inputs and foreign value added; the surplus is a customs measure of net export value, not a domestic value-added surplus[2].

"A current account surplus always raises net foreign assets." Net foreign assets also change with exchange rates and asset prices; the stock can fall in a surplus year because of valuation losses[1].

"More reserves are always safer." Reserves are a liquidity buffer but carry opportunity costs; adequacy depends on import volatility, short-term external debt and capital-flow stress, not absolute size alone (this article's judgment).

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "China's international balance of payments and industrial chain position: how to read external accounts". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Saving-investment and the current account". Column two states the transmission channel of "Gross exports and value-added trade". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Net foreign assets, valuation and reserve cover". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Gross exports and value-added trade" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Net foreign assets, valuation and reserve cover is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.
