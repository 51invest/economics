Behavior, Experiments and Public Services

Behavioral economics corrects the standard model with observable biases, while experiments and public services turn those biases into testable, retractable policy tools.

ModuleECO-M20 · 行为实验与公共服务
LevelFoundation
Read5 min · 12 sections

Definition

Behavioral economics studies how actual decisions depart from the standard rational model when attention, memory, risk perception and social comparison are limited; experimental evaluation of public services uses randomized or quasi-experimental designs to test whether an intervention actually changed outcomes[1][3]. It does not treat “irrationality” as a moral verdict; it writes observable deviations as testable mechanisms.

Intuition

For the same retirement savings scheme, participation can differ a lot between a form that is automatically ticked “join” and one that requires an active opt-in. People did not suddenly love saving; the default lowered the cost of deciding. Behavioral interventions often draw power from small arrangements rather than larger subsidies.

Formal expression

Bounded rationality

Bounded rationality means decision-makers cannot exhaust all options because of limited attention, computation, time and information. Common patterns include comparing only salient dimensions, inertia around defaults, confusing nominal with real changes, and asymmetric responses to losses and gains[3].

Preferences

Preferences are not fixed: the standard consumption model treats preferences as given and maximizes utility subject to a budget constraint[2]; behavioral economics shows that a reference point changes gain-loss judgments, framing changes the effect of the same information, and present bias makes immediate payoffs loom larger[1]. Analysis should state the object, time scale and reference point.

Experiments

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) assigns units randomly to treatment and control and estimates the effect from the difference in outcomes. The following design checklist is this article's methodological summary: pre-specify the sample, randomization unit, primary outcome, statistical power and stopping rules; observational designs must address selection and omitted variables.

Behavioral interventions

Behavioral interventions include defaults, simplification, reminders, social-norm feedback and commitment devices. They change the decision environment rather than issuing commands; effects are context-dependent, and one successful trial cannot be copied directly to another population[3].

Health and public services

In health and public services, behavioral tools are used to raise enrollment, appointment attendance, medication adherence and screening uptake. Evaluation must look at outcomes, equity—whether only the already-motivated are helped—and persistence[3][4]; these three dimensions are this article's summary, and concrete indicators depend on the program.

Conditions and limits

  1. A behavioral bias must be independently measurable; a story about “why” is not causal evidence.
  2. RCT external validity depends on sample, setting and implementation; success in one city does not imply success in another.
  3. Interventions can trigger substitution or equity problems; these risks must be specified in advance, not selected after the fact.
  4. Public-service ethics and privacy are stricter than in ordinary marketing experiments; randomization and data use need lawful authority.

Worked example

Suppose a retirement savings scheme is rolled out in two communities: A uses “enrolled by default, may opt out”; B uses “not enrolled by default, must opt in”. Other conditions are similar; the numbers are teaching assumptions.

Common mistakes

Behavioral economics proves everyone is irrational. It studies systematic deviations under specific conditions, not a blanket label; many decisions remain consistent with the rational model.

One successful trial justifies national rollout. External validity, implementation capacity and equity must be retested; replication failures are common in behavioral interventions.

Knowledge-point review

bounded rationality

Definition: Bounded rationality means decision-makers face limits on information, time, computation and attention and rely on heuristics rather than full optimisation.

Mechanism: Heuristics cut cognitive cost but create systematic biases; framing, defaults, availability and emotion shape choices.

Example: For example, faced with complex insurance terms, consumers often take the default or compare only premiums and ignore coverage.

Boundary: Bounded rationality is not stupidity; heuristics can work in familiar settings, and experimental contexts differ from real high-stakes decisions, limiting extrapolation.

constructed preferences

Definition: In behavioural economics, preferences are not always fixed and revealed, but constructed in elicitation, framing and social context.

Mechanism: Loss aversion, reference points, defaults and comparisons change valuations; the same outcome is judged differently under different descriptions.

Example: For example, the same surgery framed as 90 percent survival or 10 percent mortality can produce different patient choices.

Boundary: Constructed preferences do not deny real needs; instruments, incentives and context create artefactual differences, and policy cannot use nudges to replace informed consent.

experiments

Definition: Experiments identify causal effects by randomly assigning treatment and controlling conditions, in labs, fields or natural settings.

Mechanism: Randomisation balances observed and unobserved differences; sample, compliance, attrition and external validity shape conclusions.

Example: For example, randomly sending energy-saving tips to some households and comparing usage estimates the tip's causal effect.

Boundary: Experiments are not universal; small samples, strategic behaviour, ethics and short windows limit results, and lab behaviour may not transfer to real markets.

behavioural interventions

Definition: Behavioural interventions use defaults, reminders, simplification, social norms or commitments to change choice architecture without large economic incentives.

Mechanism: They work by cutting friction and attention costs; effects depend on the behaviour, group and continued exposure, and may decay or backfire.

Example: For example, automatic pension enrolment raises participation, but default funds and contribution rates also determine final saving.

Boundary: Nudges are not neutral; defaults can be manipulated, effects are heterogeneous, and state capacity, transparency and accountability decide whether to use them.

health and public services

Definition: Health and public services involve asymmetric information, externalities, equity and supply constraints, so ordinary market pricing is insufficient.

Mechanism: Insurance pools, physician incentives, waiting times, quality regulation and prevention shape outcomes; payment methods affect over-treatment and selection.

Example: For example, fee-for-service may encourage extra tests, while capitation may skimp on needed care without quality safeguards.

Boundary: Public services cannot be judged by efficiency alone; access, equity, trust and long-run health matter, and both markets and state provision can fail.
