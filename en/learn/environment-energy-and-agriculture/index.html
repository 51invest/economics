Environment, Energy and Agriculture

Environmental, energy and agricultural problems share a structure: resource use creates external costs, prices and property rights allocate them, and natural conditions limit how fast substitution can occur.

Definition

Environmental and resource economics studies how the costs of resource use and pollution are priced, who bears them, and how energy and agricultural systems transform under natural constraints. An externality is a cost or benefit imposed on a third party that does not enter the decision price of the activity[1]. The core question is not whether to protect the environment, but how to put unpriced costs back into decisions.

Intuition

A factory discharges wastewater. The factory pays for production but downstream residents bear the pollution. The factory prices only its private cost, so output exceeds the level that would include pollution. To make the price reflect social cost, a regulator can use a tax, tradable allowances, standards or property rights; the tools differ in who pays, how much and where abatement happens.

Formal expression

Externalities and social cost

Social cost = private cost + external cost. When the external cost is unpriced, market output exceeds the social optimum. A Pigouvian tax charges the external cost per unit and lifts the price toward social cost; cap-and-trade sets an emissions cap and lets allowances price in a market. A tax gives price certainty; a cap gives quantity certainty.

Resources

Renewable resources require attention to regeneration rates; non-renewable resources to depletion and substitution. Resource rent is the surplus from extraction; unclear property rights or subsidies can lead to over-extraction or waste.

Carbon pricing

Carbon pricing turns the external cost of greenhouse-gas emissions into a price. It can be a tax, allowances or a hybrid; coverage, price level, revenue use and leakage—shifting emissions to unpriced regions—determine the effect[3].

Energy transition

An energy transition is not a single fuel switch but a system change across generation, transmission, storage, end use and land. Incumbent assets can become stranded assets; low-marginal-cost renewables can depress wholesale prices without lowering the total bill, because network and capacity costs remain. When comparing policy tools, place taxes, caps, standards and voluntary agreements in one table with their cost and coverage[2].

Agriculture and natural risk

Agriculture is exposed to weather, pests, prices and credit cycles; natural risk cannot be fully insured, and storage, irrigation, varieties and trade are coping tools. Under the FAO framework, food security has four dimensions - availability, access, utilization and stability - and is not self-sufficiency; stocks, trade and nutrition affect all four[4].

Conditions and limits

  1. External-cost estimates depend on exposure, dose and valuation assumptions; writing uncertain ecological losses as a precise number creates false precision.
  2. Taxes and caps depend on monitoring, enforcement and market liquidity; without reliable measurement, the price signal is polluted.
  3. Energy transitions are constrained by networks, storage, permitting and supply chains; generation cost alone understates system cost.
  4. Agricultural and food policy must separate short-run price shocks from long-run productivity; capping prices can suppress the signal and deepen shortages.

Worked example

Suppose three abatement options cost 30, 50 and 80 yuan per tonne; current emissions are 1 million tonnes and the government sets a 60 yuan tax. These are teaching assumptions.

Abatement option Marginal cost (yuan/tonne) Abate under a 60 yuan tax?
A 30 Yes
B 50 Yes
C 80 No

At a 60 yuan tax, A and B cost less than the tax, so the firm abates; C costs more, so the firm pays the tax. Under a cap, the level of the cap, initial allocation and banking rules change both allowance price and where abatement occurs. Both tools reduce emissions; one fixes the price, the other fixes the quantity.

Common mistakes

The carbon price equals the whole climate cost. A carbon price covers only emissions inside the scheme and depends on policy and market conditions; it is not a comprehensive damage estimate.

Energy transition is only about generation. Grids, storage, permitting, supply chains and end-use electrification also determine speed and cost.

Knowledge-point review

environmental externality

Definition: Environmental externalities are unpriced environmental harms or benefits imposed on third parties, such as pollution, carbon emissions and ecosystem services.

Mechanism: They make market prices below social cost and cause over-pollution; effluent taxes, caps, property rights and standards can internalise them but need monitoring and enforcement.

Example: For example, sulphur dioxide from a power plant harms downstream health while electricity prices omit control costs, so generation and use are too high.

Boundary: Environmental externalities cross borders and generations, affected parties are hard to price, and single instruments can leak, substitute or shift costs onto low-income groups.

natural resources

Definition: Natural resources are minerals, energy, water, land and ecosystem stocks provided by nature, renewable or non-renewable.

Mechanism: Resource rents, extraction costs, substitutes and technology shape use; property rights and fiscal rules affect resource curses and intergenerational saving.

Example: For example, an oil exporter that spends rents on consumption rather than education and diversification suffers when prices fall.

Boundary: Resource abundance is not automatically a curse; institutions, price volatility, exchange rates and transparency matter, and stock values depend on technology and prices.

carbon pricing

Definition: Carbon pricing charges greenhouse-gas emissions or caps their total, embedding the cost in production and consumption decisions.

Mechanism: A carbon tax gives price certainty and emissions trading gives quantity certainty; coverage, free allowances, offsets and price caps shape results.

Example: For example, a tax per tonne of carbon dioxide raises fossil-fuel costs and encourages efficiency, fuel switching and low-carbon investment.

Boundary: Carbon pricing can raise energy and food prices and trigger leakage and competitiveness disputes; revenue use, border adjustment and complementary policy determine fairness and acceptance.

energy transition

Definition: The energy transition shifts from carbon-intensive fossil fuels to renewables, electrification and efficiency.

Mechanism: It requires coordinated generation, grids, storage, fuel switching and demand management; falling costs, policy, technology and security shape speed.

Example: For example, cheaper wind and solar can still be curtailed when grids and storage are insufficient, limiting substitution.

Boundary: The transition is not one technology swap; critical minerals, land, communities, jobs and energy security create trade-offs, and moving too fast or slow both cost.

food and natural risks

Definition: Food and natural risks are climate, pests, water, land degradation and price shocks affecting food supply, farm income and financial stability.

Mechanism: Risks transmit through yields, stocks, trade and prices; droughts, floods, export restrictions and biofuel demand amplify volatility.

Example: For example, a drought in a major region cuts output, exporters restrict shipments and importers face higher food prices and poverty.

Boundary: Natural risks cannot be solved by insurance alone; reserves, trade, irrigation, varieties and income support all cost, and climate forecasts and cross-border coordination are imperfect.
