Scaling Behavioural Interventions: When Defaults Cross the Line

Defaults, salience and social norms can work in randomised trials, but that does not mean they survive scale-up. This article puts effect size, reach, implementation cost and ethical boundaries into one account, explaining why opt-out defaults raise participation while potentially denying choice without users' knowledge.

Definition and intuition

Behavioural interventions change behaviour through choice architecture rather than prices or mandates. Three common types:

Defaults set an outcome when the user does not act, such as automatic pension enrolment, organ donation or green electricity. Defaults exploit inertia and usually outperform pure information campaigns.

Salience puts key information where it is easier to see, such as late fees, sugar content or energy labels. It lowers attention cost without changing the facts.

Social norms tell users what most people do, such as '80% of households in this community pay on time'. They exploit conformity but can backfire in different groups[1][2].

None of the three is inherently manipulation, but all can be abused. Defaults change outcomes without users' knowledge, salience can selectively disclose, and social norms can shame or mislead.

Formal expression: the scale-up account

The scaled net effect can be written Net = N × Δp × V − C. N is reach, Δp the behaviour-change rate, V the value per change and C implementation cost.

In randomised trials Δp is often 1–5 percentage points; at scale, implementation quality, target group and baseline change, so Δp can decay. A small Δp can still matter with large N; if C rises linearly, cost can eat the gain.

Suppose 1 million people are eligible for automatic enrolment, baseline participation is 5%, and the default raises it to 35%, so Δp=30 points; each new saver contributes 1,000 yuan a year, V=1,000, and implementation costs 20 million yuan. Net = 1,000,000×0.30×1,000 − 20,000,000 = 280 million yuan. If scale-up cuts Δp to 10 points, net falls to 80 million; if users exit because they were not informed, the real effect is lower. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Ethical boundaries

The ethics of defaults centre on three questions:

  1. Knowledge: do users know the default exists and how to opt out? If exit is difficult, the default turns from help into lock-in.
  2. Goal: does the default point to what users themselves would choose, or to the designer's or supplier's interest? Automatic renewal or plan upgrades differ from automatic pension enrolment.
  3. Reversibility: is exit easy and cheap? If it requires a phone call, a form or a waiting period, the default is not reversible.

A workable rule is exitable, understandable, comparable: disclose the default clearly, make exit no harder than joining, and let users compare the default with alternatives. Meet these three and the default is closer to a nudge; fail them and it is closer to a dark pattern.

Social norms have boundaries too. Telling users 'most people do this' can work, but inaccurate information or shaming of minorities destroys trust. Salience is similar: highlighting only favourable information while hiding the rest is incomplete disclosure[3][4].

Conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: heterogeneous effects. Defaults work on inertia, less on people with clear preferences; social norms work in homogeneous communities but can backfire in diverse cities.

Boundary 2: baseline change. If prices, access or trust already change, behavioural interventions are offset; nudges cannot replace infrastructure.

Boundary 3: implementation quality. At scale, frontline delivery, training and feedback determine effects; the careful execution of a pilot may not replicate.

Boundary 4: ethics review. In health, finance and organ donation, independent ethics review and public evaluation are required, not self-assessment by the implementing agency.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: nudges are always softer than mandates. A default can change outcomes without knowledge; softness depends on disclosure and exit cost.

Mistake 2: trial success means scale success. Effect sizes decay, costs rise and target groups change; scale-up needs re-evaluation.

Mistake 3: behavioural interventions need no transparency. Defaults, salience and norms all involve information presentation; transparency is the basis of legitimacy.

Deeper note: effect sizes, heterogeneity and ethical boundaries

Behavioural effects are usually small. Defaults, reminder texts and simplified forms may be significant in the lab but diluted at national scale, and publication bias overstates average effects. Evaluate with pre-registration, sample size and confidence intervals, not significance alone.

Heterogeneity decides who benefits. Default savings plans work for people with low financial literacy and may not work for high-income, highly educated groups; reminders work for forgetful people, not for deliberate avoiders. Policy cannot assume one nudge moves everyone.

Scale decay comes from three mechanisms: more complex target populations, lower delivery fidelity and public immunity. Small pilots run by researchers give way to local delivery at scale, and repeated messages get ignored.

Ethical boundaries: does a default exploit inertia, does a frame manipulate, does the state have the right to choose for individuals? Transparency, opt-out and preserved choice are the floor; for vulnerable groups, avoid 'nudge becoming deprivation'.

Evaluation: randomised trials, staggered rollouts, quasi-experiments and long-run tracking. Short-run behaviour change is not long-run welfare; measure savings, health and debt outcomes.

Common misuses: treating correlation as causation; extrapolating pilot effects; ignoring delivery cost and equity. Behavioural tools are low-cost complements, not substitutes for structural policy.

Cases: automatic enrolment and reminder texts

Automatic pension enrolment is one of the most successful defaults. US 401(k) auto-enrolment raised participation from about 50% to over 80%, but default savings rates are often low and auto-enrolees raise them more slowly than active choosers. Pair it with auto-escalation and a sensible default portfolio, or participation rises while adequacy stays low.

Reminder texts work for appointments, payments and medication but decay over time; repeated messages create immunity. Best practice is limited frequency, personalisation and a clear action link, not endless pushing.

Ethical debate: does auto-enrolment violate autonomy? Supporters say choice remains (opt-out); critics say inertia is exploited. Transparency, easy exit and periodic re-choicing are the compromise.

Evaluation: long-run auto-enrolment effects need savings rates, asset allocation and retirement adequacy, not participation alone; texts need behaviour change, not open rates.

Behavioural-intervention design checklist

Before designing or evaluating a nudge, answer six questions rather than fixating on the effect size:

  1. Whose choice cost does the default change? Automatic enrolment lowers action costs but can package inaction as consent; distinguish convenience from agreement.
  2. Does the effect replicate at scale? Results from small samples, one institution or short trials often shrink nationally; check preregistered replication first.
  3. Is heterogeneity stated in advance? The same message can backfire for low-income, low-literacy or high-trust groups; an average hides harm.
  4. Is opting out easy enough? Low exit friction keeps the intervention closer to choice architecture; hard exit moves it from nudge to manipulation.
  5. Are there rival explanations? Reminders, defaults and social norms often change together; use control arms to separate attention, commitment and conformity.
  6. What are long-run effects and side effects? Short-run enrolment can coexist with later non-payment; track renewal, understanding and fairness.

Quality is not 'does it work' but 'for whom, for how long, and at what cost'.

What would change the conclusion

Behavioural-intervention conclusions depend on persistence and freedom to exit:

  1. Default effects fade. If the retention advantage of automatic enrolment disappears after a few years, long-run welfare gains are below short-run readings.
  2. Exit friction rises. If opting out is complex and opaque, the intervention slides from choice architecture to manipulation and consent loses legitimacy.
  3. Heterogeneous harm is averaged away. If effects are negative for low-trust or low-literacy groups, a positive average does not make the policy desirable.
  4. Trust is overdrawn. If frequent defaults and reminders erode public trust, later interventions cost more.
  5. Rival explanations remain. If reminders, commitment and conformity move together, the mechanism is unidentified and replication fails.

Quality is not average effect size but the sum of persistence, heterogeneity, exit freedom and trust.

What would change the conclusion (continued)

One more: whether the intervention is read as official endorsement. If defaults or reminders are seen as government backing, the effect may come from legitimacy rather than cognitive bias and fail in low-trust settings.

Practical implication: judge nudges by long-run retention, freedom to exit and effects on low-trust groups; a positive average is not enough to justify the policy.

Data note: report attrition, effect sizes and confidence intervals; publishing only significant results overstates the intervention.
