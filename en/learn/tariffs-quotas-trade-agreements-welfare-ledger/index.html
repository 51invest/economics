Tariffs, Quotas and Trade Agreements: The Welfare Ledger of Trade Barriers

Tariffs and quotas both raise domestic prices, but tariff revenue goes to the government while quota rents go to licence holders. Trade agreements lower barriers among members while retaining rules costs. The welfare ledger must separate consumer losses, producer gains, fiscal revenue and efficiency losses.

Definition

A tariff is a tax on imported goods, levied as a percentage of value (ad valorem) or per unit (specific)[1]. An import quota limits the quantity that may be imported; the resulting price gap creates quota rent, whose recipient depends on how licences are allocated[1]. A trade agreement is a reciprocal arrangement in which two or more economies lower tariffs and non-tariff barriers; the World Trade Organization calls these regional trade agreements and requires members to notify them[2][3].

All three change the domestic price and quantity of imports, but the destination of the income and the administrative cost differ. This article uses "trade barrier" as the umbrella term and does not treat a tariff, a quota and an agreement as the same instrument.

Intuition

An imported appliance lands at CNY 100. A 25% tariff raises the domestic price to CNY 125. At the free-trade price, domestic producers supply 200,000 units; at CNY 125 they supply 350,000. Consumers buy 850,000 instead of 1,000,000, and imports fall from 800,000 to 500,000. The extra money consumers pay does not vanish. It becomes producer gain, tariff revenue and an efficiency loss from distorted production and consumption.

A quota can also push the price to CNY 125, but it does not automatically generate government revenue. A quota licence worth CNY 25 per unit transfers that rent to whoever holds the licence. A trade agreement lowers tariffs among members, so the price may return to CNY 100; rules of origin, standards and customs procedures still impose costs.

where A is the production distortion and B the consumption distortion. Producer gain, consumer loss and tariff revenue net out as transfers, leaving the two triangles.

Conditions

  1. The effect depends on market structure and domestic competition. If domestic firms have market power, part of the tariff may become higher profit rather than more output (author's assessment).
  2. The key quota issue is not the quantity cap itself but who receives the licences, whether they are tradable and where the rent goes[1].
  3. Trade-agreement gains come from lower barriers among members, but rules of origin, sanitary standards and customs procedures change the effective cost[2][3].
  4. Under the small-country assumption, domestic policy does not change the world price. A large country can affect the world price and gain or lose terms-of-trade benefits (author's assessment).
  5. Adjustment costs are real: job losses, regional decline and retraining needs in import-competing sectors are not automatically offset by aggregate gains[1].
  6. Tariffs and quotas can be replaced by non-tariff barriers; looking only at the tariff rate misses standards, licensing and state trading[1].

Example and calculation

Assume a linear domestic market for small appliances with a landed price of CNY 100 per unit. Under free trade:

Item Free trade 25% tariff
Domestic price (CNY/unit) 100 125
Domestic supply (million units) 20 35
Domestic demand (million units) 100 85
Imports (million units) 80 50

For a linear market with quantities in million units and prices in CNY per unit, the consumer-surplus loss is:

25×0.85+12×25×0.15=23.12525 \times 0.85 + \tfrac{1}{2}\times 25 \times 0.15 = 23.12525×0.85+21​×25×0.15=23.125

The producer-surplus gain is:

25×0.20+12×25×0.15=6.87525 \times 0.20 + \tfrac{1}{2}\times 25 \times 0.15 = 6.87525×0.20+21​×25×0.15=6.875

Tariff revenue is:

25×0.50=12.525 \times 0.50 = 12.525×0.50=12.5

The net welfare change is −23.125+6.875+12.5=−3.75-23.125 + 6.875 + 12.5 = -3.75−23.125+6.875+12.5=−3.75 million CNY per year. The production and consumption distortions are CNY 1.875 million each, so their sum equals the net loss. These are teaching assumptions, not industry data.

With a 500,000-unit quota, the price is also CNY 125 and quota rent is 25×0.50=12.525 \times 0.50 = 12.525×0.50=12.5 million CNY. If licences are auctioned and the revenue enters the public budget, the ledger resembles the tariff. If licences are given free to domestic importers, the rent becomes private income. If foreign exporters administer a "voluntary export restraint", the rent goes abroad and the domestic net loss rises to CNY 16.25 million under the same supply-and-demand assumptions (author's calculation).

Common mistakes

"Foreign exporters always pay the tariff." Only a large country that can push down the world price, or foreign firms that absorb the cost, bear part of it. In a small country, importers and consumers usually bear most of the tariff (author's assessment based on incidence logic).

"A quota and an equivalent tariff are identical." Price and quantity can match, but quota rent goes to a different recipient; free allocation gives the scarcity rent to licence holders[1].

"A trade agreement means zero barriers." An agreement lowers tariffs among members, but rules of origin, standards, customs procedures and exclusion lists still determine effective access[2][3].

At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Rules cost of trade agreements is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails."
