Consumption and Saving

Consumption is household spending on final goods and services, and saving is disposable income not consumed; the saving rate measures that split, not a bank balance at a point in time.

Definition

Consumption is household spending on final goods and services [1]. Saving is the part of disposable income not spent on consumption. The two add up to disposable income (Yd), so Yd = C + S. The saving rate is saving as a percentage of disposable income [3], and the average propensity to consume (APC) is consumption as a percentage of disposable income.

Intuition

A household earns 10,000 yuan a month, consumes 8,000 yuan, and saves 2,000 yuan, a 20% saving rate. When income rises to 12,000 yuan and consumption rises by only 1,200 yuan, saving rises by 800 yuan and the saving rate becomes 2,800 ÷ 12,000 ≈ 23.33%. Part of the extra income was not spent, so the saving rate rises. Saving is a flow over a period, not an account balance at a point in time.

Formal expression

Yd = C + S;

saving rate s = S ÷ Yd;

APC = C ÷ Yd = 1 - s (a definition this article states: s is the saving rate, and the identity follows directly from Yd = C + S).

Splitting a change in income gives the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYd, and the marginal propensity to save (MPS), MPS = ΔS ÷ ΔYd; because Yd = C + S, MPC + MPS = 1. The linear consumption function C = a + b · Yd is a teaching model in which b is the MPC. The intertemporal budget constraint is C_1 + C_2 ÷ (1 + r) = Y_1 + Y_2 ÷ (1 + r) when there is no initial wealth and the household can borrow or lend at the rate r, so consuming more today requires consuming less later.

When it applies

  1. Income definition. Disposable income is after-tax income including transfers, not gross wages; official personal saving also subtracts personal outlays, so its definition differs from the formula here [2].
  2. Durable goods count when purchased. Cars and appliances enter consumption at purchase; later use is not counted again [1]. Housing is its own CPI category, and homeowner housing cost is measured within it[2]. The specific owner-equivalent-rent imputation never appears in the cited page, so this article does not present it as verified.
  3. Saving is a flow. Moving deposits into shares is a portfolio shift, not saving.
  4. Household saving is not national saving. The textbook identity reads verbatim "Total savings = Private savings (S) + Public savings (T – G)"[4]: total saving equals private plus public saving, public saving being taxes minus purchases. Whether private saving splits further into household saving and retained earnings is not addressed there, so no such claim is made here.
  5. Use real values across periods. A rise in nominal saving may only reflect inflation, so deflate before comparing.
  6. Life cycle and permanent income. In the sense of these frameworks a transitory income change goes mostly to saving and moves current consumption little, while a persistent change is what moves consumption. The earlier wording invited the opposite reading, so it is restated. This site did not retrieve the original statement of the hypothesis, so the sentence is the article’s paraphrase and not an empirical result, and the worked example uses none of its parameters.

Worked example

Suppose disposable income rises from 10,000 yuan to 12,000 yuan and consumption from 8,000 yuan to 9,200 yuan. Saving rises from 2,000 yuan to 2,800 yuan; the saving rate rises from 20% to 2,800 ÷ 12,000 ≈ 23.33%; the APC falls from 80% to 9,200 ÷ 12,000 ≈ 76.67%; MPC = 1,200 ÷ 2,000 = 0.6 and MPS = 0.4. All figures are teaching assumptions.

Common mistakes

Saving means putting money in a bank. Saving is income minus consumption over a period; deposits, fund purchases, and debt repayment are uses of that saving.

Buying shares is investment, so it is not saving. Buying an existing financial asset creates no new capital [1] and does not change saving; it is an asset exchange.

A higher saving rate is always better. A rising rate may reflect uncertainty or weaker consumption, so read it in context.

Higher nominal saving means higher real saving. Inflation erodes purchasing power, so adjust with a price index before comparing (see ECO-012).

Knowledge-point review

consumption and saving

Definition: Consumption is household spending on goods and services; saving is income not consumed, funding future consumption or investment.

Mechanism: Permanent-income and life-cycle models smooth consumption over a lifetime; transitory shocks go more to saving, while credit limits and precautionary motives alter the path.

Example: For example, a one-off bonus is often mostly saved, whereas a permanent salary rise may lift spending sooner.

Boundary: Consumption and saving are not moral choices; wealth effects, interest rates, uncertainty and social insurance change marginal propensities, and aggregate data reflect demographics.

capital formation

Definition: Capital formation is investment in new or improved fixed assets, equipment and inventories that raises future productive capacity.

Mechanism: Firms invest according to expected returns, capital costs and financing; public infrastructure can lower transport and transaction costs for private investment.

Example: For example, a factory buying an automated line records current investment and recovers the cost through higher future output.

Boundary: Capital formation is not financial investment or property trading; depreciation, maintenance, bubbles and low-return projects can separate book investment from real capacity.

inventories

Definition: Inventories are firms' holdings of materials, work in progress and finished goods, buffering timing gaps between supply and demand.

Mechanism: Inventory change is part of investment; a demand slump creates unwanted stock, and voluntary restocking versus passive accumulation has opposite cyclical meanings.

Example: For example, a car dealer stocks up before a promotion; if sales disappoint, rising inventory forces discounts and output cuts.

Boundary: More inventory is not automatically safer; capital tie-up, spoilage, obsolescence and rapid technology change raise costs, and just-in-time depends on stable supply chains.

demand and supply

Definition: Aggregate demand is planned real spending by households, firms, government and foreigners; aggregate supply is real output feasible under prices and capacity.

Mechanism: Short-run demand shocks move output and prices, while long-run supply depends on productivity, labour and capital; inventories, imports and prices absorb gaps.

Example: For example, fiscal stimulus raises demand and output when capacity is idle, but near full capacity it shows up more in prices and imports.

Boundary: Aggregate demand and supply are not single curves; expectations, bottlenecks, energy shocks and global supply chains make gaps hard to measure, and policy lags matter.

expectations

Definition: Expectations are households' and firms' views of future prices, incomes, policy and risk, shaping current spending, investment and pricing.

Mechanism: Adaptive expectations extrapolate the past, while rational expectations use all available information; either way, expectations propagate through wages, prices and financial conditions.

Example: For example, firms expecting weaker demand may delay investment, and households expecting inflation may buy earlier, amplifying the outcome.

Boundary: Expectations are unobservable; surveys, market pricing and experiments each have biases, and central-bank communication can guide but not guarantee accurate or uniform beliefs.

shocks and cycles

Definition: A shock is an exogenous change in demand, supply, finance or institutions; a cycle is the expansion and contraction of activity around trend.

Mechanism: Shocks amplify through credit, inventories, employment and expectations; countercyclical policy can buffer them but may arrive late or build debt.

Example: For example, a pandemic hits supply and demand together, and lost tourism income feeds into jobs and spending, prolonging the contraction.

Boundary: Cycles are not regular oscillations; each shock has a different origin, persistence and transmission, and extrapolating short swings into long trends is a common error.

Data and institutional check: consumption

Before using "consumption" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns saving.

Third, the institutional boundary. disposable income is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: consumption

Learning "consumption" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "saving": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "disposable income" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of consumption still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind saving reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "consumption" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "saving" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Review questions: consumption

  1. State the numerator, denominator and unit of "consumption" in one sentence, then explain why it is not the same metric as "saving".
  2. Give an example where a change in "disposable income" reverses the conclusion, and name the rule that changed.
  3. Find a time series and show how seasonal adjustment, base period or revision changes its direction.
  4. Design a transfer test: from firm to household, or bank to fund; which parameters must be re-estimated?
  5. Write a falsifiable claim: which observable signal would make you abandon this article's conclusion?

Short answers are enough, but each must leave a number, date or source location. If you cannot answer, the module is still at the noun level; if you can, you have the minimum condition for transferring the concept to new data.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Is a household purchase of a newly built house consumption or investment in the national accounts?
  2. Calculation. Disposable income is 8,000 yuan with consumption of 6,800 yuan; income then rises to 9,000 yuan with consumption of 7,400 yuan. Find the saving rate in both periods and the MPC.
  3. Conditions. Why should saving rates of households with different incomes be compared carefully?
Suggested answers
  1. Investment. New housing counts as residential fixed investment, not consumption [1].
  2. Saving is 1,200 yuan and 1,600 yuan; the saving rates are 15% and 1,600 ÷ 9,000 ≈ 17.78%; MPC = 600 ÷ 1,000 = 0.6.
  3. Income, household size, life-cycle stage, and social insurance differ, so the same rate means different things; temporary income also distorts the current-period saving rate.
  1. Concept check. Explain "consumption" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
