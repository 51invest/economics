What Economics Studies

Economics studies how people choose under scarcity, and how those choices interact at the level of individuals, markets and the whole economy.

Definition

Economics is the study of how people make decisions in the face of scarcity. OpenStax's Principles of Economics 3e defines economics as “the study of how humans make decisions in the face of scarcity” and defines scarcity as the condition in which “human wants for goods, services and resources exceed what is available.”[1]

Three points follow: wants exceed what is available; choice is therefore unavoidable; and choices have consequences, some intended and some not. The subject is not only about money. Time, attention, land, labor, machines and rules are limited too.

Intuition

A student has three hours on Friday evening and must choose between studying, a paid shift and rest. A city owns one plot and must choose between a hospital and a parking lot. A government has limited tax revenue and must cut somewhere to spend more on schools.

The shared structure is limited means with more than one valued end. Economics writes this as choice under constraints and asks two questions: how people actually choose, and how outcomes change when prices or rules change.

Formal expression

Economics usually asks questions at two levels. Microeconomics focuses on the actions of individual agents — consumers, workers, firms — and their interaction in markets. Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole: total output, employment and the price level.[2]

Analysis relies on models: they keep the mechanisms relevant to the question, omit other detail, and state their assumptions. When you read a conclusion, ask what assumptions it rests on, and what happens if a key assumption fails.

Measuring the whole economy requires official definitions. The World Bank defines gross domestic product (GDP) as the total income earned through the production of goods and services in an economic territory during an accounting period.[3] Because the definition is explicit, its boundaries are explicit too: a different definition can produce different numbers and different conclusions.

When it applies

  1. It explains choice under constraints, not all behavior. Where resources are abundant, habits are fixed, or ends involve no trade-off, standard models explain little.
  2. Models are simplifications, not reality. An unrealistic assumption is not by itself a refutation; what matters is whether the assumption affects the question and whether a key mechanism is missing.
  3. Correlation is not causation. Two variables can move together because of a third factor or reverse causation; policy judgments need an identification strategy.
  4. Description and evaluation must be kept apart. “Raising the parking fee will lower occupancy” can be tested with data. “The fee should be raised” also involves judgments about revenue, congestion and who bears the burden (the conventional labels are positive and normative statements). Data can constrain the consequences, but cannot settle the second question alone.

Worked example

Suppose a city has 2,000 on-street parking spaces; 1,600 are usually occupied in the peak hour, and the fee is ¥5 (CNY) per hour. The city considers raising the fee to ¥8 and assumes peak occupancy falls by 10%. That 10% is a teaching assumption, not an estimate.

Item Current Hypothetical proposal
Occupied spaces in the peak hour 1,600 1,440
Fee per hour (¥) 5 8
Revenue per hour (¥) 8,000 11,520

Occupancy becomes 1,600 × 0.9 = 1,440 spaces; revenue rises from 1,600 × 5 = ¥8,000 to 1,440 × 8 = ¥11,520, an increase of ¥3,520 (44%). Two different questions follow. What happens to occupancy, revenue and nearby congestion depends on how sensitive drivers are to price, which must be estimated. Whether the fee should rise depends on how revenue, congestion and the burden on lower-income drivers are weighed. A table replaces a figure here because only two options are compared.

Models and evidence

A model answers “if these mechanisms hold, what follows”; evidence answers “do they hold in reality, and how large is the effect”. Neither substitutes for the other: a coherent model is still a conditional deduction, while a large dataset with weak identification is still a set of correlations.

This site reads evidence in the following order: direct observation or a controlled experiment usually ranks above a natural or quasi-experiment, which in turn ranks above a correlation with no identification strategy; a case can suggest a mechanism but cannot estimate its population-wide size on its own. At minimum, an empirical claim should be read for four things: measurement definition, sample and period, the counterfactual or control group, and the limitations the author acknowledges.

Parking fees illustrate the difference. The model predicts that a higher fee reduces occupancy. Testing that prediction requires occupancy data before and after the fee change, while ruling out concurrent weather, a subway shutdown or a nearby mall event. A cross-sectional correlation — “blocks with higher fees have lower occupancy” — does not by itself show that the fee caused the decline. Even if the data support “the fee reduces occupancy”, they do not answer “should the fee be raised”: that requires weighing the gains and losses of drivers, residents, merchants and transit users, which is a normative judgment.

The boundary: evidence can overturn a model’s key assumptions and mechanisms, not its mathematical derivation; evidence can change a judgment about consequences, but cannot by itself decide a ranking of values. When both are missing, the conclusion remains an assumption, not a fact.

Common mistakes

Economics is personal finance and investing. Investment is one application; economics also studies work, education, the environment, rules and household decisions.

Economics cares only about profit. Prices and profit are important constraints and signals, but models also handle time, health, risk and institutions.

Economics predicts what each person will do. It mostly offers conditional conclusions about groups; individual choices also depend on preferences, information and context.

Numbers make a claim scientific. The definition, quality and identification behind the numbers determine how strong a conclusion is; a correlation chart does not prove causation.

Knowledge-point review

scarcity

Definition: Scarcity means resources are limited relative to unlimited wants, forcing choices among competing uses.

Mechanism: Scarcity is allocated by prices, rationing, queues and institutions; opportunity cost is the price of choice, and greater scarcity sharpens trade-offs.

Example: For example, a plot can be housing, farmland or park, and choosing one forgoes the others.

Boundary: Scarcity is not absolute poverty; air can be non-scarce in some settings, time and attention are scarce, and abundant resources can become economically scarce through monopoly or pollution.

opportunity cost

Definition: Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone, not an accounting expense.

Mechanism: It includes explicit and implicit costs, depends on available alternatives and constraints, and decisions compare marginal benefit with opportunity cost.

Example: For example, using an owned storefront has zero book rent but the forgone rental income is an opportunity cost.

Boundary: Opportunity cost is not the sum of all forgone options; only the best alternative counts, the set changes with information, institutions and time, and sunk costs are excluded.

sunk costs

Definition: Sunk costs are expenditures already incurred and unrecoverable, and should not affect forward-looking decisions.

Mechanism: They are irrelevant to marginal decisions, but mental accounting and loss aversion cause the sunk-cost fallacy of continuing to invest.

Example: For example, after heavy R&D spending on a hopeless project, further funding should depend on future returns, not past outlays.

Boundary: Sunk costs are not irrelevant history; they affect cash flow and accountability but should not justify continuing a bad project, and contractual obligations and exit costs still count.

incentives

Definition: Incentives are rewards and penalties shaping behaviour, including prices, wages, taxes, subsidies, contracts and institutions.

Mechanism: They work by changing marginal benefits and costs; information asymmetry, externalities and multiple tasks produce unintended results.

Example: For example, piece-rate pay raises output but may cut quality, and congestion pricing reduces peak travel.

Boundary: Incentives are not a universal lever; people value fairness, identity and intrinsic motivation, strong incentives can crowd out moral behaviour, and rules are gamed.

marginal analysis

Definition: Marginal analysis examines the change from one more or fewer unit, comparing marginal benefit and marginal cost.

Mechanism: Totals and averages do not determine the optimum; diminishing marginal utility slopes demand down and marginal cost shapes supply.

Example: For example, water is abundant overall, but one more unit is highly valuable in a drought.

Boundary: Marginal is not average; marginal cost can rise while average falls, discrete and indivisible investment needs adjustment, and externalities drive a wedge between private and social margins.

equilibrium

Definition: Equilibrium is a state where interacting variables have no inherent tendency to change, and market equilibrium often means supply equals demand.

Mechanism: Price adjustment eliminates excess demand; equilibria can be stable, unstable or multiple, and expectations and institutions affect convergence.

Example: For example, higher home prices damp demand and encourage supply until a new equilibrium emerges.

Boundary: Equilibrium is not permanent stillness; it can be temporary, multiple or shocked, and rigid prices, imperfect information and power keep markets away.

positive and normative analysis

Definition: Positive analysis describes what is and causal relations, while normative analysis discusses what ought to be and value judgments.

Mechanism: Positive work uses data and testable claims, normative work involves welfare, fairness and ethics; policy advice mixes both and should state value premises.

Example: For example, “how much does a minimum wage reduce employment” is positive, while “is it fair” is normative.

Boundary: Positive is not value-free; question choice, measurement and models embed assumptions, normative claims need more than preferences, and conflating them disguises politics as science.

models and evidence

Definition: A model is a simplified representation of reality, and evidence is data, experiments or history that tests or supports its claims.

Mechanism: Models organise thinking through assumptions, mechanisms and testable implications; evidence assesses them through identification, samples and robustness, iteratively.

Example: For example, a supply-demand model predicts shortages from a price cap, testable with market data while controlling monopoly and regulation.

Boundary: A model is not reality; strong assumptions, omitted variables and external validity limit conclusions, and evidence is not infallible because measurement, publication bias and conflicts matter.

Data and institutional check: economics

Before using "economics" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns microeconomics.

Third, the institutional boundary. macroeconomics is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: economics

Learning "economics" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "microeconomics": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "macroeconomics" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of economics still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind microeconomics reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "economics" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "microeconomics" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is a macroeconomic question? A. Whether one firm raises its price; B. Why the national unemployment rate rose; C. How one household allocates its time; D. The entry barriers in one industry.
  2. Calculation. Using the parking example, suppose occupancy falls by only 5% while the fee is still ¥8. What is hourly revenue, and how far is it from the current level?
  3. Conditions. Why is an unrealistic assumption not by itself enough to reject a model?
Suggested answers
  1. B. A, C and D concern a single agent or a single market and belong to microeconomics; B concerns economy-wide employment.
  2. Occupancy is 1,600 × 0.95 = 1,520 spaces; revenue is 1,520 × 8 = ¥12,160, which is ¥4,160 above the current ¥8,000. This is arithmetic under a hypothetical, not a forecast of the effect of a fee increase.
  3. A model only needs to keep the mechanisms that matter for the question. In studying how a parking fee affects occupancy, weather can be set aside; if weather is strongly related to travel and would change the conclusion, that assumption must be relaxed.
  1. In 100 words, state the core metric in "ECO-001", including its denominator and unit.
  2. Write "economics" and "microeconomics" as observable variables and name the time gap between them.
  3. Find one rule change in "macroeconomics" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
  6. Write one falsifiable claim with an observable signal, a waiting period and a review metric.
