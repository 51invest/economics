Price Indices and Inflation

A price index tracks the cost of a fixed basket, and inflation is its percentage change; the index level itself is not the inflation rate.

ModuleECO-M10 · 通胀与就业
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 11 sections

Definition

A price index compares the total cost of the same basket of goods and services in two periods, with the base period set to 100 [1]. Inflation is a sustained rise in the price level, measured as the percentage change in a price index [1]. The consumer price index (CPI) is built from a household consumption basket [1]. The World Bank carries it as a comparable country series under the labels "Inflation, consumer prices (annual %)" and "International Financial Statistics database, International Monetary Fund (IMF)" (verbatim text sits in source 3's supports), i.e. an annual, year-on-year percentage [3]; the π_t used in this article is a period-over-period change, so the two bases must not be mixed and a citation of that series has to say it is an annual rate. How the basket and weights are set differs by jurisdiction: the textbook gives the U.S. procedure, where a Consumer Expenditure Survey of about 7,000 households determines the representative basket [2]; this article does not generalise that to every statistical agency. The weights show the relative importance of each spending category.

Intuition

Suppose a basket of three goods costs 200 yuan in the base period and 214 yuan now. The index is 214 ÷ 200 × 100 = 107. That means prices are 7% above the base period, not "107% higher." If the previous index was 105, the current inflation rate is 107 ÷ 105 - 1 ≈ 1.90%. The index answers "how much more expensive than the base period," while the inflation rate answers "how much more expensive than the previous period." The two are not interchangeable.

Formal expression

I_t = (basket cost_t ÷ basket cost_0) × 100; I_t is the index level, expressed in index points with the base period equal to 100, so it carries no currency; basket costs are in the currency used here (yuan) [1].

π_t = (I_t ÷ I_{t-1} - 1) × 100%; π_t is the inflation rate of the current period relative to the previous one, in percent [1].

Deflating a nominal figure gives its value in base-period prices: real value = nominal value ÷ (I_t ÷ 100); the real value is in the same currency as the nominal value, but stated at base-period prices. The CPI uses a fixed-weight basket drawn from consumer expenditure surveys and covers household consumption [2]. The GDP deflator is nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100 and covers all GDP components [2]. Because coverage and weights differ, the two measures usually differ.

When it applies

  1. The basket must represent the population. A national average basket does not describe the spending patterns of low-income or elderly households, and the same headline index can feel very different across households.
  2. Fixed weights create substitution bias. Consumers substitute toward goods whose prices rise less, so fixed weights can overstate the rise in the cost of living [2].
  3. Quality and new goods. Quality improvements and new products enter the basket with a lag; incomplete adjustment biases the index upward [2].
  4. Level versus rate. Rebasing changes the index level but usually not the inflation rate.
  5. Prices do not move together. The index is a weighted average, so it can rise while some individual prices fall [1].

Worked example

Suppose a basket costs 200 yuan in the base period, 210 yuan in year 1, and 214 yuan in year 2. The index is 210 ÷ 200 × 100 = 105 in year 1 and 214 ÷ 200 × 100 = 107 in year 2. Year-1 inflation is 105 ÷ 100 - 1 = 5.00% and year-2 inflation is 107 ÷ 105 - 1 ≈ 1.90%; the cumulative rise relative to the base period is 107 ÷ 100 - 1 = 7%. Each inflation rate in the table is the change relative to the previous period, in percent. All figures are teaching assumptions, not statistics.

Common mistakes

An index of 107 means prices rose 107%. It means prices rose 7% relative to the base period [1].

Falling inflation means falling prices. Inflation falling from 5% to 2% still means prices are rising, only more slowly; a sustained fall in prices is deflation.

A higher CPI means every good became more expensive. The index is a weighted average, and individual prices can fall.

One index can deflate everything. Coverage and weights differ, so the index should match the series being deflated.

Knowledge-point review

wages and prices

Definition: Wages are labour compensation and prices are the level of goods and services; they interact through costs, demand and expectations.

Mechanism: Wage growth above productivity raises unit labour costs and may lead firms to price higher, while price inflation feeds back into wage bargaining.

Example: For example, a restaurant raises menu prices after a minimum-wage increase, but weaker demand can blunt the wage-to-price pass-through.

Boundary: A wage-price spiral is not automatic; bargaining power, productivity, import prices and monetary policy determine pass-through, and real wages depend on inflation.

unemployment

Definition: Unemployment is the state of being able to work, seeking work and lacking a job; the rate divides unemployed people by the labour force.

Mechanism: Frictional unemployment reflects matching and turnover, structural unemployment reflects skill-job mismatch, and cyclical unemployment reflects weak demand.

Example: For example, after automation, assemblers lacking new skills may stay jobless even when nearby logistics jobs are open.

Boundary: Statistics may miss discouraged workers and zero-hour jobs; low unemployment can coexist with poor job quality, so one indicator is not enough.

inflation expectations

Definition: Inflation expectations are households', firms' and markets' views of future price growth, feeding into wages, pricing and financial contracts.

Mechanism: Expectations can become self-fulfilling through front-loading purchases, wage indexation and interest pricing; central-bank communication, rules and credibility shape anchoring.

Example: For example, a firm expecting 5 percent cost growth writes that increase into contracts, bringing actual inflation closer to the expectation.

Boundary: Expectations are unobservable; surveys, market breakevens and models are noisy, and household and market expectations can diverge.

Phillips curve

Definition: The Phillips curve describes a short-run negative relationship between unemployment and wage or price inflation.

Mechanism: Expected inflation and supply shocks shift the curve; in the long run, if expectations adjust, unemployment returns to its natural rate and the trade-off weakens.

Example: For example, overheating lowers unemployment and firms bid up wages, raising inflation; tightening then lifts unemployment and cools prices.

Boundary: The curve is not a stable law; globalisation, energy shocks, productivity and monetary institutions change its slope, and empirical versions are sensitive to sample and definition.

deflation and stagflation

Definition: Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level; stagflation is the unusual combination of stagnation or high unemployment with inflation.

Mechanism: Deflation raises real debt burdens and delays spending, potentially creating a spiral; stagflation often combines supply shocks, wage indexation and loose policy.

Example: For example, an energy shock raises costs, cuts output and lifts prices, producing stagflation, while a demand collapse can bring deflation.

Boundary: Deflation is not always harmful because technology can lower prices benignly; stagflation cannot be blamed on one policy, and supply versus demand shocks must be separated.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. An index rises from 120 to 126. What is the inflation rate?
  2. Calculation. Basket cost rises from 250 yuan in the base period to 262.5 yuan in the current period, and the previous index was 102. Find the current index and the current inflation rate.
  3. Conditions. Why might a fixed-weight CPI overstate the rise in the cost of living?
Suggested answers
  1. 126 ÷ 120 - 1 = 5%. The value 126 is an index level, not an inflation rate.
  2. The index is 262.5 ÷ 250 × 100 = 105; inflation is 105 ÷ 102 - 1 ≈ 2.94%.
  3. Consumers substitute away from goods that become relatively expensive, new products enter the basket with a lag, and quality improvements are hard to price fully, so fixed weights can overstate the rise in the cost of living.
