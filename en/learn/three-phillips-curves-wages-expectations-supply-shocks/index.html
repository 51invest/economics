Three Phillips Curves: Wages, Expectations and Supply Shocks

The Phillips curve is not one line with a fixed slope. The wage-bargaining version trades inflation against unemployment; the expectations-augmented version removes anticipated inflation from the trade-off; the supply-shock version shifts the whole curve. Only the three together explain why the same unemployment rate can come with high or low inflation.

Definition and intuition

The Phillips curve describes the empirical relation between inflation and unemployment. The simplest version comes from wage bargaining: when unemployment is low, the labour market is tight and workers or unions can push for higher pay; firms pass wage costs into prices, so low unemployment comes with high inflation. In this version inflation is a wage-pressure outcome, not a direct result of the money supply[1].

The simple version failed in the 1970s: oil shocks pushed inflation and unemployment up together. That produced the expectations-augmented and supply-shock versions. The first splits inflation into anticipated and unanticipated components; the second adds energy, imported inputs and supply-chain costs as forces that shift the whole curve. The three versions are not substitutes; they explain the same phenomenon at different time scales.

Three versions

Wage bargaining: π = π_w − β(u − u*). π_w is baseline inflation from wage settlements, u is unemployment, u* the natural rate, and β measures how tight labour markets push inflation. When u is below u*, inflation runs above the wage baseline; when above, it runs below.

Expectations-augmented: π = π^e − β(u − u*) + ε. π^e is expected inflation. The key change: if the central bank can lower π^e, the same unemployment rate supports lower actual inflation. If expectations become unanchored, disinflation requires a larger unemployment cost.

Supply-shock: π = π^e − β(u − u*) + s. s is a shock to energy, imported intermediates, freight or food. When s > 0, the curve shifts up: the same unemployment rate comes with higher inflation. The stagflation of the 1970s was a sequence of positive s combined with sticky π^e.

Formal expression and worked example

Let the natural rate be u* = 4%, β = 1.5, current unemployment u = 6%, expected inflation π^e = 2%, and a supply shock s = 1%. Substituting into the expectations-augmented version:

π = 2% − 1.5×(6% − 4%) + 1% = 0%.

At the same 6% unemployment, without the supply shock inflation would be −1%, i.e. deflation; if expectations rose to 4%, inflation would be 2%. Unemployment alone does not determine inflation; expectations and shocks matter as much.

Now the cost of disinflation. If inflation is 5% and the target is 2%, it must fall by 3 percentage points. If the sacrifice ratio is 2 percentage points of unemployment for 1 point of inflation per year, disinflation costs about 6 unemployment-rate points (for example, unemployment 2 points above the natural rate for three years). The sacrifice ratio is not a physical constant; it depends on how fast expectations fall and on labour-market institutions. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Conditions and boundaries

The Phillips curve becomes unreliable in four situations:

  1. Expectations are unanchored. If households and firms stop believing the central bank will return inflation to target[4], π^e follows actual inflation, the curve steepens and disinflation costs rise.
  2. Supply shocks dominate. A large one-off rise in energy or food prices cannot be offset quickly by demand compression; tightening also cuts output, so policy must allocate the cost between second-round inflation and recession.
  3. Labour-market structure changes. Union density, minimum wages, contract length and migration all change β; the same unemployment rate produces different wage pressure under different institutions.
  4. Measurement error. The unemployment rate counts only active job-seekers; discouraged workers drop out, so true labour-market slack may be underestimated[2].

The Phillips curve is useful for direction and cost estimates, not as a precise autopilot.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: low unemployment always means high inflation. That holds only in the wage-bargaining version with stable expectations and no supply shock. When expectations fall or supply shocks are negative, low unemployment can coexist with low inflation.

Mistake 2: disinflation always requires high unemployment. If expectations are anchored and supply shocks fade, inflation can fall without a large unemployment cost; credibility determines the price[3].

Mistake 3: the Phillips curve has disappeared. The 2021–2023 inflation rebound shows it has not; it was shifted by supply shocks and expectations. Ignoring s and π^e misreads the data.

Deeper note: identifying the three Phillips curves and their policy meaning

The 'three Phillips curves' usually mean three relationships: unemployment and wage growth, unemployment and price inflation, and inflation and inflation expectations. They are not three lines on one chart but three mechanisms; mixing them turns wage pressure into price pressure, or an expectation shift into demand overheating.

The wage curve reads labour-market tightness: low unemployment and high vacancies push wage growth up. But between wages and prices sit productivity, margins and import costs; 5% wage growth with 2% productivity growth leaves unit labour cost up 3%, so price pressure is limited.

The price curve reads the output gap and cost shocks. Demand overheating lifts core inflation, but energy and food shocks lift headline inflation without necessarily lifting core; 2011 and 2022 differ exactly here. Central banks watch core and expectations more than short-run headline moves.

The expectations curve asks whether expectations are anchored. If household and business long-run expectations rise with actual inflation, wage bargaining and pricing front-run it, and policy must be larger. Expectations are the least stable part of the Phillips curve and the focus of communication.

Identification is hard: all three curves can be shifted by supply shocks, globalisation, union density, minimum wages and migration. Single-country, single-period estimates go stale; cross-country comparisons must control for institutions.

Policy meaning: for demand overheating, rate hikes work by lowering the gap and expectations; for supply shocks, hikes cannot add energy supply and only prevent second-round pass-through; for de-anchored expectations, policy must be faster and more persistent. Separating the three avoids 'inflation means hike'.

Common misuses: equating low unemployment with high inflation; equating wage growth with price growth; treating one supply shock as demand overheating. Read the three curves separately, with lags and revisions.

What would change the conclusion

The three-curve account depends on identification assumptions. Reassess it if any of the following fails:

  1. Lagged expectation measures. If survey expectations only reflect past inflation, the expectation-augmented slope is overstated; cross-check with market pricing and forward-looking surveys.
  2. Persistent supply shocks. If energy or supply-chain shocks permanently change cost structures rather than fading, the sacrifice ratio rises and short-term rate hikes do less.
  3. Mismeasured labour slack. If discouraged workers, gig work and part-time status are misclassified, unemployment does not represent true slack and beta is biased.
  4. A credibility regime shift. If confidence in the inflation target changes institutionally, the same unemployment rate maps to different expected and actual inflation.
  5. Price controls or subsidies suppressing the print. If administrative measures temporarily hold down measured inflation, the slope is artificial and cannot be extrapolated.
  6. A sample spanning different monetary regimes. If the estimate covers the gold standard, Bretton Woods and floating rates, beta averages heterogeneous structures rather than one law.

Falsifiers do not reject the model; they specify the institutions and measurements under which it holds.

What would change the conclusion (continued)

Three further conditions would change the three-curve judgement:

  1. Bargaining coverage falls. If union density and collective-bargaining coverage decline, the wage curve flattens and low unemployment pushes wages less.
  2. Import prices dominate inflation. If globalisation makes imported goods and intermediates the main inflation source, domestic slack explains less and the supply-shock version matters more.
  3. Core and headline are mixed. If the slope is estimated on headline but policy is judged on core, energy and food noise misleads the conclusion.

Practical implication: the test of a Phillips curve is not fit but whether the policy conclusion survives a different expectation measure or monetary regime. If it does not, the curve describes an institution, not a law of nature.

Data note: estimate the curve with consistent frequency and seasonal adjustment for inflation, unemployment and expectations; mixing headline with core or unemployment with underemployment changes the slope.

Scope note: cross-country comparisons need the same expectation survey and unemployment definition; institutional differences may themselves explain slope differences.

Exercises

  1. Use the wage-bargaining version to explain why inflation may rise when unemployment falls from 5% to 4%.

  2. Given u*=4%, β=1.2, π^e=2%, s=0, calculate inflation at u=3%, 4% and 6%.

  3. If inflation must fall from 4% to 2% and the sacrifice ratio is 1.5, estimate the required unemployment-rate years.

  4. Identify which version best explains 1970s stagflation and state the roles of s and π^e.

  5. Compare high- and low-credibility central banks: which faces a smaller unemployment cost of disinflation, and why?

  6. Give a real-world combination of low unemployment and low inflation, and list at least two possible explanations.

  7. Use the framework to judge whether an oil-price shock followed by an immediate rate hike must lower inflation; state the conditions.

  8. With 5% wage growth and 2% productivity growth, how much does unit labour cost rise, and what does that mean for prices?

  9. How does policy response differ between a supply shock and demand overheating?

Working paper: a four-column check for "Three Phillips Curves: Wages, Expectations and Supply Shocks"

The subject of this article is "Three Phillips Curves: Wages, Expectations and Supply Shocks". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Phillips curve". Column two states the transmission channel of "Inflation expectations and supply shocks". Column three states the institutional boundary of "the institutional boundary". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Inflation expectations and supply shocks" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. the institutional boundary is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.
