Opportunity Cost and Sunk Cost

Opportunity cost measures the value of the next-best alternative; a sunk cost is already unrecoverable and should not keep driving the next decision.

ModuleECO-M01 · 经济学导论
LevelFoundation
Read5 min · 11 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

By the end of this article, you can price a choice against its next-best alternative, separate accounting from economic profit, and make a forward-looking decision when money is already sunk.

Definition

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone when a choice is made. It is not the same as money already paid, and it may not appear in accounting records. OpenStax's Principles of Economics 3e defines it in the same terms (paraphrased here, not quoted verbatim); Econlib's entry stresses that a resource is valued by its best alternative use.

A sunk cost, by contrast, is an expense that has already occurred and cannot be recovered. Whether the decision maker continues or stops, that expense will not change, so it should not justify the next marginal decision.

Intuition

Suppose you have two hours on Friday evening. You can study statistics or take a paid shift. The shift pays ¥200. If you study, the opportunity cost includes the ¥200 of forgone income and possibly less rest. If you take the shift, the opportunity cost includes the long-run benefit of studying. Neither option is free; they differ in what is given up.

The example also shows that opportunity cost is not only money. Time, attention, an owned building, and owned funds all have alternative uses. Valuing those uses makes the real cost of each option comparable.

Formal expression

  • Explicit cost: money actually paid to an outside party.
  • Implicit cost: income forgone by using an owned resource, such as rent on an owned building or the owner's wage.
  • Economic cost = explicit cost + implicit cost.
  • Economic profit = total revenue - economic cost.
  • Accounting profit = total revenue - explicit cost.

Therefore:

Economic profit = total revenue - explicit cost - implicit cost

A budget constraint can be written with a simple model. Suppose 40 hours are available each week, the market wage is ¥100 per hour, hours worked are h_w, and hours studied are h_s:

h_w + h_s ≤ 40, income = 100 × h_w

In this simplified model, one more hour of study has an opportunity cost of ¥100 in forgone wages. The long-run return to study, risk, and preferences are not included and must be stated separately.

When it applies

  1. Use the next-best alternative only. Opportunity cost is not the sum of every option not chosen. When several alternatives exist, the relevant value is normally the highest-valued one.
  2. Compare future increments affected by the choice. An expense that has already occurred and cannot be recovered does not change with the current decision; it is a sunk cost.
  3. Do not treat non-monetary benefits as zero. Health, learning, risk, and leisure have value. When they cannot be monetized, state the comparison basis rather than pretending they do not exist.
  4. Market prices are not always available. Owned assets, regulated prices, or missing markets require estimates from rent, shadow prices, or replacement cost, with assumptions made explicit.

Worked example

A small shop operates in a building it owns. Suppose the building could be rented for ¥8,000 per month. The shop's monthly revenue is ¥30,000 and its explicit costs are ¥20,000.

Item Amount (¥ per month)
Shop revenue 30,000
Explicit costs 20,000
Forgone rent (implicit cost) 8,000
Accounting profit 10,000
Economic profit 2,000

Amounts are in yuan (¥). Accounting profit is 30,000 - 20,000 = 10,000; economic profit is 30,000 - 20,000 - 8,000 = 2,000. If comparable nearby space rents for 15,000, economic profit becomes -5,000: the shop still reports an accounting profit, but it is worse than renting out the space once the opportunity cost is included.

The rent figures are illustrative assumptions, not observed market data for a particular location.

Common mistakes

“Free” means no cost. Free activities still consume time, attention, and queueing opportunities that could be used elsewhere.

Sunk cost is opportunity cost. It is not. A non-refundable movie ticket does not change because you stay to the end. Whether to stay should compare the value of the remaining 90 minutes in “finish the film” versus “leave and do something else.” Arkes and Blumer found that people often continue a course of action because of prior investment, even when that investment cannot be recovered; this is the sunk cost effect [3].

A positive accounting profit means the activity is worth continuing. Accounting profit does not deduct the opportunity cost of owned resources. Positive economic profit means the use is at least as good as its next-best alternative.

A larger opportunity cost is always worse. Opportunity cost is the price of a choice, not the loss itself. Compare the net benefits of alternatives rather than staring at one cost figure.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which of the following are opportunity costs? A. A non-refundable course fee already paid; B. Wages forgone by starting a business; C. Rent forgone by using an owned office; D. Advertising spending from last month.
  2. Calculation. The hourly wage is ¥80 and 20 hours are available in a week. What income is forgone by choosing 5 additional hours of study? What else must be compared to judge whether the choice is worthwhile?
  3. Conditions. Why should a sunk cost not affect a marginal decision about whether to continue? Explain using “future increments.”
Suggested answers
  1. B and C. A and D are expenses that have already occurred and cannot be recovered; they are sunk costs. They may affect psychology, but they are not the value of a current next-best alternative.
  2. Forgone income is 5 × 80 = 400 yuan. One must also compare the future benefits of study, their probability, the value of time, and uncertainty. The ¥400 is only the current monetary opportunity cost, not a complete conclusion.
  3. Because the sunk cost does not change whether one continues or stops. A marginal decision compares the future incremental benefits and costs of continuing versus stopping. Including a sunk cost lets an unrecoverable expense influence a choice that should be judged on its own forward-looking terms.
