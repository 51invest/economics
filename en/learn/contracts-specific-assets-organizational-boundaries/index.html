Contracts, Specific Assets and Organizational Boundaries: A Transaction-Cost Ledger

Markets, long-term contracts and internal organization are not three ideologies; they are three ways of handling transaction costs. The more specific the asset, the easier each party is locked in, and the more a sunk investment can be eroded by later renegotiation. Put contracting, monitoring, enforcement and bureaucratic costs on one ledger to decide whether a transaction belongs in the market, in a contract or inside a firm.

Definition

A transaction cost is the cost of finding a counterparty, negotiating terms, monitoring performance and resolving disputes. Asset specificity is the degree to which an investment is worth much in one relationship and little elsewhere: a mould built for one carmaker, software customized for one client, or a rail link serving one mine. Quasi-rent is the difference between an asset's value in the current relationship and its value in the next-best use; it measures how much the investor would lose if the relationship ended. The organizational boundary decides whether a transaction is governed by spot markets, long-term contracts, alliances or a firm's internal hierarchy[1].

This article does not argue that "bigger firms are better" or "freer markets are better". It turns organizational choice into a cost comparison: when the market is cheap, when a contract saves trouble, when internalization is cheaper, and under what conditions those answers fail.

Intuition

Imagine a parts supplier building a dedicated mould for a carmaker. Once the mould is installed, it can serve only that carmaker; switching customers requires costly re-tooling and the resale value is low. The carmaker now has bargaining power at renewal: the supplier has already sunk the investment and would lose heavily by exiting, so the carmaker can demand a lower price, longer payment terms or extra services. Anticipating this, the supplier may under-invest, delay investment or demand a higher contract price to cover the risk.

If the parties could write a contract covering every future contingency, the problem would be small. But future demand, technical specifications and quality disputes are hard to foresee completely. The choice becomes: use spot markets (flexible but easy to terminate), use a long-term contract (stable but requiring monitoring and remedies), or buy the supplier (replace negotiation with internal commands, at the cost of bureaucracy and management layers). The boundary is not about who is more virtuous; it is about which governance mode keeps contracting, monitoring, enforcement and residual control costs lower.

Formal expression

Let the asset's value in the current relationship be (V_s) and its value in the next-best use be (V_a). The quasi-rent is:

[ Q=V_s-V_a ]

If the probability of opportunistic renegotiation is (p), the investor's expected loss of quasi-rent is:

[ H=pQ ]

Market governance cost can be written as "contracting and monitoring cost plus expected hold-up loss":

[ M(k)=m_0+h k ]

where (k) is asset specificity, (m_0) is the base contracting cost and (h) is the bargaining and hold-up cost per unit of specificity. Internal hierarchy cost is:

[ G(k)=f+v k ]

where (f) is the fixed cost of building a hierarchy, budget, personnel and internal coordination, and (v) is the internal coordination cost per unit of specificity. Setting the two equal gives the organizational boundary:

[ k^*=\frac{f-m_0}{h-v} ]

When (k<k^), market governance is cheaper; when (k>k^), hierarchy is cheaper. If (h\le v), market cost does not rise faster with specificity, so no boundary exists and the market is always cheaper (this article's derivation).

Contract enforcement also depends on whether remedies can actually be delivered. Let damages be (D), the probability of winning and enforcing be (\pi), and litigation cost be (C_L). The injured party's expected net recovery is:

[ E=\pi D-C_L ]

Suing is worthwhile only if (E>0). If enforcement probability is low or litigation cost is high, a damages clause is only a number on paper. This condition separates "law on the books" from "law in practice": formal rights are not the same as enforceable rights[2].

Conditions

  1. Asset specificity must be observable and measurable; if it is only a subjective feeling, the comparison lacks a reliable scale (this article's judgment).
  2. Contracts cannot foresee everything; when technology and demand change quickly, contract gaps widen and relational governance or internalization becomes relatively more attractive[1].
  3. Courts, arbitration and industry norms determine whether contracts can be enforced; when enforcement is costly, written damages have less deterrent force[2].
  4. Asymmetric bargaining power changes how quasi-rent is split; the same specific investment produces different outcomes when the parties are evenly matched and when one is highly dependent[2].
  5. Reputation, repeated dealing and community norms can substitute for some formal contracts, but only when relationships persist and information travels[1].
  6. Cooperatives, mutual organizations and non-profits do not pursue profit alone; they may replace capital control with member control, but that brings its own governance and financing costs[3].

Example and calculation

Suppose a parts supplier invests CNY 1 million in a dedicated mould. The mould is worth CNY 1.4 million in the current relationship and CNY 0.9 million in the next-best use. The quasi-rent is:

[ Q=140-90=50\text{ ten-thousand CNY} ]

If the probability of later renegotiation is 40%, the expected hold-up loss is:

[ H=0.4\times50=20\text{ ten-thousand CNY} ]

For governance costs, let market governance be (M(k)=8+0.6k) and hierarchy governance be (G(k)=38+0.2k), both in ten-thousand CNY, with (k) an index from 0 to 100. Setting them equal:

[ k^*=\frac{38-8}{0.6-0.2}=75 ]

Specificity index Market governance cost (CNY) Hierarchy governance cost (CNY) Lower-cost mode
50 380,000 480,000 Market
90 620,000 560,000 Internal hierarchy

At (k=50), market governance costs CNY 380,000 and hierarchy costs CNY 480,000; at (k=90), market costs CNY 620,000 and hierarchy costs CNY 560,000. The threshold is near 75. These numbers are teaching assumptions, not the financial statements of a particular firm.

For contract remedies, let damages be CNY 300,000, enforcement probability 60% and litigation cost CNY 80,000:

[ E=0.6×30-8=10\text{ ten-thousand CNY} ]

Expected net recovery is CNY 100,000, so suing is worthwhile. If enforcement probability falls to 20%, (E=0.2×30-8=-2) ten-thousand CNY and litigation is not worthwhile; the contract then needs guarantees, arbitration or relational governance to supplement enforcement.

Common mistakes

"Internalization always saves transaction costs." Internalization turns external bargaining into internal command but adds budgeting, personnel, coordination and agency costs. It is cheaper only when specificity is high enough that market hold-up losses exceed bureaucratic costs[1].

"A damages clause is enforceable because it is written." A damages clause is only a paper right; when enforcement probability is low, litigation is costly or the counterparty has no attachable assets, expected recovery can be negative[2].

"Cooperatives have no transaction costs." Cooperatives replace capital control with member control and may reduce some hold-up, but member heterogeneity, decision procedures and financing constraints create another set of governance costs[3].
