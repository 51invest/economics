Scarcity and Choice

Scarcity means wants exceed what is available, so choice is unavoidable; the production possibilities frontier turns that trade-off into a boundary that can be calculated and compared.

ModuleECO-M01 · 经济学导论
LevelFoundation
Read5 min · 11 sections

Definition

Scarcity is the condition in which wants for goods, services and resources exceed what is available.[1] It is not temporary unavailability, and it is not the same as poverty: it is a condition every society faces at all times, because time, land, labor, capital and technical knowledge are all limited.

Because of scarcity, choice cannot be avoided. Every choice forgoes other options, and the value of the best forgone option is the opportunity cost, treated fully in ECO-003.

Intuition

A farmer has one field: more wheat means less corn. A student has one evening: mathematics or English. A country has limited doctors and beds: spare capacity is comfortable in a pandemic but costly in ordinary times.

Scarcity is not “things are few.” Air is usually not scarce, because at a zero price the amount available exceeds what people want; once maintaining clean air costs something, air becomes economically scarce.

Formal expression

The production possibilities frontier (PPF) shows the combinations of two outputs an economy can produce with given resources, technology and time: points on the frontier use resources fully and efficiently, points inside it leave room for improvement, and points outside it cannot be reached under current conditions.[2]

Let the outputs be x and y, each measured in units of 10,000 items. One hypothetical frontier is:

y = 100 - 0.01x², 0 ≤ x ≤ 100

Distinguish two ways to compute the cost of one more unit of x. The discrete difference is y(x) - y(x+1) = 0.02x + 0.01 units of y - 0.41 at x = 20 - while the derivative approximation dy/dx = 0.02x gives 0.40 at the same point. Both rise with x, which is increasing opportunity cost: resources are not perfectly interchangeable, so shifting more into x uses resources better suited to y[2]. The table below averages over segments of 20 units, (y(a) - y(b))/(b - a), giving 0.2, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4 and 1.8 - a different convention from the per-unit figures, and not to be mixed with them.

For a household this article writes the budget constraint in standard notation as p_x·x + p_y·y <= m, where x and y are quantities of two goods, p_x and p_y their prices, and m the budget available to those two. That is this article's own notation: none of the three registered sources states the inequality, so it is not offered as a cited claim. The slope of the boundary, -p_x/p_y, is then the opportunity cost of one unit of x in terms of y; changes in relative prices rotate the boundary, and income changes shift it (also the article's own framing).

When it applies

  1. Scarcity differs from shortage. A shortage is a market phenomenon at a given price, possibly caused by price controls or a temporary supply interruption; scarcity is the general relation between resources and wants.
  2. The frontier depends on assumptions. Change resources, technology or the period and the frontier moves; it is a model, not a statistical estimate. Official estimates of potential output are the same kind of object: FRED notes that real potential GDP is the CBO's estimate of output with a high rate of use of capital and labor.[3]
  3. Two outputs are treated as homogeneous. Real economies produce many goods; the two-good model keeps only the trade-off mechanism.
  4. Efficiency is not fairness. Every point on the frontier is productively efficient, but which point is chosen involves distribution and value judgments.
  5. Scarcity does not dictate an allocation method. Prices, queues, rationing, lotteries and custom can all allocate scarce resources.

Worked example

At x = 20, y = 96; at x = 40, y = 84: 20 more units of x cost 12 units of y, or 0.6 units of y per unit of x on average. From x = 80 to 100, the average cost is 1.8 units of y per unit of x, three times as much.

In Figure 1, A (20, 96) and B (40, 84) lie on the frontier, C (40, 60) lies inside it and D (80, 60) lies outside. C means resources are not fully or efficiently used; D becomes feasible only with better technology or more resources.

Common mistakes

Scarcity means shortage or poverty. A shortage is a short-run price or supply phenomenon; poverty is a level of income; rich societies face scarcity too.

Free goods are not scarce. Goods that are free but rationed by queues or quantity limits are still scarce; they are allocated with time rather than price.

A point inside the frontier means resources are unused. It may also mean misallocation or inefficient use of technology; the frontier itself depends on assumptions.

Opportunity cost is the money spent. Opportunity cost is valued by the next-best use forgone; see ECO-003.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Are these mainly scarcity or shortage? A. Leafy vegetables run out for two weeks after a typhoon; B. A day has only 24 hours whatever the income.
  2. Calculation. With y = 100 - 0.01x², find y at x = 50 and the average opportunity cost from x = 40 to 50.
  3. Conditions. Why are all points on the frontier efficient without all of them being worth choosing?
Suggested answers
  1. A is a shortage: a temporary supply interruption with price adjustment; B is scarcity: the total amount of time is fixed.
  2. y = 100 - 0.01 × 2,500 = 75; from x = 40 to 50, y falls from 84 to 75, so 9 units of y are forgone, or 0.9 units of y per unit of x on average.
  3. Efficiency only means one output cannot rise without the other falling; which point to choose depends on how the two outputs and their distribution are valued, a value judgment.
